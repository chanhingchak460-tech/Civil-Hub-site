The craft of reading, writing and speaking in an academic register: paragraph logic, claims that match their evidence, honest citation and clear presentation. It decides whether sound engineering is understood and trusted.

01What makes English academic

Academic English is a register built for one job: to make a claim another person could check. It prefers evidence to assertion, precise nouns to vague ones, and a calm tone that persuades by reasoning rather than volume.

Three habits carry most of the work:
- Define a term before using it.
- Keep one main idea to a paragraph.
- Signal how each sentence follows from the last.

Verbs do more than adjectives — establish, indicate and contradict tell the reader what you are doing, while very and really add heat without information.

02Paragraphs have a job

A paragraph is an argument in miniature. It opens with a topic sentence stating the claim, supplies evidence, analyses what that evidence shows, and links forward. The analysis proves you can interpret rather than merely report, so it should never be left implicit.

A useful test: read only the first and last sentence. If they already state and conclude the idea, the middle is evidence.

The commonest student fault is the evidence dump — three examples and no explanation of what they jointly prove.

03Comparison without confusion

When a task asks you to compare, choose the criteria first, then apply them in the same order to both cases. Otherwise the reader must hold two mental lists and rebuild the comparison themselves.

Keep the axis explicit: not "the two policies differ", but "both reduce emissions, though one changes the fuel and the other the route". Parallel grammar helps — if one half of a sentence starts with a verb, the other should too.

04Hedging: the strength of a claim

Academic writing separates the certain from the probable from the merely possible. Hedging is the wording that keeps a claim proportionate to its evidence, and it is precision rather than weakness.

Compare "The results prove the policy failed" with "The results suggest the policy did not meet its target". The second is still clear, but its strength matches what two cases can support.

Strong verbs need strong evidence; measured verbs suit interpretation. Ask what evidence would justify the stronger version. If it is absent, step down the ladder.

Claim strength ladder
demonstrates > indicates > suggests > may reflect
step down when evidence is indirect or comes from one case

05Paraphrasing and citation

Paraphrasing restates an idea in your own sentence structure, not by swapping synonyms inside the original order. If a reader could rebuild the source sentence from yours, it is still copying.

Read the passage, close it, write the idea from memory in one sentence, then compare and cite. Even with every word new, the idea needs a citation, because ideas have owners too.

Author–date referencing is standard in engineering reports: name and year in the sentence, full details in the list.

Four checks for a paraphrase
meaning kept + structure new + wording your own + source cited
three out of four is still plagiarism

06Speaking and signposting

A presentation is a guided tour of your own argument. Give the route at the start, mark each turning point, and summarise at the end. Phrases such as first, this leads to and to conclude are road signs that let a listener hold the structure in working memory.

Speak to the back of the room, slow down on numbers, and let silence do the punctuation. One clean visual per idea beats a dense slide, and in group talks the handovers need rehearsing — a clumsy transition is more noticeable than a small stumble.

07The report spine: IMRaD and beyond

Longer assignments follow the IMRaD spine: Introduction, Method, Results and Discussion.

  • The introduction moves from context to problem to aim, and ends by stating what the report covers and what it leaves out.
  • The method must let another person repeat the work — samples, tools, procedures, assumptions.
  • Results report what was found; the discussion explains what it means and answers "so what".

Around that spine sit the abstract, the conclusion and the recommendations. The abstract is a miniature of the whole: aim, method, key finding and implication, written last so that it can never promise more than the report delivers.

IMRaD spine
Introduction → Method → Results → Discussion
with an abstract before and conclusions after
Abstract skeleton
Aim + Method + Key finding + Implication
write last, and check it against the finished report

08Style and register in engineering writing

Academic style is about controlled choices, not decoration. Prefer precise nouns and measured verbs, keep one term for one concept, and stay consistent in spelling and units — a report that mixes metre with meter mid-page reads as sloppy even when the analysis is sound.

Active voice keeps responsibility visible ("we sampled three boreholes"), while passive voice suits method sections where the doer is unimportant ("samples were oven-dried at 105 °C"). Use either, but choose deliberately.

Nominalisations such as "the undertaking of an investigation" hide the verb; "we investigated" is shorter and clearer.

09Evaluating sources before trusting them

Not all sources deserve equal weight. Before building an argument on one, check its currency (is it recent enough for the claim?), authority (who wrote it, with what expertise?), purpose (written to inform or to persuade?) and corroboration (do independent sources agree?).

Evaluation also shapes how you use a source: summarise when you only need its content, evaluate when you must judge its quality.

A paragraph that says "the study reports a 12% reduction" summarises; one that adds "but the sample was two sites and the follow-up short" evaluates — and that second move is the difference between reporting and academic writing.

Source checks
Currency + Authority + Purpose + Corroboration
run all four before a source carries part of your argument

10Presentation craft: numbers, slides and timing

A spoken argument is heard once, so simplify what the eye could re-read. Put one claim in each slide title, keep numbers large enough to read from the back row, and round figures into spoken forms — "about two-thirds" lands better in the ear than "66.7%", and it shows you have digested the data.

Practise against the clock rather than the script: a twelve-minute rehearsal for a ten-minute slot finishes at fourteen. Build in a slow opening, a pause at each transition and a rehearsed closing line.

If the talk is recorded, watch one full replay and cut anything that costs time without carrying meaning.

Key terms

Academic register
The vocabulary, grammar and tone a subject expects in formal writing and speech.
Hedging
Language that adjusts the strength of a claim to match the evidence behind it.
Topic sentence
The opening sentence of a paragraph, stating the claim the paragraph will support.
Paraphrase
A restatement of a source's idea in new wording and structure, with a citation.
Cohesion
The links of repetition, pronoun and connector that make sentences read as one argument.
Author–date citation
In-text referencing giving author and year, matched to an entry in the reference list.
IMRaD
The Introduction–Method–Results–Discussion order that most scientific and engineering reports follow.
Abstract
A miniature of the whole report — aim, method, key finding and implication — written last so it matches the finished work.
Worked example

Turning a vague sentence into an academic claim

A draft reads: "A lot of airlines are doing good things for the environment but some are not as good." Rewrite it as a claim suitable for a comparison essay, keeping only what the evidence can support.

  1. Name the claim: environmental performance varies between airlines.
  2. Replace the vague quantities with a criterion, such as emissions per passenger-kilometre or the share of sustainable fuel used.
  3. Choose a verb whose strength matches the evidence: write "performs better on" when data are available, and "appears to perform better" when the sample is small.
  4. Make the comparison explicit and state the limitation in the same sentence.
AnswerEnvironmental performance varies among airlines: on the measures reported here, some carriers achieve lower emissions per passenger-kilometre than others, although the sample is small, so the pattern should be read as indicative rather than conclusive.

Exam method

  • Read the command word first — compare, evaluate, discuss — and let it decide your paragraph structure before you write.
  • Keep a short list of precise reporting verbs (states, argues, reports, concedes) instead of repeating says.
  • Cut vague intensifiers such as very, a lot and really; replace each with a number, a criterion or nothing.
  • Reserve ten minutes to confirm that every in-text citation has a reference entry, and every entry is cited.
  • Write the abstract last and check it against the finished report, so it never promises more than the evidence supports.
