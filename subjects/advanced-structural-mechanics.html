Load paths in members that are neither symmetrical nor circular: principal axes, shear centres, warping torsion and the stability of real columns. It explains why a channel twists and why a slender strut fails below its yield load.

01Symmetry is the exception

Textbook sections are usually symmetric, so the load plane and the neutral axis stay perpendicular and the two bending directions can be treated separately. Real members are less obliging: unequal angles, channels loaded off-centre, Z-purlins and crane rails all bend in a direction that matches neither centroidal axis.

The coupling comes from the product of inertia I_xy = ∫xy dA, which measures how the area is spread through the four quadrants. When it is zero, bending about one axis produces no stress at points defined by the other coordinate and the simple flexure formula applies. When it is not, the neutral axis tilts.

Product of inertia
\[I_{xy} = \int x\,y\,\mathrm{d}A\]

02Product of inertia and the principal axes

For a section built from rectangles, I_xy sums each part as A_i x̄_i ȳ_i, because a rectangle contributes nothing about its own centroidal axes. Doubly symmetric sections have I_xy = 0; a section with one axis of symmetry also has it zero about that pair of axes.

The principal axes are the centroidal axes for which I_xy vanishes. Finding them is the same algebra as principal stresses: the angle obeys tan 2θ_p = 2I_xy/(I_y − I_x), and the principal second moments sit between the two extremes. Substitute the angle back to check which axis carries which value.

Principal angle
\[\tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2 I_{xy}}{I_y - I_x}\]
Principal second moments
\[I_{u,v} = \frac{I_x + I_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{I_x - I_y}{2}\right)^2 + I_{xy}^2}\]

04Shear flow and the shear centre

Transverse shear is not uniform. In a thin-walled section, cut the wall somewhere and balance the longitudinal force on the area beyond the cut: the shear flow is q = VQ/I, where Q is the first moment of that area about the bending axis, and the wall stress is q divided by the thickness.

For a channel, the flange and web flows combine into one vertical force whose line of action is not the centroid. The point through which a transverse load produces no twist is the shear centre, a purely geometric property. For a channel it lies outside the web, away from the flanges, at e = 3b²/(h + 6b) from the web centre-line, with b the flange width and h the depth between flange centre-lines. An equal angle has its shear centre near the heel.

Shear flow
\[q = \frac{V Q}{I}\]
Shear centre of a channel
\[e = \frac{3 b^2}{h + 6 b}\]

05Torsion beyond the circular shaft

A circular shaft twists without warping, so τ = Tr/J and θ = TL/GJ are exact. Any other section warps: plane sections do not stay plane, and the simple torsion formula fails. A solid non-circular bar reaches its maximum shear stress on the boundary nearest the centroid, with the value taken from a coefficient table.

A closed thin-walled tube carries torsion by circulating shear flow, so τ = T/(2A_m t) and J = 4A_m²/∮(ds/t), with A_m the area enclosed by the wall mid-line. An open section has no closed path for the flow and is far weaker, J ≈ Σ(1/3)bt³. That is why a box resists torsion far better than an open channel of the same mass.

Bredt shear stress
\[\tau = \frac{T}{2 A_m t}\]
Closed-tube constant
\[J = \frac{4 A_m^2}{\oint \frac{\mathrm{d}s}{t}}\]
Open-section constant
\[J \approx \sum \frac{1}{3} b t^3\]

06Struts and effective length

A perfectly straight, centrally loaded column stays straight until the Euler load is reached: P_cr = π²EI/(KL)², where KL is the effective length set by the ends. K is 1.0 for pinned–pinned, 0.7 for fixed–pinned, 0.5 for fixed–fixed and 2.0 for fixed–free. Beyond that load the straight shape is unstable and any small disturbance grows.

The governing parameter is the slenderness ratio λ = KL/r, with r = √(I/A) the radius of gyration about the bending axis. Stocky columns yield before they buckle; slender ones buckle at stresses well below yield. A section is only as strong as its weaker axis.

Euler buckling load
\[P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}\]
Slenderness ratio
\[\lambda = \frac{K L}{r}\]

07Imperfections and the Southwell plot

No real column is perfectly straight or perfectly loaded. An initial bow δ₀ is amplified by the axial load, so the mid-height deflection becomes δ_max = δ₀/[1 − P/P_cr], and the extreme fibre must add P/A and Pδ_max/Z without exceeding yield.

Failure therefore arrives below the Euler load: the column is driven into yielding by its own growing deflection, which the Perry–Robertson method solves together. The Southwell plot extracts the Euler load from a test that never reaches instability: plotting measured deflection divided by load against deflection gives a straight line of slope 1/P_cr. Buckling is a gradual loss of stiffness.

Deflection amplification
\[\delta_{\max} = \frac{\delta_0}{1 - P/P_{cr}}\]
Southwell plot
\[\frac{\delta}{P} = \frac{\delta}{P_{cr}} + \frac{\delta_0}{P_{cr}}\]

08Energy methods as a shortcut

Strain energy stores the work of deformation: for bending U = ∫M²/(2EI) ds, with similar integrals in axial force, shear and torsion. Castigliano's theorem then gives the displacement in the direction of a load as Δ = ∂U/∂P, which handles curved and indeterminate members without integrating the differential equation.

The unit load method is the same idea in algebraic form: apply a unit load where the displacement is wanted, find the internal moment m it produces, then 1·Δ = ∫Mm/(EI) ds. Buckling loads also follow from energy, by equating the work of the axial load as the strut bows to the bending energy stored.

Bending strain energy
\[U = \int \frac{M^2}{2 E I}\,\mathrm{d}s\]
Castigliano's theorem
\[\Delta = \frac{\partial U}{\partial P}\]
Unit load method
\[1 \cdot \Delta = \int \frac{M m}{E I}\,\mathrm{d}s\]

09Plastic bending and the shape factor

When the extreme fibre first reaches yield, elastic theory says failure — but the section still carries load. Capacity grows as yielding spreads inward until the whole section is at f_y: the plastic moment is M_p = f_y S, where S is the plastic modulus, the first moment of area about the equal-area axis. For a rectangle S = bh²/4 against elastic Z = bh²/6, so the shape factor M_p/M_y is 1.5; an I-section sits near 1.1–1.2.

A plastic hinge forms where M reaches M_p and rotates freely at constant moment, turning the member into a mechanism. That redistribution is the basis of plastic design: the collapse load comes from mechanism geometry, not from the first point of yield.

Yield and plastic moments
\[M_y = f_y Z, \quad M_p = f_y S\]
Plastic modulus, rectangle
\[S = \frac{b h^2}{4}\]
Shape factor
\[\frac{M_p}{M_y} = \frac{S}{Z}\]

10Warping torsion and the bimoment

An open thin-walled section under torsion does not stay plane: the flanges bend sideways, and the resulting flange stresses are warping stresses, described by a bimoment. The total torque splits into St Venant and warping parts, T = T_sv + T_w, with T_sv = G J θ′ and T_w = −E I_w θ″′, where I_w is the warping constant, with units of length to the sixth. For an I-section I_w ≈ I_f h²/2, where I_f is the second moment of one flange.

End restraint changes everything: a torsionally restrained beam carries much of its torque by warping, so the simple τ = Tr/J figure can be badly unconservative for open sections.

Torque split
\[T = T_{sv} + T_w\]
Warping torque
\[T_w = -E I_w \theta'''\]
Warping constant, I-section
\[I_w \approx \frac{I_f h^2}{2}\]

Key terms

Product of inertia
The integral ∫xy dA over a section; non-zero means the centroidal axes are not principal and bending about one axis induces stress related to the other.
Principal axes
The pair of centroidal axes about which the product of inertia is zero, so bending about either one produces no cross-coupling.
Shear centre
The point in a section through which a transverse load must pass to cause no twisting; a geometric property independent of material.
Shear flow
The shear force per unit length along a thin wall, q = VQ/I, used to locate the resultant shear and the shear centre.
Warping
The out-of-plane distortion of a non-circular section under torsion, caused by shear strains varying around the wall.
Effective length
The distance KL between points of contraflexure of a buckling strut, chosen from its end fixity.
Perry–Robertson method
A column approach that combines elastic buckling with yielding at the extreme fibre to predict failure of an imperfect strut.
Southwell plot
A graph of deflection over load against deflection for a column test; its slope gives the reciprocal of the critical buckling load.
Plastic modulus
The first moment of area about the equal-area axis; multiplied by the yield stress it gives the plastic moment.
Bimoment
The self-equilibrating pair of flange moments caused by restrained warping; its gradient along the member gives the warping torque.
Worked example

Shear centre of a channel and torsion from an eccentric load

A thin-walled channel has flange width b = 75 mm measured from the web centre-line, depth h = 200 mm between flange centre-lines and thickness t = 6 mm. A vertical load of 5 kN acts through the centroid of a 2.0 m long member. Find the shear centre, the torque caused by the eccentric load, and the St Venant angle of twist (G = 79 GPa).

  1. Second moment about the bending axis: I_x = t h³/12 + 2 b t (h/2)² = 6 × 200³/12 + 2 × 75 × 6 × 100² = 4.0 × 10⁶ + 9.0 × 10⁶ = 13.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴.
  2. Shear centre offset from the web centre-line: e = 3b²/(h + 6b) = 3 × 75²/(200 + 450) = 16 875/650 = 26.0 mm, on the side of the web away from the flanges.
  3. Centroid offset toward the flanges: A = t h + 2 b t = 1200 + 900 = 2100 mm², so x̄ = (2 b t)(b/2)/A = 33 750/2100 = 16.1 mm. The centroid load is therefore 26.0 + 16.1 = 42.1 mm from the shear centre.
  4. Torque about the shear centre: T = V × e = 5000 N × 0.0421 m = 210 N·m = 210 500 N·mm.
  5. Open-section torsion constant: J ≈ Σ(1/3) b t³ = (1/3)(200 + 75 + 75)(6³) = 25 200 mm⁴.
  6. Angle of twist: θ = T L/(G J) = (210 500 × 2000)/(79 000 × 25 200) = 0.212 rad, about 12°.
  7. Sanity check: 12° over 2 m is unacceptable for a roof member, which is why channels are loaded through the shear centre, restrained by sheeting, or replaced by a closed box. Warping restraint would reduce this estimate, but not to zero.
AnswerThe shear centre lies 26.0 mm from the web centre-line, on the side opposite the flanges. The centroid load applies a 0.21 kN·m torque about it, and the St Venant twist is about 0.21 rad (12°), confirming that open sections need restraint or a load path through the shear centre.

Exam method

  • State the sign convention for M_x and M_y before substituting into the unsymmetrical bending formula; most lost marks are sign errors, not algebra.
  • For any thin-walled section, sketch the shear flow path first: free edges start at zero flow, and corners are where the resultants meet.
  • In torsion questions, say whether the section is closed or open before writing a formula; the difference in stiffness is enormous.
  • For struts, quote both the Euler load and the yield load, then say which one governs for the stated slenderness.
  • For open sections under torsion, quote the warping constant alongside J — end restraint can make the warping torque the dominant term.

Q01A section has a non-zero product of inertia. Which statement about its bending behaviour is correct?

WhyCoupling through I_xy means a moment about x produces stress that varies with x as well as y, and the neutral axis tilts away from the geometric axis.

Q02A channel section carries a vertical load. Where is its shear centre?

WhyFor a channel the flange shear flows form a couple that pushes the resultant shear outside the web, opposite the flanges, at e = 3b²/(h + 6b).

Q03Two members have the same mass and the same enclosed area, one a closed tube and one an open channel. For the same torque, which twists less?

WhyA closed tube carries torsion by a circulating Bredt shear flow, giving J = 4A_m²/∮(ds/t); an open section has J ≈ Σbt³/3, orders of magnitude smaller.

Q04A pinned-pinned strut of length L has Euler load P. If both ends are made fixed and nothing else changes, the Euler load becomes approximately:

WhyThe effective length falls from L to 0.5L, and P_cr varies as 1/(KL)², so the load increases by 1/0.5² = 4.

Q05A rectangular section has elastic modulus Z = bh²/6 and plastic modulus S = bh²/4. Its shape factor is:

WhyThe shape factor is S/Z = (bh²/4)/(bh²/6) = 6/4 = 1.5, meaning the plastic moment is half as much again as the first-yield moment.

Q06In the shear centre experiment, the shear centre of a channel is found as the loading position where:

WhyA load through the shear centre produces bending without twist, so the rotation gauges at the two corners read zero.
