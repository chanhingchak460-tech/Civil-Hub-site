Data is only as good as the questions asked of it. This page covers describing a dataset, fitting and judging a regression line, and spotting the quality problems that make a confident answer wrong.

01Data before models

Analysis begins with what was recorded, not what software can do. Each variable is a measured property — compressive strength, chloride content, delay in minutes — and its type sets the legal arithmetic:

  • nominal labels can only be counted
  • ordinal ranks ordered
  • interval values subtracted
  • only ratio scales have a true zero

Most engineering data are a sample from a larger population, so every summary is an estimate. Ask how rows were selected; a convenient sample measures the sampling method as much as the subject.

02Summarising a dataset

The first pass is descriptive and cheap: centre, spread and shape. The mean is the balance point, the median the middle value, and the mode the most frequent category. Spread is reported as the standard deviation, which shares the data's units, or the coefficient of variation, which is dimensionless and comparable across variables. Skew drags the mean towards the long tail while the median barely moves, which is why repair costs and rainfall are quoted as medians; keep the sample size beside any summary.

Sample mean
\[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\]
n observations
Sample standard deviation
\[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}}\]
n − 1 corrects for sample size
Coefficient of variation
\[\mathrm{CV} = \frac{s}{\bar{x}}\]
dimensionless
Four summary statistics and where each one misleads
StatisticWhat it summarisesWhere it misleads
MeanBalance point of all valuesPulled by extreme values
MedianMiddle value once rankedIgnores the size of most values
Standard deviationTypical distance from the meanAssumes a symmetric spread
Coefficient of variationSpread relative to the meanUnstable near a zero mean

03Correlation is neither a slope nor a cause

Plot first. Two variables can follow a clear curve and still return a correlation near zero, because the Pearson coefficient r measures only linear association. It is unit-free, runs from −1 to +1, and is not a slope: r = 0.9 does not tell you how much y changes per unit of x. Correlation is not causation either. A third factor can drive both variables, the causal arrow may run backwards, or the sample may be selected on the outcome. Randomisation, a control group or a natural experiment licenses a causal sentence.

Pearson correlation coefficient
\[r = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \cdot \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}\]
−1 ≤ r ≤ +1; it detects linear association only

04Fitting a line by least squares

A simple linear regression fits ŷ = a + b x by choosing the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical gaps between each observation and the line. Squaring treats positive and negative misses alike and punishes large misses hardest, so one outlier can tilt the fit. The slope b is the predicted change in y per one-unit increase in x. The intercept a is the prediction at x = 0, which is often outside the measured range and physically meaningless. Quote both with units, and never present an extrapolated prediction as if it had been measured.

Least squares slope
\[b = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\]
the change in y per unit change in x
Intercept
\[a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}\]
the fitted line is ŷ = a + b x

05Judging the fit

The coefficient of determination R² is the share of the variation in y that the model accounts for: 1 means every point lies on the line, 0 means the model is no better than predicting the mean. It says nothing about whether the form of the model is right — a curve fitted over a narrow range can still score well. Residual plots are the honest test. A pattern means the model is misspecified, a funnel means the variance changes with x, and a handful of very large residuals flags influential points. Report typical error, not just R².

Coefficient of determination
\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{res}}}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\]
SS_res = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² and SS_tot = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²

06Testing a model honestly

A model is useful only if it performs on data it has not seen. Split off a test set before tuning, or cross-validate when the sample is small; otherwise the model can memorise the training rows and appear excellent. For a numeric prediction, report the mean absolute error or the root mean square error, both in the units of y; the root mean square penalises large misses more heavily. A low error on a well-chosen test set is evidence; a low error on the training set is arithmetic.

Root mean square error
\[\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}\]

07Data-quality pitfalls

Most modelling failures are data failures. Missing values may be missing for a reason — a sensor that saturates above a threshold — so deleting incomplete rows quietly changes the population being described. Outliers deserve investigation: a transcription error is a correction, but a genuine extreme is information. Leakage happens when a predictor contains information that would not exist at prediction time, giving superb test scores and a useless deployed model. Check units, duplicates, rounding that hides variation, and row order. A model trained on the future is not a forecast.

08Source, bias, inference (SBI)

Three questions keep an analysis honest. Source: who collected the numbers, with what instrument, and under what conditions? Bias: which part of the story does this sample systematically miss — a capped sensor, a convenient site, a category collapsed into "other"? Inference: how far does the evidence actually stretch, and what uncertainty belongs beside the estimate? The last question is the one exam answers forget. A slope fitted to ten points supports a direction and a rough magnitude, not a precise forecast: state the sample size, the residual error and the fitted range.

09Classification: a score becomes a decision

When the outcome is a category — defective or sound, pass or fail — the model outputs a score and a threshold turns it into a decision. Logistic regression squeezes a linear score through the sigmoid so the output is a probability; a threshold of 0.5 is a default, not a law, and moving it trades one error against the other. Judge a classifier with a confusion matrix, not accuracy alone. Precision is the share of flagged cases that are genuinely positive, and recall is the share of genuine positives that were found; they punish opposite mistakes. On imbalanced data, a model that never flags anything scores high accuracy and finds nothing.

Logistic probability
\[p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-s}}, \quad s = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + \cdots\]
the linear score s is mapped to a probability between 0 and 1
Precision and recall
\[\mathrm{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \quad \mathrm{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\]
TP, FP and FN come from the confusion matrix

10Feature engineering and scaling

The columns you hand a model are half the model. Standardisation centres a variable at zero and rescales it to unit standard deviation, which stops one large-numbered variable from dominating the fit; a log transform tames right-skewed variables such as costs and concentrations; one-hot encoding turns a nominal category into indicator columns. Scale and encode using statistics computed from the training set only, then apply the identical transform to the test set. Scaling on all the data first quietly leaks the test set into training and flatters every result.

Standardisation
\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]
μ and σ are the training-set mean and standard deviation
Fit the transform on training data only Compute means, standard deviations and category levels from the training split, then reuse them on the test set; otherwise the evaluation is optimistic.

11Baselines and honest comparison

No model is good or bad in the abstract; it is good or bad against a baseline. Before fitting anything, score the simplest defensible alternative — the historical mean, the previous period's value, or a one-rule classifier — on the same test set and report it beside the model. Compare candidates on the identical split with the identical metric, and prefer the mean absolute error when the units should be read literally: it reports the typical miss directly, where the root mean square error magnifies rare large ones. A model that cannot beat a trivial baseline is not an improvement, however clever it is.

Mean absolute error
\[\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum |y_i - \hat{y}_i|\]
the typical error in the units of y

Key terms

Residual
The vertical gap between an observed value and the value predicted by the fitted model.
Coefficient of determination
The proportion of the variation in the response that the fitted model explains.
Least squares
The fitting rule that chooses parameters by minimising the sum of squared residuals.
Sample
The subset of a population that was actually measured, and from which estimates are made.
Outlier
An observation far from the rest of the data, which may be a recording error or a genuine extreme.
Data leakage
Using a predictor that contains information unavailable at the moment a prediction must be made.
Confusion matrix
A table of true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives that shows where a classifier errs.
Standardisation
Rescaling a variable to zero mean and unit standard deviation, usually with statistics taken from the training set.
Worked example

Fitting strength against curing time

A small trial measures concrete strength y (MPa) after x days of curing at five ages: (1, 10), (2, 12), (3, 15), (4, 18), (5, 20). Fit a least squares line, find R², and interpret the slope.

  1. Summaries: Σx = 15 so x̄ = 3 days; Σy = 75 so ȳ = 15 MPa.
  2. Deviations from the mean give Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10 and Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = 10 + 3 + 0 + 3 + 10 = 26.
  3. Slope: b = 26 / 10 = 2.60 MPa per day. Intercept: a = ȳ − b x̄ = 15 − 2.60 × 3 = 7.2 MPa, so ŷ = 7.2 + 2.60x.
  4. Predictions are 9.8, 12.4, 15.0, 17.6 and 20.2 MPa, giving residuals 0.2, −0.4, 0, 0.4 and −0.2. Then SS_res = 0.40 and SS_tot = 25 + 9 + 0 + 9 + 25 = 68.
  5. R² = 1 − 0.40/68 = 0.994, so the line accounts for 99.4% of the variation in strength over these five ages.
Answerŷ = 7.2 + 2.60x (MPa), with R² = 0.994. Each extra day is associated with about 2.6 MPa more predicted strength, but with five points and a range of only 1–5 days the line should not be extrapolated to a 28-day design strength.

Exam method

  • Give the slope units (y-units per x-unit) and the intercept units — a bare number loses the mark.
  • Never describe R² as accuracy: it is the share of variation explained, not a percentage of correct predictions.
  • Plot the data and then the residuals before defending any fit; a pattern in the residuals means the model is wrong.
  • State the fitted range and the sample size, and say when a prediction is an extrapolation.
  • Report a naive baseline on the same test set before claiming any model is good; a score is only meaningful relative to something.

Q01A regression predicts concrete strength from curing time and reports R² = 0.02. The most accurate reading is:

WhyR² is a share of variance, not a percentage of correct predictions; here the line is barely better than predicting the mean.

Q02A least squares fit of strength against curing time gives b = 2.6 MPa per day. Which sentence is correct?

WhyThe slope is a predicted change per unit of x; association is not proof of cause, and the slope is not a percentage.

Q03A model predicts 28-day strength using a ductility test recorded after the concrete was poured, and scores superbly on both training and test data. The most likely fault is:

WhyLeakage lets information from the future into the predictors; the model looks excellent but cannot be used at prediction time.

Q04For right-skewed repair-cost data, which pair best describes a typical value and its spread?

WhyThe median and interquartile range are resistant to the long tail, while the mean and standard deviation are pulled by it.

Q05A defect detector is run on 400 panels: it flags 60, of which 45 are genuinely defective, and 25 genuine defects go unflagged. Its precision is:

WhyPrecision is the share of flagged panels that were really defective: TP/(TP + FP) = 45/60 = 0.75.

Q06A team standardises its variables using the mean and standard deviation of the full dataset before splitting. The likely consequence is:

WhyComputing the transform on all rows lets the test set influence the training pipeline, inflating the reported performance.
