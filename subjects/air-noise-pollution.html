What we breathe and what we hear are both energy problems, measured in concentrations and decibels and controlled by understanding how a pollutant travels from source to receptor. This page covers air-quality units and dispersion, then environmental noise.

01Source, pathway, receptor

Air and noise problems share one structure: something emits, the emission travels and changes, and a receptor is exposed. Control can act at any stage:
- Source: cleaner fuel, process changes
- Pathway: taller stack, barrier
- Receptor: insulation, land-use separation

The earliest option is usually the cheapest. A tall stack is not a treatment device: it lowers ground-level concentration by spreading the same mass through a larger volume — the pollutant is diluted, not destroyed.

02Measuring what is in the air

Concentrations are reported as mass per volume (μg/m³) or volume fraction (ppm or ppb), linked through the gas's molar mass using a molar volume of 24.45 L at 25 °C and 1 atm. The instrument matters as much as the number:
- Gravimetric samplers: weigh filtered particulate matter; reference method
- Real-time monitors: reading every few seconds; need calibration
- Passive diffusion tubes: cheap; integrate over weeks

Averaging time is part of the result: a one-hour peak and an annual mean describe different effects.

Volume fraction to mass concentration
\[C\,(\mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{m}^3) = \frac{C\,(\mathrm{ppm}) \times M \times 1000}{24.45}\]
M is molar mass in g/mol; 24.45 L/mol at 25 °C and 1 atm

03Dispersion: the Gaussian plume

Once released, a plume spreads by turbulence. The standard screening model treats concentration as Gaussian across the wind and vertically, with spread parameters σy and σz that grow with distance and depend on atmospheric stability. Stability is graded from class A (very unstable, strong convection) to class F (very stable, clear and calm). Unstable air mixes strongly and lowers ground-level peaks; a stable night traps the plume near the ground. The effective stack height H adds plume rise from buoyancy and momentum to the physical stack, and it dominates because concentration falls roughly exponentially with H.

Gaussian plume, ground-level centreline
\[C = \frac{Q}{\pi u \sigma_y \sigma_z} \exp\left(-\frac{H^2}{2 \sigma_z^2}\right)\]
Q in g/s, u in m/s, σy and σz in m, H the effective height

04What the plume model assumes

The Gaussian model is a screening tool. It assumes steady wind, flat terrain, no building downwash, no chemical reaction and a single source, so it is best used for comparing options rather than for proving compliance. Where those assumptions fail the error is large: valleys and street canyons trap pollutants, an inversion caps the mixing depth, and reactions form secondary pollutants such as ozone well downwind. A real assessment adds terrain correction, a measured background and monitoring.

05Controlling air pollution

Control options fall into prevention, collection and dilution:
- Prevention: cleaner fuel, low-NOx burners, process changes, energy efficiency
- Collection: cyclones for coarse dust; electrostatic precipitators and fabric filters for fine particles; wet scrubbers for soluble gases; catalytic converters on vehicles
- Dilution: tall stack — last resort, turns a local problem into a regional one

Siting decisions and buffer zones are planning controls, and often the most effective.

06Air quality management and the health account

Measurement exists to be compared with an objective. Air quality management sets objectives for each pollutant — annual and short-term limits for particulates, nitrogen dioxide, ozone and sulphur dioxide — and reports the comparison publicly through an index that translates measured concentrations into simple bands and health advice. The health account explains why the objectives matter: fine particles penetrate to the deep lung, ozone irritates the airways, and the burden falls most on children, the elderly and those who already have heart or lung disease. Management therefore acts on exposure as well as emissions: an emission inventory identifies the sources, dispersion modelling maps who is exposed, and the control plan reduces the highest-exposure source first.

Air quality management cycle
inventory → monitor → model → compare with objectives → control → monitor
the loop repeats; objectives and the health evidence set the targets

07Sound is a pressure fluctuation

Sound is a small, fast variation in air pressure. Because the ear responds over an enormous range, levels are logarithmic: the sound pressure level compares the measured pressure with a reference of 20 μPa, roughly the threshold of hearing. Doubling the pressure adds 6 dB; multiplying it by ten adds 20 dB. Adding sources uses energy, not arithmetic. Two independent sources of equal level give 3 dB more rather than double the decibel value, so levels are combined by summing their energies.

Sound pressure level
\[L_p = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)\]
p₀ = 20 μPa; the result is in decibels
Adding incoherent sources
\[L_{\text{total}} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\sum 10^{L_i/10}\right)\]

08Frequency, weighting and the A-scale

The ear is not flat: it hears mid frequencies best and low frequencies poorly, and this sensitivity changes with level. Instruments measure in octave or third-octave bands, then apply a frequency weighting that mimics the ear. The A-weighting is the standard for environmental noise, so a traffic level quoted as 70 dB(A) has already been de-emphasised where hearing is weak. Combining band levels is an energy sum: each band level has its A-weighting added, the results are converted back from decibels, summed, and converted again. The A-weighted total is almost always lower than the unweighted total, because the deep bass bands carry large energies that the ear barely notices.

A-weighted total from bands
\[L_A = 10 \log_{10}\left(\sum_i 10^{(L_i + A_i)/10}\right)\]
Lᵢ band level in dB, Aᵢ the A-weighting correction in dB for that band

09Averaging noise over time: Leq

Environmental noise is never steady, so it is summarised by the energy-equivalent level Leq: the constant level that would deliver the same sound energy over the measurement period. It is a logarithmic average and is always dominated by the loudest periods. Other descriptors describe the distribution rather than the energy. L10 is the level exceeded for 10% of the time; L90 is exceeded for 90% and is often taken as the background. To recover a source level from a total and a background, subtract energies.

Equivalent continuous level
\[L_{\mathrm{eq}} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{T} \sum t_i\, 10^{L_i/10}\right)\]
Source level by background subtraction
\[L_s = 10 \log_{10}\left(10^{L_t/10} - 10^{L_b/10}\right)\]
L_t is the total, L_b the background

10Predicting traffic noise

Road noise is often predicted rather than measured, using a relationship between the traffic flow and a percentile level such as L10 — the level exceeded for ten per cent of the hour, which tracks the noisier part of the traffic stream. A common form adds ten times the logarithm of the hourly flow to a constant, so doubling the flow adds about 3 dB. Distance and spreading then apply: a line source such as a road loses about 3 dB for each doubling of distance. Predicted levels feed design — barrier heights, facade insulation, setbacks — so a prediction must state its traffic data, its speed and its ground conditions.

Traffic noise, L10 (1h)
\[L_{10} = 42.2 + 10 \log_{10} q\]
q light vehicles per hour at moderate speed; corrections apply for speed, gradient and heavy vehicles
Line source distance correction
\[L_2 = L_1 - 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)\]
a road is a line source: doubling distance costs about 3 dB

11Noise control and barriers

Control follows the same source–path–receiver logic: quieter plant and enclosures at the source, barriers and berms on the path, better glazing and ventilation at the receiver. A barrier works by forcing sound to diffract over its top edge, so it must break the line of sight and extend past it. The extra distance travelled, the path difference δ, sets the Fresnel number, and the insertion loss grows with it up to a practical ceiling. Low frequencies bend around the edge easily, so barriers are weakest at low frequency.

Fresnel number
\[N = \frac{2 \delta}{\lambda}\]
δ is the path difference in m; λ = c / f
Barrier attenuation, Maekawa approximation
\[\mathrm{IL} \approx 10 \log_{10}(3 + 20 N)\]
N ≥ 0; real barriers depend on material and leaks

Key terms

Leq
The steady sound level that would deliver the same sound energy as the real, varying noise over a stated period.
Decibel
A logarithmic ratio of a measured quantity to a reference value, used because hearing responds over a huge range.
Pasquill stability class
A grade from A to F describing how strongly the atmosphere mixes a released plume.
Effective stack height
Physical stack height plus plume rise from buoyancy and momentum, treated as the release height in dispersion models.
Insertion loss
The reduction in sound pressure level at a receiver caused by fitting a barrier, enclosure or screen.
PM2.5
Airborne particles below 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter, small enough to reach the deep lung.
A-weighting
A frequency weighting that mimics the ear's sensitivity so that one number approximates perceived loudness, reported as dB(A).
L10
The noise level exceeded for ten per cent of a measurement period, used as the standard descriptor for road traffic noise.
Worked example

Ground-level concentration from a stack

A stack releases sulphur dioxide at Q = 50 g/s. The wind speed is 4 m/s, the effective release height is H = 60 m, and at the receptor 2 km downwind the spread parameters are σy = 250 m and σz = 150 m. Estimate the ground-level concentration on the plume centreline.

  1. On the centreline y = 0 and at ground level z = 0, so the cross-wind term is 1 and the two vertical terms add to twice the same exponential. The result is C = Q / (π u σy σz) · exp[−H²/(2σz²)].
  2. Denominator: π × 4 × 250 × 150 = 4.712 × 10⁵ m³/s.
  3. Exponent: H²/(2σz²) = 60² / (2 × 150²) = 3600 / 45 000 = 0.080, so exp(−0.080) = 0.923.
  4. Then C = (50 × 0.923) / (4.712 × 10⁵) = 9.79 × 10⁻⁵ g/m³, and multiplying by 10⁶ gives 97.9 μg/m³.
  5. Sanity check: C is proportional to 1/u, so doubling the wind speed would halve the value to about 49 μg/m³. A value near 100 μg/m³ is well above a typical 24-hour air-quality objective, so the release needs control or a greater effective height.
AnswerThe ground-level centreline concentration is about 98 μg/m³, which would exceed a typical 24-hour objective by a wide margin; the plume model therefore flags the stack for emission control or redesign rather than accepting it.

Exam method

  • Convert ppm to μg/m³ (or back) before comparing with a guideline, and state the temperature and pressure you assumed.
  • Add levels as energies: two equal sources give +3 dB, never double the number, and a background 10 dB below a source changes it by only about 0.4 dB.
  • In barrier questions, find the path difference δ first; the wavelength follows from λ = c/f.
  • State the averaging time — a one-hour peak, an eight-hour Leq and an annual mean are not interchangeable.
  • Always attach the descriptor and the period to a level — 70 dB(A) L10(1h) is a different fact from 70 dB(A) Leq(8h) — and add levels as energies, never by arithmetic.

WhyIndependent sources add energies, and doubling the energy adds 10 log₁₀2 ≈ 3 dB, so the total is about 88 dB.

WhyPressure falls with distance, so L₂ = L₁ − 20 log₁₀(r₂/r₁) = L₁ − 6 dB for a doubling.

WhyLeq is an energy average, so loud periods dominate it; the level exceeded for 90% of the time is L90.

WhyThe concentration is inversely proportional to wind speed, which dilutes the same mass into a larger volume of air.

WhyL10 rises with 10 log₁₀ q, and doubling q adds 10 log₁₀ 2 ≈ 3 dB.
