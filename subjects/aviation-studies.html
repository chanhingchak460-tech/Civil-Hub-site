How aircraft fly and how the ground system that supports them is engineered: the four forces, lift and stall, runway and terminal constraints, air traffic separation, and the industry's environmental account.

01Four forces, one balance

Flight is a balance of four forces: lift upward, weight downward, thrust along the flight path and drag against it. In steady level cruise lift equals weight and thrust equals drag; any imbalance changes speed, altitude or both.

  • Lift grows with air density, airspeed squared and wing area.
  • Density falls with altitude and rises in cold air.
  • Runway performance therefore depends on the day's temperature and pressure, not just the aircraft.
Lift
\[L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_L\]
ρ density, V airspeed, S area, C_L lift coefficient
Steady level flight
\[L = W, \quad T = D\]
W weight, T thrust, D drag
Drag
\[D = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_D\]
C_D the drag coefficient; total drag adds parasite and induced parts

02Angle of attack and the stall

The angle of attack is the angle between the wing chord and the oncoming air. Lift rises with it until the airflow can no longer follow the upper surface; beyond the critical angle the wing stalls and lift drops sharply. A stall is about angle, not speed.

  • Flaps and slats raise low-speed lift at the cost of extra drag, so they are retracted for cruise.
  • Induced drag falls as speed rises, because less angle of attack is needed for the same lift.
  • Total drag therefore has a minimum at one speed, where the lift-to-drag ratio is highest and range is longest.
Lift-to-drag ratio
\[\frac{L}{D} = \frac{C_L}{C_D}\]
maximum at the speed for best range in still air

03Navigation aids and the instrument approach

Aircraft navigate by a chain of aids:

  • VOR radiates bearings so a receiver can hold a radial.
  • DME adds distance.
  • Satellite navigation fixes position anywhere with no ground station.
  • The instrument landing system guides the final approach with localiser and glide slope beams followed to the runway threshold.

An instrument approach is a choreographed sequence: the arrival route feeds a published approach procedure, the aircraft intercepts the localiser and glide slope, and stabilisation gates check speed and configuration at fixed heights. If the runway is not in sight at the decision height, the approach is abandoned and a designed missed approach procedure flown.

Instrument approach sequence
arrival → approach procedure → localiser intercept → glide slope → decision height
stabilised by fixed gates; a missed approach is a designed path, not a failure

04The flight deck and its instruments

Six basic instruments tell the pilot the aircraft's state, arranged in the classic T-pattern: airspeed indicator, attitude indicator, altimeter and vertical speed indicator across the top, with heading and turn coordination below. Modern glass cockpits merge them onto screens, but the underlying quantities are unchanged: speed, attitude, altitude, rate, heading and turn.

The instruments work on different physics — pitot pressure for speed, static pressure for altitude, gyros for attitude and heading — and that independence matters. When one disagrees with the others, the pilot cross-checks rather than trusting the odd one out; scan, interpret, verify is the discipline of the whole panel.

The six-pack T-pattern
airspeed · attitude · altitude · vertical speed · heading · turn
the six basic quantities every instrument scan must cover

05Airports are engineered systems

An airport separates two flows: aircraft on the airside — runway, taxiways and apron — and people and goods on the landside, meaning the terminal, road access and baggage system. Security and border control sit on the boundary.

  • Runway orientation follows the prevailing wind, since aircraft take off and land into wind.
  • Runway length is set by the most demanding aircraft and the worst conditions: high altitude, high temperature, a wet surface and an approach obstacle all lengthen it.
  • Capacity is rarely one number: runway movements, stands, check-in desks and baggage belts each impose a limit, and the airport's capacity is the tightest.

06Runway markings, lighting and airspace

The runway is a visual system as well as a physical one:

  • Markings identify the threshold, the centreline, the touchdown zone and the aiming point.
  • Lights guide the approach and show the edge, centreline and end, in colours the pilot reads as position.
  • Signs and painted holding points stop aircraft short of the protected zone around an active runway.

Around the airport, airspace is organised into controlled and uncontrolled classes with defined services and entry rules, and standard departure and arrival routes connect the runway to the airway network. Markings, lights and airspace all exist so that one pilot's intentions are legible to everyone else without a radio call.

markings → lighting → signs → holding points → protected surfaces

07Air traffic management and safety

Air traffic control keeps aircraft apart by assigning routes, altitudes and times, using radar and satellite surveillance. Separation standards are the core idea: two aircraft must never occupy the same airspace at once.

Flow management smooths peaks by delaying departures on the ground rather than holding aircraft in the air; slots allocate scarce runway time. Safety is managed through reporting rather than blame. Occurrences are recorded, trends analysed and procedures changed, so a healthy system learns from near misses before they become accidents.

08Why aviation matters economically

Air transport carries little world freight by weight but much by value, because it moves urgent, high-value and perishable goods. For passengers it compresses distance, which supports tourism, business travel and family links across countries.

  • Aviation supports employment directly, in flight and ground crew, and indirectly through manufacturing, logistics and tourism.
  • For some regions, connectivity is the difference between participating in the global economy and being peripheral.
  • Hub airports amplify this by concentrating connecting traffic into a few banks of flights.

09The environmental account

Aviation produces roughly 2–3% of global carbon dioxide emissions, and its non-carbon effects — nitrogen oxides, contrails and cloud formation — add to the climate impact. Noise is the other local burden, felt most under flight paths.

The levers are efficiency, fuel and operations:

  • Newer engines and airframes burn less per seat-kilometre.
  • Sustainable aviation fuel can cut lifecycle carbon substantially but remains scarce and costly.
  • Better routeing, continuous descent and reduced taxiing save fuel without new hardware.
  • Airports contribute through energy, water, waste and surface access.

Emissions per passenger fall while total traffic rises, so absolute emissions depend on both.

Key terms

Lift
The aerodynamic force perpendicular to the airflow that supports an aircraft in flight.
Angle of attack
The angle between the wing chord line and the oncoming airflow.
Stall
The sudden loss of lift when the angle of attack exceeds its critical value.
Induced drag
Drag produced as a by-product of generating lift; it falls as speed rises.
Airside
The part of an airport beyond security where aircraft, runways and aprons are located.
Sustainable aviation fuel
A drop-in fuel made from non-fossil feedstocks that lowers lifecycle carbon emissions.
VOR
A ground radio beacon that lets an aircraft read and hold a bearing, one of the classic en-route navigation aids.
Instrument landing system
Radio beams giving lateral and vertical guidance down a precision approach to the runway threshold.
Worked example

Estimating take-off lift

An aircraft has a wing area of 120 m² and a lift coefficient of 1.2 at rotation. On a day when air density is 1.20 kg/m³, it rotates at a true airspeed of 80 m/s. Estimate the lift and compare it with the weight of an aircraft of mass 56 000 kg (take g = 9.81 m/s²).

  1. Apply the lift equation: L = ½ ρ V² S C_L = 0.5 × 1.20 × 80² × 120 × 1.2.
  2. Evaluate in parts: 80² = 6400, then 0.5 × 1.20 × 6400 = 3840 N/m² of wing area coefficient, so 3840 × 120 × 1.2 = 552 960 N.
  3. Compute the weight: W = m g = 56 000 × 9.81 = 549 360 N, about 549 kN.
  4. Compare: lift is about 553 kN against a weight of about 549 kN, a narrow surplus.
  5. Sanity check: at a lower density, such as a hot high-altitude day, the same speed would give less lift, which is exactly why take-off distance grows.
AnswerThe lift is about 553 kN, just above the aircraft's weight of about 549 kN, so it can leave the ground with little margin; that margin is why take-off performance is sensitive to density, mass and speed.

Exam method

  • Draw the four forces before any calculation and state which pairs balance in the flight condition given.
  • In performance questions, check whether air density changes: altitude, temperature and humidity all alter lift and runway length.
  • For sustainability questions, separate emissions per passenger from total emissions, because the two trends can move in opposite directions.
  • Distinguish airside from landside when describing constraints, and name the actual bottleneck instead of listing every component.
  • Split drag into parasite (rises with V²) and induced (falls with V²) whenever a question asks why a minimum-drag speed exists.

Q01In steady, level flight at constant speed, which pair of forces balances?

WhyThere is no vertical acceleration and no change of speed, so lift balances weight and thrust balances drag.

Q02An aerofoil stalls when:

WhyBeyond the critical angle the boundary layer separates and lift falls sharply, regardless of airspeed.

Q03For a given aircraft and load, the required runway length generally increases when:

WhyThinner, warmer air reduces lift and thrust, so a higher true airspeed and a longer run are needed.

Q04The component of the instrument landing system that guides the aircraft laterally onto the runway centreline is the:

WhyThe localiser beam defines the lateral path and the glide slope the vertical path; together they form the ILS.

Q05In level flight at a given weight, total drag is the sum of parasite drag and induced drag. As speed increases:

WhyParasite drag grows with speed squared while induced drag falls as less angle of attack is needed; their sum has a minimum at the best-range speed.
