Building information models describe individual assets with rich data, while 3D GIS describes the ground they sit on. Combining them needs open standards, a common data environment and a shared coordinate system.

01BIM is an information model, not a drawing

A building information model is a set of objects — walls, slabs, pipes, spaces — each carrying geometry, relationships and attributes. Because each object is defined once, the same model can produce drawings, schedules, quantities, coordination checks and handover data.

A federated model combines discipline models into one coordination environment while each author keeps control of their own file. Model quality, naming and information structure matter as much as the drawing itself.

The model is also the record handed to the operator at completion, so its information should be defined by the people who will use it, not only by those who build it.

02The dimensions of BIM

Information models are described by dimensions. Three dimensions give geometry; the higher dimensions add information that other people use.

  • The numbering is a communication device rather than a formal standard, so agree on what each dimension means on a project.
  • The value of the higher dimensions depends on the lower ones.
  • A schedule or cost model built on badly named and uncoordinated objects produces confident answers that are wrong.

03IFC and open exchange

IFC (Industry Foundation Classes) is the open, vendor-neutral schema for exchanging building models. It defines entities such as walls, spaces and properties, and the relationships between them, so a model authored in one tool can be read in another without losing its meaning.

  • Exchange is controlled by model view definitions, which select the subset of the schema needed for a purpose such as coordination or quantity take-off.
  • Property sets carry the attributes a receiving party needs, and classification systems align objects with work breakdowns.
  • IFC does not replace native formats; it is the contract between them.

04CityGML and semantic city models

CityGML is the open standard for 3D city models: thematic classes such as buildings, terrain, roads and vegetation, organised by graded levels of detail from a block model up to full interiors. Unlike IFC, which describes one building's components for design and construction, CityGML describes many assets and their context for analysis and administration.

Objects carry semantics as well as surfaces, so a query can ask for all residential floors above a flood level rather than a bare mesh. Because the two schemas overlap but do not align one-to-one, converting between them needs a defined mapping, and every conversion loses some information on the way.

Two schemas, two jobs

05The common data environment

A common data environment (CDE) is the single agreed source of project information. Models and documents are held in containers and move through controlled states: work in progress, shared, published and archived. Only shared or published information may be relied on by other parties.

  • Information requirements — what information, in what format, at what date — are defined before work starts, and the delivery plan says who provides it.
  • Version control, naming and status codes show which file is current, which solves the oldest problem in coordination.
  • Where several organisations contribute, the environment also records who approved each container and when, so responsibility stays traceable.

06GIS data models and 3D city models

GIS describes the world with spatial data models. Vector models store points, lines and polygons with attributes and topology, suited to networks and boundaries; raster models store grid cells, suited to continuous surfaces such as terrain and imagery; TINs represent terrain as triangulated surfaces.

Three-dimensional city models add height, roofs and detail, graded by level of detail: a simple block model, then roof shapes, then detailed facades. Because a city model and a building model describe the same place at different scales, the two are increasingly linked rather than kept apart. GIS is strong on location, scale and many datasets; BIM is strong on individual assets and their properties.

Raster cell count
\[N = \frac{W \times H}{c^2}\]
W and H are the extent dimensions and c the cell size; halving c quadruples the cell count

07From point clouds to as-built models

Existing structures rarely match their drawings, so capture starts with laser scanning or photogrammetry producing a point cloud — millions of measured coordinates. A time-of-flight scanner derives each range from the round-trip time of a laser pulse, and the density follows from the scanning resolution: halving the point spacing quadruples the stored points.

Turning the cloud into a model is scan-to-BIM: register the scans into one coordinate system, clean the noise, classify points into structure, ground and services, then trace or auto-fit the model elements. The process is part automation and part judgement — software proposes, an engineer confirms, and the model inherits every error left in the cloud.

Time-of-flight range
\[R = \frac{c \Delta t}{2}\]
c is the speed of light and Δt the round-trip time; the factor 2 accounts for the return path
Point density
\[\rho = \frac{N}{A}\]
N points over area A; fine grids suit small features but grow storage quickly

08Coordinate systems and georeferencing

BIM models are authored in local coordinates, but infrastructure lives in a coordinate reference system. A geographic system uses latitude and longitude on a datum; a projected system flattens it onto a grid in metres. Combining them needs a transformation with translation, rotation and scale:

E = E_0 + s(x cos θ − y sin θ)
N = N_0 + s(x sin θ + y cos θ)

where (x, y) are model coordinates, θ the rotation between model and grid north, s the scale and (E_0, N_0) the translation. A wrong sign or a stale datum shifts a model by metres, and every asset on it inherits the error.

Model to grid transformation
\[E = E_0 + s(x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta), \quad N = N_0 + s(x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)\]
θ measured from model north to grid north; s near 1 for a map projection

093D analysis and web GIS

3D GIS earns its keep in analysis: a viewshed shows which terrain is visible from a point, shadow models test daylight on proposed towers, flood simulation checks which basement entries a storm reaches, and line-of-sight checks whether a crane can reach a lift zone. Each analysis needs a terrain surface, the asset model and a correctly shared coordinate system.

Web GIS moves these into browsers through scene services, so stakeholders review without desktop licences; BIM viewers do the same for the building model, and the two are increasingly embedded in one dashboard. What travels across the web is the published, checked version — never the working file.

Combined scale factor
\[\text{grid distance} = \text{ground distance} \times \mathrm{CSF}\]
CSF combines the projection scale factor with the height factor; survey measurements are reduced to grid distance before entering GIS

10Digital twins and interoperability

A digital twin is a model kept current with an asset: as-built geometry, asset data and live sensor readings in one environment. It supports operation and maintenance rather than construction, so it depends on consistent identifiers between model, GIS and asset register.

Interoperability is the practical limit. Geometry may transfer while properties, units, materials and classifications are lost, so exchanges are tested against a defined purpose and a checking rule set. Clash detection and automated checking find conflicts and non-compliance, but only against the rules that were written.

11Joining BIM and GIS data

Integration is mostly a data problem, not a visual one. The two worlds must share identifiers — a building or component must carry the same reference in the asset register, the GIS feature and the model — or every update diverges.

ETL pipelines map IFC classes onto the GIS schema, drop construction detail that analysis does not need, and log what was transformed. Indoor GIS extends the same logic inside buildings: rooms, routes and sensors referenced to a floor plan.

The practical test of any integration is the round trip — change the asset register, then confirm the change appears in both the GIS view and the BIM view without manual re-keying.

Common data environment
The agreed single source of project information, with controlled states for work in progress, shared, published and archived data.

A graded measure of how much geometric and semantic detail a 3D city model represents, from block models to detailed facades.
Georeferencing
Locating a model in a coordinate reference system so that it aligns with survey and GIS data.
Digital twin
A model kept current with a physical asset through as-built data and ongoing sensor information.
Clash detection
Automated checking of a federated model for physical or spatial conflicts between disciplines.
CityGML
An open standard for semantic 3D city models, describing buildings, terrain and infrastructure by theme and level of detail.
Scan-to-BIM
The workflow of registering, classifying and tracing a point cloud into an as-built information model.
Worked example

Georeferencing a local model

A building model uses local coordinates in metres. A column centre sits at x = 120.000 m, y = 45.000 m. The model must be placed on a project grid with a rotation of 30° from model north to grid north, a scale factor of 0.9996, and a translation to grid easting 500 000.000 m and northing 100 000.000 m. Find the grid coordinates of the column and check that distance from the origin is preserved apart from the scale.

  1. cos 30° = 0.866025 and sin 30° = 0.5.
  2. East offset: x cos θ − y sin θ = 120 × 0.866025 − 45 × 0.5 = 103.923 − 22.500 = 81.423 m.
  3. North offset: x sin θ + y cos θ = 120 × 0.5 + 45 × 0.866025 = 60.000 + 38.971 = 98.971 m.
  4. Apply the scale: 0.9996 × 81.423 = 81.390 m and 0.9996 × 98.971 = 98.931 m.
  5. Add the translation: E = 500 081.390 m, N = 100 098.931 m.
  6. Sanity check: local offset length was √(120² + 45²) = 128.160 m; scaled it becomes 128.109 m, and the transformed offsets give √(81.390² + 98.931²) = 128.108 m, matching within rounding.
AnswerThe column is at grid easting 500 081.390 m and northing 100 098.931 m. The check confirms rotation and scale were applied consistently.

Exam method

  • Distinguish the model from the information: geometry, properties, relationships and status are separate questions.
  • For CDE questions, name the state and say who may rely on the information in that state.
  • When transforming coordinates, write rotation and scale separately before adding translation — it exposes sign errors.
  • In integration questions, name the shared identifier and the coordinate system first; everything else is downstream of those two choices.
