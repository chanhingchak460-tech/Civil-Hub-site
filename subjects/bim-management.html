Buildings described as structured information rather than drawings: one coordinated model, defined requirements and a controlled way of exchanging data through every stage. BIM management is the discipline of making that information trustworthy.

01The model is only half the subject

Building Information Modelling describes a building through structured digital information, not a single clever file. Each discipline produces its own information container — a model, a schedule, a report — and those containers are brought together into a federated model that stays coordinated without merging ownership.

A 3D picture is the visible part, but the value sits in the data attached to it: what an element is, where it belongs, what it costs, when it is installed and who is responsible. A model without defined uses and requirements is an expensive drawing.

02Dimensions: 3D to 7D

The dimensions describe what is layered onto the geometry.

  • 3D is the coordinated spatial model.
  • 4D adds time by linking elements to construction activities.
  • 5D adds cost through quantities and rates.
  • 6D adds energy and sustainability analysis.
  • 7D covers operation and maintenance information.

The names are shorthand for uses, not a maturity ladder: a project can use 4D without a 5D cost model. Adding a dimension means adding discipline: the schedule must be maintained, the cost data priced, the asset data structured. That is why BIM uses are agreed and scoped rather than assumed.

Dimension stack
3D geometry → + 4D time → + 5D cost → + 6D energy → + 7D operation
each dimension is a use layered on the same model, not a replacement

03ISO 19650 and the requirements chain

Modern BIM management rests on a chain of information requirements. Organisational requirements (OIR) set the strategy and cascade into asset information requirements (AIR) and project information requirements (PIR); together these become exchange information requirements (EIR) for each appointment. The appointing party asks; the delivery team answers with a BIM execution plan (BEP) describing how it will meet them.

The same chain runs through the information model: the project information model is created during delivery, while the asset information model supports operation after handover. The discipline is delivering the right information, to the right people, in the right form, at the right time.

Requirements chain
OIR → AIR / PIR → EIR → BEP → TIDP + MIDP → information containers
trace any deliverable back to its owner and purpose

04EIR and BEP: the contract for information

The EIR defines what information is exchanged, when, by whom, to what level of detail and in what format. It must be precise enough to price and check; vague requirements produce models that nobody can use. Level of information need clarifies how much geometry, data and documentation is actually required at each stage.

The delivery team answers twice. The pre-appointment BEP shows approach, capability and capacity; the post-appointment BEP sets out methods, standards, the CDE and the delivery plans. Task teams write TIDPs for their own deliverables; the lead consultant combines them into the MIDP.

05Level of information need

Level of information need replaces vague detail labels: for each element and stage, state how much geometry, alphanumeric information and documentation each purpose requires. A door may need full 3D geometry at construction but only its fire rating, hardware set and maintenance interval at handover — more detail everywhere is a cost, not a virtue.

Setting the level per purpose stops both under-modelling (a drawing pretending to be a model) and over-modelling (geometry nobody will ever read). In practice a team writes it into the EIR and BEP as a table of model elements against stages, so every author knows exactly when detail is due and can be checked against it.

Level of information need
need = geometry + alphanumeric information + documentation
three columns defined per element and per stage; agree them before modelling starts

06The common data environment

A CDE is one source of truth for project information, with controlled workflows rather than a shared folder. Information moves through four states:

  • Work in progress belongs to the author.
  • Shared has been checked and is available to the team.
  • Published has been authorised for a defined purpose.
  • Archived is retained as a record.

Each state has rules for naming, revision and status codes, plus access permissions. The CDE is also where federation happens: discipline models are combined for review without losing the link to their authors.

07Naming, classification and data quality

Reliable exchange starts with naming. Every information container gets a unique identifier built from coded fields — project, originator, volume, level, type and revision — separated by fixed delimiters and restricted to a safe character set. A name that encodes nothing is fine for one author and unmanageable for a federation of forty. Classification aligns objects with a recognised system of work breakdowns so schedules and cost plans can consume the model automatically.

Data quality is then checked in layers:

  • a visual check that the geometry looks right,
  • an integrity check that properties are defined, sensible and unique,
  • rule-based checks that the required information exists for each agreed purpose.

Checking is scheduled before every exchange, not after disputes.

Container identifier
project-originator-volume-level-type-revision
hyphen-separated alphanumeric fields; every revision of every file stays traceable
Layered model check
quality = visual check + integrity check + rule check
run all three before shared status, on a fixed schedule
Never share what you have not checked A shared container that fails the integrity check becomes the source of everyone else's errors. Run the checks, record the result, then move the status.

08Clash detection and coordination

Clash detection finds geometric conflicts between discipline models before they become site problems.

  • A hard clash is two components occupying the same space.
  • A soft clash is insufficient clearance for installation, access or maintenance.
  • A logical clash is a sequencing conflict, such as work blocked by an activity that has not finished.

Software only finds candidates. A useful workflow filters hits by tolerance and priority, groups them by responsibility, issues them to the team that must change its model, then re-runs the checks. Expect false positives, and agree tolerances in the BEP.

09Capability, maturity and the pre-appointment phase

Before appointing anyone, the client tests capability: can a prospective team run the information management processes this project needs? A structured capability assessment questions their standards, security, modelling processes and CDE experience, and the answers feed tender evaluation rather than decoration. Maturity describes where an organisation sits on the road from isolated drawing production to fully managed information exchange.

In multi-stage procurement this happens early: capability is assessed at tender, the pre-appointment BEP shows the approach, and the contract then fixes the post-appointment BEP and delivery plans. Skipping the assessment merely moves the discovery cost to mid-project, when changing direction is far more expensive.

Pairwise model interfaces
\[\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\]
with n discipline models, the number of pairwise combinations a federation must keep coordinated

10BIM across the lifecycle

Each stage has appropriate uses. Early stages need existing conditions, site analysis and design authoring; design coordination needs spatial coordination and design review; construction adds 4D sequencing, 5D cost, site planning and fabrication; handover produces as-built information and the asset model for operation.

Asset information tests the whole process: if the maintenance team cannot find the equipment, its location and its history in the model, delivery has failed at the last step. What asset data is needed at handover should be decided at the start, not later.

BIM uses by project stage
StageTypical useMain information output

Construction4D sequencing, 5D cost and site planningCoordinated model, quantities, as-built updates

11Technologies around the model

The model is surrounded by capture and presentation technology. Laser scanning and photogrammetry capture existing conditions as point clouds — millions of measured 3D points — from which as-built models are traced. Scanning density sets the detail: halving the point spacing quadruples the points per square metre. Drones extend capture to roofs and whole sites, while IoT sensors feed live performance back into the model during construction and operation.

VR puts stakeholders inside the unbuilt design for review; AR overlays model information on the real site for setting out, inspection and maintenance. Each technology earns its place by closing a specific gap — capture, review or feedback — and each brings a data-management burden the CDE must absorb.

Point cloud density
\[\rho = \frac{1}{d^2}\]
d is the point spacing in metres and ρ the points per square metre; finer spacing costs scanning time and file size

12Roles, responsibility and the limits

Information management is a set of functions, not one job title. The appointing party has an information manager; the delivery team has its own; each task team has a manager, an information manager and an interface manager for coordination; information authors produce and own their content. A responsibility matrix records who produces, checks, reviews and authorises each container.

BIM has limits worth stating. Interoperability between software remains imperfect, so open formats and agreed standards matter; data quality depends on disciplined checking; and training, licences and coordination time cost money. The benefits follow from management, not from software.

Key terms

Federated model
A review model assembled from separate discipline models, linked together without giving up each author's ownership.
Information container
A named and versioned package of information such as a model, a drawing, a schedule or a report.
Exchange information requirements
The appointing party's statement of what information must be delivered, when, and in what form.
BIM execution plan
The delivery team's response describing how it will produce and manage the required information.
Common data environment
The agreed single source of project information, with controlled states, naming and access rules.
Clash detection
Automated checking of combined models for physical overlaps, clearance failures or sequencing conflicts.
Level of information need
A statement of how much geometry, information and documentation each element, stage and purpose requires.
Point cloud
A large set of measured 3D points from laser scanning or photogrammetry, used to capture existing conditions.
Worked example

Information flow for a construction-stage clash check

A client's exchange information requirements demand a coordinated federated model at each monthly milestone, with clashes resolved at the agreed tolerance. Describe how that requirement travels from the client to a resolved clash, and what counts as evidence that the milestone is met.

  1. The client's organisational and project requirements feed the EIR: a coordinated model, monthly, at a stated tolerance and in a stated format.
  2. The delivery team answers in the BEP: federation strategy, model standards, CDE workflow, responsibility matrix and the milestone dates.
  3. Each task team's TIDP names its own models and delivery dates; the models are produced, self-checked and uploaded to work in progress on the CDE.
  4. The information manager checks status and revision codes, moves accepted models to shared, and federates them for coordination.
  5. Clash rules are run: hard clashes at zero tolerance, clearance clashes at the project tolerance. Hits are filtered, grouped and issued to the responsible task team.
  6. The task teams revise their models, the rules are re-run, and only then is the coordinated model published for the milestone, with asset data scheduled for the handover model.
AnswerThe requirement is met when the re-run shows the agreed clashes closed and the coordinated model is accepted in the CDE at the published status. The evidence is the published model with its clash report, not the software's claim that no conflicts remain.

Exam method

  • Learn the requirement chain as a flow; most questions ask who owns which document, not which software is used.
  • Describe CDE states in order and say what may change in each — shared means checked, not final.
  • Distinguish hard, soft and logical clashes with one concrete example each.
  • When asked for benefits, name the project stage and the information output, not just better collaboration.
  • If a question asks how the client de-risks BIM, name the chain: capability assessment at tender, pre-appointment BEP, contractual post-appointment BEP, then scheduled checks before each exchange.
