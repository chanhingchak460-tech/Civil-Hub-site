Construction turns drawings into a built asset through a temporary organisation of clients, designers, contractors and suppliers. This page follows a project from procurement through site work, safety, programme and cost control to handover.

01Projects, not products

Construction is organised project by project. Each site assembles a one-off product in the open air, using labour and plant that move on when the job ends.

The industry is fragmented: a few large contractors sit above many small specialist subcontractors and suppliers, and much of the work is done by small firms.

That structure explains the familiar risks:
- Design and construction are separated
- Decisions pass through many hands
- Weather and ground conditions interrupt the programme
- Margins are thin
- Much of the knowledge sits with people rather than with the product

02The project team

Every project has a client who pays and defines the need, a design team that turns the need into documents, and a contractor who builds.

On the design side:
- The architect arranges the spaces
- The civil and structural engineers make the ground and frame work
- Services engineers size the systems
- The quantity surveyor measures and prices the work

On site:
- The contractor's project manager runs the programme
- The commercial manager watches cost
- The site agent runs the day-to-day work
- A clerk of works or inspector checks that what is built matches the approved design
- The regulator grants consents to build and occupy

03Procurement: how the work is bought

Procurement decides who designs, who builds and who carries which risk.

  • Traditional (design–bid–build): the client's consultants finish the design and contractors tender on it; the design is settled before prices are committed, but change is slow and the relationship can turn adversarial.
  • Design–build: one contract for design and construction, shortening the programme and placing design risk with the builder, at the cost of less client control.
  • Construction management: brings a contractor in early to manage packages, which suits complex or fast projects but leaves the client carrying more cost risk.

Underneath all three sit the payment terms — lump sum, remeasurement or cost plus — each shifting risk.

04Temporary works

Temporary works are the structures that make the permanent work possible: formwork and falsework to shape and support wet concrete, scaffolding for access, shoring and props for excavations, crane bases, piling mats and edge protection. They carry real loads and fail like real structures.

The discipline is procedural as well as structural. A temporary works coordinator keeps a register, each scheme is designed and checked in proportion to its risk, and striking — removing the support — waits until the concrete has gained enough strength, judged from test results rather than the calendar. Load paths must run continuously from the new work down to the ground.

05Concrete practice: mix, place, cure

Concrete arrives ready-mixed or is batched on site, and a mix is defined by its proportions rather than its brand. The key ratio is the water–cement ratio: the mass of free water divided by the mass of cement. Strength falls as the ratio rises, because the surplus water leaves capillary voids behind; a workable mix and a strong mix pull in opposite directions, and admixtures exist largely to reconcile them.

Consistency is checked with a slump test, the classic site quality control. Placing follows with compaction to expel trapped air, and curing — keeping the surface moist and shaded for days — lets hydration continue. A member stripped early and left to dry never reaches the strength its cubes suggest.

Water–cement ratio
\[\frac{w}{c} = \frac{W_w}{W_c}\]
W_w free water mass, W_c cement mass; a higher ratio means lower strength
Concrete volume for a pour
\[V = L \times B \times D\]
length, breadth and depth of the element; add wastage when ordering

06Safety: hazard, risk and the hierarchy of controls

A hazard is something that can cause harm; risk combines how likely that harm is with how severe it would be. A simple risk score multiplies the two, so severity still counts when likelihood is low.

Controls are ranked:
- Elimination removes the hazard
- Substitution swaps it for something safer
- Engineering controls isolate people from it, through guard rails, excavation support or machine guarding
- Administrative controls change how people work, through method statements, permits and training
- Personal protective equipment is the last line

The common fatal risks are falls from height, lifting operations, excavation collapse, and being struck by plant.

Risk score
\[R = L \times S\]
L is likelihood, S is severity, on agreed scales

07Programme and productivity

A programme is a network of activities with durations and dependencies. The critical path is the longest chain through that network, so it sets the project duration; activities on it have no float, and any delay there delays completion.

Output is measured against input. Productivity — output per labour-hour — falls when work is congested, when design changes, when materials arrive late or when crews wait for access. Because labour is paid by time, a productivity loss becomes a cost overrun even when every rate is correct.

Labour productivity
\[P = \frac{\text{output}}{\text{labour-hours}}\]
e.g. cubic metres of concrete per labour-hour

08Plant, cycles and the site layout

Plant is chosen by what it must lift, reach and produce. A tower crane is sized by its radius and its load moment: the heaviest lift at the worst radius must sit inside the manufacturer's chart. Excavators and loaders are sized by cycle output — bucket volume, fill factor, cycle time and job efficiency — and the number of trucks must match the digger, or one side of the pair waits.

The site layout plans the movement before the plant arrives. Access roads, hoarding, storage areas, batching points, crane bases and welfare all compete for the same ground. Deliveries are scheduled against the programme because a site stores little, and a lorry that cannot be unloaded blocks the gate it came through.

Plant cycle output
\[Q = \frac{3600\, V F E}{T_c}\]
V bucket volume in m³, F fill factor, E job efficiency, T_c cycle time in seconds; Q in m³/h

09Digital construction: xR, printing and robotics

Site technology is moving from paper to sensors and machines. Extended reality overlays the design on the real site or builds a virtual mock-up, so clashes and sequences are rehearsed before they are built. 3D printing extrudes concrete into walls and columns with little waste and unusual geometries, though materials and scale still limit it. Robotics takes over cutting, drilling, welding and other repetitive or dangerous tasks, trading a high capital cost for speed, precision and fewer people in harm's way.

Each of these is judged with the same factors as any method: technical suitability, safety, cost, productivity, quality and design constraints. New is not automatically better; the test is whether the method outperforms the traditional trade on the factors that matter for the job.

Adoption test for a new method
technical fit + safety + cost + productivity + quality + design limits
score any candidate method, traditional or digital, against the same factors

10Cost control and earned value

Money is controlled by comparing what has been earned with what has been spent. In earned value terms, the budgeted cost of work performed is the value of progress achieved, the actual cost of work performed is what that progress cost, and the budgeted cost of work scheduled is what should have been done by now.

A negative cost variance means overspend and a negative schedule variance means the work is behind programme. The cost performance index turns the same figures into an efficiency: below 1, every unit of value costs more than a unit to earn.

Cost variance
\[\mathrm{CV} = \mathrm{BCWP} - \mathrm{ACWP}\]
negative means over budget
Schedule variance
\[\mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{BCWP} - \mathrm{BCWS}\]
negative means behind programme
Cost performance index
\[\mathrm{CPI} = \frac{\mathrm{BCWP}}{\mathrm{ACWP}}\]

11Duties, approvals and insurance

Construction is also an exercise in binding paperwork. Before work starts the site needs consents and notifications to utilities and authorities, and a chain of approvals for each stage: excavation, formwork erection, concrete pours, load tests and the like. Supervision and inspection duties attach to named people, not to the company in general.

Insurance and bonds allocate the losses when things go wrong: contract works insurance covers the work itself, third-party cover protects the public, and performance bonds back the contractor's promise. Every instruction, drawing revision and day's labour record exists partly so that, if a dispute arises, the paper trail tells the story.

Notice periods are not decoration Most contractual and statutory rights to claim time or money expire unless written notice is given within the stated period — record the event, the date and the effect on the same day it happens.

12Quality and handover

Quality is built in and then verified. An inspection and test plan names the checks, the acceptance criteria and who witnesses them, and hold points stop the next operation until a check is passed. Records matter because the finished work is often hidden: reinforcement, waterproofing and drainage are inspected before they disappear under concrete or fill.

At handover the works are commissioned, tested and snagged, as-built records and manuals are passed over, and a defects liability period covers failures that appear later. A project is not finished when the last pour is made; it is finished when the client can operate the asset.

Key terms

Temporary works
Structures and supports needed only during construction, such as formwork, falsework, scaffolding and excavation support.
Procurement
The process of choosing and contracting the organisations that will design and build the works.
Earned value
A cost-control method that compares the budgeted value of work done with its actual cost and planned schedule.
Critical path
The longest chain of dependent activities through a programme, which fixes the shortest possible project duration.
Method statement
A document setting out the sequence, resources and controls for a construction activity before work starts.
Snagging
The process of listing and correcting minor defects found during inspection before handover.
Float
The spare time available to an activity before it delays the critical path.
Water–cement ratio
The mass of water divided by the mass of cement in a mix; strength falls as the ratio rises.
Curing
Keeping fresh concrete moist and protected so that hydration continues and strength develops.
Worked example

Productivity and cost variance on a slab pour

A 240 m³ slab was planned to use 480 labour-hours at a fixed labour rate of 55 per hour (all costs in the same currency). It actually took 520 labour-hours at the same rate. Find the planned and actual productivity, the productivity change, and the labour cost variance.

  1. Planned productivity: P = 240 / 480 = 0.500 m³ per labour-hour.
  2. Actual productivity: P = 240 / 520 = 0.462 m³ per labour-hour.
  3. Change: 0.462 / 0.500 = 0.923, so output per hour fell by about 7.7%.
  4. Budgeted labour cost = 480 × 55 = 26 400; actual = 520 × 55 = 28 600. Cost variance CV = BCWP − ACWP = 26 400 − 28 600 = −2 200, an overspend of 2 200.
  5. Sanity check: 2 200 / 26 400 = 8.3% over budget, which matches the 8.3% excess hours exactly. The hourly rate did not change, so the cause is hours, not price.
AnswerActual productivity was 0.46 m³ per labour-hour, about 92% of plan, and the labour cost variance was −2 200 (8.3% over budget). Since the rate was unchanged, the overspend must be investigated as lost hours — rework, access delays or a slower placement method — rather than as a costing error.

Exam method

  • Match the procurement route to the risk question being asked: who designs, and who pays if the design is wrong?
  • When calculating cost variance, state the sign convention first; a negative CV usually means an overrun, not an underspend.
  • Name temporary works by their load path — what the support carries and what it bears on — rather than by name alone.
  • In safety answers, apply the hierarchy of controls in order and justify why a lower-ranked control was necessary.
  • Plant output questions: multiply volume × fill × efficiency × 3600 and divide by the cycle time — then check whether the trucks or the digger limit production.

Q01Under traditional design–bid–build procurement, who is responsible for the design?

WhyThe client appoints designers to complete the design before tender, so the contractor builds to documents it did not produce.

Q02Formwork and falsework used to support wet concrete are best described as:

WhyThey exist only to make the permanent work possible and are designed, checked, loaded and struck as temporary works.

Q03A risk assessment scores an activity as likelihood 3 and severity 5 on scales of 1 to 5. The risk score is:

WhyThe score is the product R = L × S = 3 × 5 = 15, which ranks high because even a moderate likelihood of a severe outcome matters.

Q04A project reports cost variance CV = BCWP − ACWP = −12 000. This means:

WhyEarned value is below actual cost, so the work done has cost more than it was budgeted to cost: a 12 000 overrun.

Q05A concrete mix is changed to use more water with the same cement content. The most likely effect on the hardened concrete is:

WhyRaising the water–cement ratio improves workability while wet but leaves more capillary voids, so hardened strength falls.

Q06An excavator bucket holds 1.2 m³, the fill factor is 0.9, job efficiency is 0.8 and the cycle takes 30 s. Its output is about:

WhyQ = 3600 × 1.2 × 0.9 × 0.8 / 30 = 104 m³/h; losing any one factor cuts output in the same proportion.
