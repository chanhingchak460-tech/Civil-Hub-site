How a construction project is planned, procured, programmed and controlled: work breakdown, critical path, cost and earned value, site records, BIM coordination and safety. The management layer that turns drawings into a delivered building.

01The project as a system

A construction project is a temporary organisation assembled to deliver a defined scope within time, cost and quality constraints. Scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources and stakeholders are interdependent: a change in one moves the others, and the cost of absorbing a change rises sharply as the project advances.

Integration means keeping those trade-offs visible and decided, not letting each discipline optimise alone. The client's brief is the reference point: a clear statement of what is required, what is excluded and how success will be measured prevents most later disputes.

02Work breakdown and programming

Programming starts with a work breakdown structure: the scope is divided into packages, each package becomes activities, and the activities are linked by logic and given durations and resources.

  • A network shows the logic; a Gantt chart shows the same programme against a calendar and communicates it better.
  • The programme is a forecast, not a record, so it is updated as work and information change.
  • Resource levelling flattens peaks that cannot be staffed.
  • Crashing adds resources to shorten the critical path, usually at extra cost.
  • Both change the risk profile and should never be done silently.

03Critical path and float

The critical path is the longest path through the network and therefore sets the project duration. A forward pass gives earliest start and finish; a backward pass gives latest start and finish.

  • Total float is the amount an activity can slip without delaying completion; activities with zero float are critical.
  • Float belongs to the project, not one trade. Using it early removes the buffer that could absorb a later delay, so float is not necessarily spare time.
  • Free float is the smaller amount that can be used without affecting the next activity's earliest start.
Earliest finish
\[\mathrm{EF} = \mathrm{ES} + \text{duration}\]
ES is the earliest start, from the forward pass
Total float
\[\mathrm{TF} = \mathrm{LS} - \mathrm{ES} = \mathrm{LF} - \mathrm{EF}\]
latest start and finish come from the backward pass

04Crashing and the cost–time trade

Crashing shortens an activity by spending money: more crews, more plant, longer shifts or a faster method. It only helps if the activity sits on the critical path; spending on an activity with float buys nothing.

The cost slope prices the trade — the extra cost per day saved — and the cheapest slopes are crashed first. Slopes rise as an activity is compressed, because the easy savings run out. Beyond a limit the activity cannot be shortened at all, and quality and safety deteriorate under rush. Crashing is therefore a ranked list of slopes, not a single decision, and the network must be recomputed after each step.

Cost slope
\[\mathrm{CS} = \frac{C_c - C_n}{t_n - t_c}\]
crash cost and time against normal cost and time; crash the smallest slope first

05Procurement and contracts

Procurement decides who designs, who builds and who carries which risk.

  • Traditional design-bid-build: the client holds the design.
  • Design and build: one organisation holds both design and build.
  • Management contracting: brings the builder in early and awards packages as design develops.
  • Each route trades cost certainty against speed, flexibility and the client's exposure to change.

The contract must match the route: lump sum, remeasurement or cost-plus, with clear provisions for variations, delay and payment. Most disputes trace back to a mismatch between route, contract and expectation.

06Contract administration: variations and claims

Contracts change after award. A variation is an instructed change to the work: an addition, a deletion, a different material or a changed sequence. The machinery matters as much as the change — who may instruct, how it is valued, and what happens if the parties disagree.

Claims arise where one party believes the other's act or a risk event has cost it time or money. The entitlement test is disciplined: was there a contractual ground, was notice given in time, and can the event be linked to the critical path? An extension of time relieves liquidated damages; it does not automatically carry money, which depends on who owned the risk.

Claim entitlement test
ground → notice → records → causation → quantum
each link must hold; a time-barred notice defeats an otherwise valid claim
Notice or nothing Most forms of contract require notice within days of the event; a valid entitlement argued months later is routinely rejected, so record the date, the cause and the effect immediately.

07Cost control and earned value

Cost control compares what has been spent with what has been earned. Earned value uses planned value (PV), the budget for scheduled work; earned value (EV), the budget for completed work; and actual cost (AC).

  • The cost performance index CPI = EV/AC and the schedule performance index SPI = EV/PV show whether the project is ahead or behind on each dimension.
  • A CPI below 1 means cost overrun per unit of work, and the estimate at completion EAC = BAC/CPI projects the final cost if the trend continues.
  • Cash flow is separate: a profitable project can still fail if payments lag behind expenditure.
Cost performance index
\[\mathrm{CPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{AC}}\]
below 1 means work costs more than budgeted
Schedule performance index
\[\mathrm{SPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{PV}}\]
below 1 means work is behind schedule
Estimate at completion
\[\mathrm{EAC} = \frac{\mathrm{BAC}}{\mathrm{CPI}}\]
assumes the current cost trend continues

08The S-curve and progress measurement

Progress is rarely linear: mobilisation starts slowly, production peaks, then finishes and commissioning tail off. Cumulative progress against time therefore plots as an S-curve, and the planned curve is the baseline against which actuals are drawn.

The vertical gap between planned and actual at a date shows schedule slippage in value terms, while the horizontal gap shows how many days behind the project has fallen. Percentage complete comes from measured quantities, not from money spent — spent does not mean done — and the curve is only as honest as the assessment behind each point.

Percentage complete
\[\mathrm{PC} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{BAC}}\]
earned value over budget at completion, expressed as a percentage
Time estimate at completion
\[\mathrm{TEAC} = \frac{T}{\mathrm{SPI}}\]
planned duration T divided by the schedule performance index

09Progress, records and quality on site

Site records are the project's memory: daily reports, diaries, photographs, measurement sheets, delivery notes and correspondence. They support payment, progress assessment, variation claims and, if necessary, dispute resolution. A record made on the day is evidence; one reconstructed later is an argument.

Quality is controlled by inspection and test plans, hold points and signed check sheets, with non-conformances recorded and closed out. Snagging before handover is cheaper than rectification after occupation, and as-built information closes the loop between design and reality.

10Stakeholders and communication

A project fails or succeeds as much through its stakeholders as through its concrete: the client, users, neighbours, authorities, utilities and the public all hold expectations that can become requirements or objections. Stakeholder management maps who they are, what they need, and who must be kept informed at each stage.

Communication runs on a cadence: daily site briefings, weekly progress meetings, monthly reports, and formal submissions for consents and payments. Requests for information, drawing transmittals and instructions are logged and closed out, because an unanswered question is a future delay claim with today's date on it.

11BIM coordination and digital delivery

A federated building information model lets the design team see structure, services and architecture together before construction. Clash detection finds physical conflicts, 4D sequencing links model objects to the programme, and 5D take-off links them to quantities and cost. Coordination meetings work through clash reports and record decisions against the model.

The model is only as good as its information. Clear naming, a common data environment, defined information requirements and version control prevent the digital equivalent of drawing over the wrong revision. BIM does not remove coordination; it makes coordination visible.

12Health, safety and risk

Safety is managed by design and by planning the work. The hierarchy of controls prefers eliminating the hazard, then substituting a safer method, then engineering controls, then administrative controls, with personal protective equipment last. Method statements and permits to work translate that hierarchy into site practice.

Risk management follows the same logic: identify, assess likelihood and consequence, treat, and monitor. Near-miss reporting is valuable because it exposes failures before they injure anyone. A safe site is usually a well-planned site; hurry and poor housekeeping are symptoms of programme pressure.

Key terms

Work breakdown structure
A hierarchical division of project scope into packages that can be planned, estimated and controlled.
Critical path
The longest path through the activity network; its length fixes the project duration and its activities have zero total float.
Total float
The time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project completion date.
Earned value
The budgeted value of work actually completed, used to compare progress and cost against the plan.
Cost performance index
Earned value divided by actual cost; a value below 1 indicates a cost overrun.
Clash detection
Automated checking of a federated model for physical conflicts between building elements or services.
Variation
An instructed change to the works after contract award, which must be valued under the contract machinery.
Extension of time
Additional time granted for excusable delay, relieving liquidated damages without automatically granting money.
Worked example

Critical path and float for a small fit-out

A fit-out has five activities: A site set-up (3 days, no predecessor); B partitions (4 days, after A); C first-fix services (2 days, after A); D finishes (5 days, after B and C); E handover and snagging (1 day, after D). Find the project duration, the critical path and the total float of activity C.

  1. Forward pass: A runs 0–3; B runs 3–7; C runs 3–5; D can start only when both B and C are finished, so it starts at day 7 and ends at day 12; E runs 12–13.
  2. Backward pass: E has latest start 12; D has latest start 7; B and C must both finish by day 7, giving B latest start 3 and C latest start 5; A has latest start 0.
  3. Total float from TF = LS − ES: A and B have zero, C has 5 − 3 = 2 days, D and E have zero.
  4. The critical path is the chain of zero-float activities: A → B → D → E, with duration 3 + 4 + 5 + 1 = 13 days.
  5. Activity C may slip by up to 2 days without delaying handover, but only while B stays on the critical path.
AnswerProject duration is 13 days, the critical path is A → B → D → E, and activity C has 2 days of total float.

Exam method

  • Draw the network before the Gantt chart; float is a consequence of the logic, not of the bar chart.
  • State whether an index or variance is favourable or adverse, and show the numbers you used.
  • In procurement questions, answer with risk allocation and cost certainty, not just the names of routes.
  • Keep earned value definitions exact: EV is the budget for completed work, not the money spent on it.
  • Crash the cheapest slope on the critical path first, and recompute the network after each step — crashing can move the critical path.
