Concrete and steel as engineering materials: how cement hydrates, how mixes are proportioned and tested, and how steel behaves in tension. It links laboratory measurements to the durability decisions made on site.

01Cement is a chemical system

Portland cement is made by burning limestone and clay to a clinker, then grinding it with a little gypsum. The clinker contains four main phases:
- Alite gives most of the early strength.
- Belite contributes slowly over months.
- Aluminate sets fastest and releases the most heat.
- Ferrite adds little.

Gypsum is the set regulator, keeping the aluminate from stiffening the paste within minutes. When water is added, the silicates hydrate into calcium silicate hydrate (the gel that glues everything together) plus calcium hydroxide. Strength is not a property of the powder; it is the product of a reaction that needs water, time and a controlled temperature.

02Water, setting and w/c ratio

The water/cement ratio is the mass of water divided by the mass of cement, and it controls almost everything: workability, strength, permeability and shrinkage. Only about 0.23 times the cement mass is chemically bound at full hydration, but extra water is needed for workability, so practical mixes sit near 0.40–0.55. More water means more capillary space, and strength falls as w/c rises.

Setting and hardening are different: setting is the loss of plasticity, hardening is the gain of strength. The Vicat apparatus finds the water content for standard consistency, then the initial and final setting times. Accelerators shorten setting, retarders lengthen it, and calcium chloride accelerates but risks reinforcement corrosion.

Water/cement ratio
\[\frac{w}{c} = \frac{\text{mass of water}}{\text{mass of cement}}\]

03Aggregates and mix proportioning

Aggregate fills roughly 60–75% of the volume and provides the skeleton of concrete. Grading matters: a continuous range of sizes fills voids, reduces the paste needed and improves workability, while single-sized aggregate needs much more paste. Angular, rough particles interlock but demand more water; weak or absorptive particles limit strength and durability.

Mix design works backwards from a target:
- Choose a target mean strength above the specified characteristic value to allow for site variability.
- Pick the w/c ratio that delivers it.
- Fix the water content for the required slump.
- Proportion cement and aggregates.

Fly ash and slag replace part of the cement, lowering hydration heat and improving long-term performance.

Target mean strength
\[f_m = f_{ck} + 1.64\,s\]
s is the standard deviation of cube results; 1.64 gives roughly a 5% failure rate

04Workability, compaction and curing

Workability describes how easily a mix can be placed and compacted without segregation. The slump test is the field check: a cone is filled in three layers, lifted, and the drop of the concrete is measured. A true slump is acceptable; a shear or collapse means the mix is unsuitable and must be repeated. Slump describes consistency, not strength.

Compaction removes entrapped air — typically 2–5% of the volume if neglected, costing a similar share of strength. Curing keeps water available so hydration continues: about seven days of moist curing is normal, longer for hot, marine or low-w/c work. Poor curing leaves a weak, permeable surface layer, which is where durability problems begin.

05Steel: composition and tensile behaviour

Structural steel is an iron–carbon alloy. Carbon raises strength and hardness but reduces ductility and weldability, so structural grades keep it low and rely on manganese and small alloy additions. Hot-rolled sections, plates, cold-formed sheet, reinforcement and fasteners are all steel products with different forming histories and properties.

The tensile test defines the material: a linear region gives E ≈ 200 GPa, then a yield point, a plateau, strain hardening up to the ultimate strength, necking and fracture. Yield strength is the design property, the ultimate strength an upper bound, and the elongation at fracture describes ductility. Hardness tests give a quick correlation with tensile strength.

Engineering stress and strain
\[\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0}, \quad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\]
A₀ and L₀ are the original area and gauge length
Percentage elongation
\[\text{elongation} = \frac{L_u - L_0}{L_0} \times 100\%\]

06Durability and corrosion

Concrete is durable because its pore water is strongly alkaline, which passivates the reinforcement. That protection is lost in two ways:
- Carbonation: carbon dioxide neutralises the calcium hydroxide and the pH falls towards 9.
- Chloride ingress: breaks the passive film locally and causes pitting.

Both are transport processes, so depth grows roughly with the square root of time; dense, low-w/c concrete and adequate cover are the cheapest protection. Steel corrodes when water and oxygen reach it, and salt accelerates the process. Galvanising, coatings, stainless steel and cathodic protection all interrupt that circuit. Fire is a separate durability issue: unprotected steel loses much of its strength above about 500 °C.

Carbonation depth
\[x = k \sqrt{t}\]
k depends on the mix and exposure; x grows with the square root of time t

07Absolute volumes: designing the mix from first principles

Mix proportioning works in volumes, not masses: one cubic metre of concrete must contain one cubic metre of ingredients plus trapped air. With cement and water particle densities near 3.1 and 1.0 Mg/m³, the absolute volume method fixes the water content for the target slump and the w/c ratio for the target strength, then fills the remaining volume with combined aggregate, split between fine and coarse by the grading curve.

Aggregate moisture corrupts the arithmetic unless corrected: a wet stockpile carries surface water into the mix, and an under-absorbed one steals mixing water. Correct to the saturated surface-dry state so that batch water is the true free water.

Absolute volume balance
\[\frac{W_c}{\rho_c} + \frac{W_w}{\rho_w} + V_{agg} + V_{air} = 1\ \mathrm{m}^3\]
per cubic metre of compacted concrete
Aggregate absorption
\[\mathrm{abs} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{SSD}} - M_{\mathrm{dry}}}{M_{\mathrm{dry}}} \times 100\%\]
mass gain when oven-dry aggregate reaches the saturated surface-dry state
Fresh density
\[\rho = \frac{m}{V}\]
mass of a known volume of compacted fresh concrete

08Fresh-state checks: consistency, density and set

The Vicat test defines standard consistency as the water content at which the plunger stops so that its underside lies 6 ± 2 mm above the base plate, read about four minutes after mixing. The needle test on the same apparatus finds the initial and final set; in one laboratory run with a retarder these came at about 100 and 130 minutes.

On site the slump cone is filled in three equal layers, each rodded, and the drop is read to the nearest 10 mm; a true slump near 80 mm was typical of the laboratory mix. A sheared or collapsed result is not a slump — the test is repeated. Wet density, the mass of a known volume of fresh concrete, is the delivery check that the batching plant sent the mix ordered.

Standard consistency in one line Vicat plunger underside 6 ± 2 mm above the base plate: the paste is at standard consistency, and the water content that achieves it is recorded to the nearest 0.5%.

09Steel in service: hardness, fatigue and fire

Hardness is resistance to local plastic deformation, and the Rockwell test measures it as the permanent depth of an indenter under a minor then major load. Laboratory readings on the HRA scale placed mild steel at about 44, brass at 46, aluminium at 46 and high-tensile steel at 62 — hardness ranks with strength.

Under repeated loading steel fatigues: the S–N approach plots stress amplitude against cycles to failure, and design keeps stress ranges below the endurance line, with welded details far more vulnerable than plain material. In fire, unprotected steel loses much of its strength by about 500 °C, so sections are boxed, sprayed or intumescent-coated to buy escape time.

S–N (Basquin) relation
\[\sigma_a^m N_f = C\]
σ_a stress amplitude, N_f cycles to failure; log-linear on an S–N plot

Key terms

Hydration
The chemical reaction between cement compounds and water that forms C-S-H gel and calcium hydroxide, setting and hardening the paste.
Water/cement ratio
The mass of water divided by the mass of cement; the single strongest influence on strength, permeability and shrinkage.
Workability
How easily a fresh mix can be placed, compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding.
Curing
Keeping concrete moist and at a suitable temperature after casting so hydration can continue and the surface layer gains strength.
Characteristic strength
The strength below which no more than a small defined proportion of test results is expected to fall.
Carbonation
The reaction of carbon dioxide with calcium hydroxide in concrete, lowering the pH and removing the protection from reinforcement.
Ductility
The ability of a material to deform plastically before fracture, giving warning of failure and allowing force redistribution.
Passivation
The thin oxide film that protects steel in the alkaline environment of good concrete, lost when carbonation or chlorides arrive.
Standard consistency
The water content at which the Vicat plunger settles to 6 ± 2 mm above the base plate, defining a paste of reproducible stiffness.
Saturated surface-dry
The aggregate moisture state in which the pores are full but the surface is dry; the reference state for mix proportioning.
Worked example

Judging a batch from cube and cylinder results

Three 150 mm cubes from a batch failed at 780 kN, 810 kN and 762 kN. A 150 mm diameter by 300 mm cylinder split at 240 kN. The mix used 380 kg of cement and 190 kg of water per cubic metre, and the specification requires characteristic strength f_ck = 30 MPa with a maximum w/c of 0.45 for the exposure class. Assess the batch.

  1. Cube cross-section: A = 150 × 150 = 22 500 mm². Strengths: 780 000/22 500 = 34.7 MPa, 810 000/22 500 = 36.0 MPa, 762 000/22 500 = 33.9 MPa.
  2. Mean strength = (34.7 + 36.0 + 33.9)/3 = 34.9 MPa. Sample standard deviation s = √[(0.04 + 1.21 + 1.00)/2] = √1.125 = 1.06 MPa.
  3. Characteristic value = mean − 1.64 s = 34.9 − 1.74 = 33.2 MPa, which exceeds the 30 MPa specified, so the strength criterion is met.
  4. Splitting tensile strength: f_ct = 2F/(πDL) = 2 × 240 000/(π × 150 × 300) = 480 000/141 372 = 3.4 MPa.
  5. Ratio f_ct/f_ck = 3.4/33.2 = 0.10, i.e. about 10% of the compressive strength, which is inside the usual 8–12% range and confirms a normal concrete.
  6. Durability check on the mix: w/c = 190/380 = 0.50, against a limit of 0.45. The strength passes but the mix fails the exposure requirement.
  7. Sanity check: a 0.50 w/c with 380 kg/m³ of cement is a workable but porous mix; it should be revised with a plasticiser or extra cement rather than extra water.
AnswerMean cube strength is 34.9 MPa and the estimated characteristic strength is 33.2 MPa, so the batch satisfies the 30 MPa requirement, and the tensile ratio of 0.10 is normal. However w/c = 0.50 exceeds the 0.45 durability limit, so the mix must be redesigned before it is accepted.

Exam method

  • Quote the units and the test age whenever you state a concrete strength; 35 MPa at 28 days is a different claim from 35 MPa at 7 days.
  • For any durability question, start from permeability and cover, then name the transport mechanism (carbonation, chloride diffusion, absorption).
  • In mix design, show the target mean strength and the margin before proportions; examiners look for the 1.64 s allowance.
  • When comparing cube and cylinder results, mention platen restraint and quote a conversion factor rather than treating them as equal.
  • Whenever an aggregate's moisture state is given (oven-dry, air-dry, SSD), adjust the batch water before quoting w/c — the absorption correction is worth several kilograms of water per batch.

Q01Which clinker compound is mainly responsible for the early strength of Portland cement?

WhyAlite, the tricalcium silicate phase, hydrates quickly and gives most of the strength in the first few weeks; belite contributes slowly over months.

Q02Why is gypsum added to cement clinker during grinding?

WhyWithout gypsum the aluminate would react almost immediately and cause flash set; gypsum controls the rate of setting.

Q03A concrete cube and a cylinder of the same concrete are tested at the same age. The cube result is higher because:

WhyFriction at the loading platens confines the cube, raising its apparent strength; the cylinder is slender enough for that restraint to matter less.

Q04Carbonation of concrete matters for durability mainly because it:

WhyCarbon dioxide converts calcium hydroxide to calcium carbonate, dropping the pH towards 9, at which the steel is no longer passivated.

Q05A wet aggregate stockpile is batched by mass without any moisture correction. Compared with the intended mix, the concrete will most likely:

WhySurface water on the aggregate enters the mix as extra free water, raising the effective w/c ratio and reducing strength and durability.

Q06Which reading best matches the definition of standard consistency of cement paste?

WhyStandard consistency is defined by the Vicat plunger settling to within 6 ± 2 mm of the base plate about four minutes after mixing.
