Junctions and rail lines are sized by the capacity they must deliver, not by the space they occupy. This page covers priority junctions, roundabouts, signal capacity, railway headways and the standards that hold them together.

01Design is a capacity question

A transport facility is designed for a design-year demand expressed in passenger car units per hour rather than raw vehicles, because a heavy lorry occupies more road space than a car. The key ratio is the degree of saturation, demand divided by capacity; the key outputs are delay and queue length.

Capacity alone does not choose a junction type. Safety, land, pedestrians, cost and the surrounding network all constrain the answer, which is why standards offer layouts rather than one solution.

02Priority junctions: gap acceptance

At a give-way junction the minor arm must find a gap in the major flow. Its capacity therefore falls as the conflicting flow rises, and the shape of that fall follows gap acceptance theory: a driver needs a critical gap before emerging, and successive vehicles follow at the follow-up time.

The practical conclusion is that the minor approach is the bottleneck, and its capacity is sensitive to major-road volume, visibility and turning proportions. An extra minor lane or a flare helps only when lanes, not gaps, are the constraint.

Minor-arm capacity (gap acceptance)
\[C = \frac{q e^{-q t_c/3600}}{1 - e^{-q t_f/3600}}\]
q is the conflicting major flow in pcu/h, t_c the critical gap and t_f the follow-up time in seconds

03Roundabouts: circulating flow sets entry capacity

A roundabout entry gives way to the circulating flow, so entry capacity is a function of the traffic already on the ring. Empirical models of the linear form below are fitted to observed behaviour; the coefficients depend on the entry geometry rather than being universal constants.

Geometry matters because it controls speed and gap acceptance. Entry deflection, entry width, flare length and island radius all change how quickly vehicles can enter. A mini-roundabout simplifies the crossing points, but because its island is traversable and lanes are shared it can become overloaded quickly.

Roundabout entry capacity
\[C_{\mathrm{entry}} = a - b\,q_c\]
q_c is the circulating flow in pcu/h; a and b are fitted to the entry geometry

04Signals: saturation flow and green ratio

A signal approach discharges at its saturation flow while the green is fully used — roughly 1,800 to 2,000 pcu/h per lane under ideal conditions. Over a cycle of length C with effective green g, capacity is saturation flow times the green ratio.

The flow ratio y = q/s measures how much saturation flow the demand needs, and the degree of saturation X = q/c compares demand with capacity. Lost time — start-up and clearance in every stage — is why a short cycle wastes a larger share of green, and why Webster's formula trades that waste against the delay of a long cycle.

Approach capacity
\[c = s \left( \frac{g}{C} \right)\]
s in pcu/h, g effective green and C cycle length in the same time units
Webster optimum cycle
\[C_o = \frac{1.5L + 5}{1 - Y}\]
L is total lost time per cycle and Y the sum of critical flow ratios

05Measuring saturation flow in the field

Saturation flow is best measured, not assumed. The field method counts departing vehicles during saturated cycles — green periods that end with a queue still present — and needs at least about five queued vehicles to be reliable. The first few vehicles are excluded because they are still overcoming start-up lost time, so only the steady headway portion counts:

s = 3600 n / t_g,

where n is the number of vehicles counted over the saturated portion of green of length t_g.

  • Gradient, turning radius, lane width and the vehicle mix each justify a correction factor.
  • The measurement must average enough cycles on a representative lane of the approach — a straight-ahead or mixed lane, not an exclusive turning lane — because one congested minute is not a saturation flow.
  • Where the junction justifies it, direct measurement beats the empirical adjustment equations, which is why the manual prescribes it for detailed signal plans.
Field saturation flow
\[s = \frac{3600\,n}{t_g}\]
n is the vehicle count over the saturated part of green, t_g in seconds; exclude the first few vehicles of each queue

06Pedestrians and cyclists are design users

Every junction is also a pedestrian crossing. Corner radii set by vehicle swept paths can lengthen the crossing, pushing people to cross where they can rather than where they are meant to. Tight radii, refuge islands, level crossings and sensible staging keep the crossing short and visible.

Cyclists need continuous, predictable space. A cycle lane that stops at the junction, or sits where turning vehicles must cross it, recreates the conflict it was meant to remove. Design standards address this through lane continuity, visibility envelopes and signal staging.

Signal timing check for a crossing
\[t_{\mathrm{needed}} = \frac{L}{v} + t_s\]
walking speed is often taken as about 1.2 m/s for design

07Rail capacity is a headway calculation

On a railway, capacity is the number of trains passing a point in an hour, so everything reduces to the minimum headway. Signalling defines a blocking time for each train, and a station adds dwell time plus the time to accelerate away and clear the platform.

A first-order station headway is dwell plus acceleration time plus braking time plus the time for train and clearance to pass, with a signalling margin. Trains per hour then follow from that headway.

Minimum station headway
\[h_{\min} \approx t_{\mathrm{dwell}} + \frac{v}{a} + \frac{v}{b} + \frac{L_{\mathrm{train}} + L_{\mathrm{clear}}}{v} + t_{\mathrm{margin}}\]
v in m/s, accelerations in m/s², lengths in m, giving h in seconds
Line capacity from headway
\[C = \frac{3600}{h} c_{\mathrm{train}}\]
c_train is the crush capacity of one train in passengers

08Rail capacity in numbers: offered and utilised

Rail capacity arithmetic runs from the vehicle up. A vehicle's capacity combines seats and standing room:

C_vehicle = S_seats + A_stand × ρ,

where ρ is the standing density, about 4 passengers per square metre in normal planning. A six-car train of 150-seat cars each offering 45 m² of standing space carries about 330 per car and 1,980 per train.

The offered line capacity is C_offered = c_train × 3600 / h; the utilised capacity applies a design load factor of about 1.1 to reflect real crowding: C_utilised = C_offered × F_load. A 135 s peak headway then offers about 52,800 passengers per hour.

Minimum headways set the ceiling: the station headway (dwell plus clearing time, times a safety factor) usually binds, but the way headway from braking must be checked too:

h_way ≈ [v/a + (L_train + L_clear)/v] × f_safety. Whichever headway is larger governs the timetable, and the line capacity follows from it — so a 5 s dwell saving is a real capacity gain.

Vehicle capacity
\[C_{\mathrm{vehicle}} = S_{\mathrm{seats}} + A_{\mathrm{stand}}\,\rho\]
ρ is the standing density, about 4 pax/m² in planning
Utilised line capacity
\[C_{\mathrm{utilised}} = C_{\mathrm{offered}}\,F_{\mathrm{load}}\]
F_load is the design load factor, about 1.1
Minimum way headway
\[h_{\mathrm{way}} \approx \left[\frac{v}{a} + \frac{L_{\mathrm{train}} + L_{\mathrm{clear}}}{v}\right] f_{\mathrm{safety}}\]
braking time plus the time for train and clearance to pass, times the safety factor

09Stations and interchanges

A station is a capacity device. Platform width, stairs, escalators and gates each have a flow rate, and the slowest link sets the transfer time. A simple two-platform station tolerates long dwells; a busy interchange needs wider platforms, more vertical circulation routes and level access.

Interchange design trades walking distance against congestion. A long, well-signed walk can beat a short, crowded one, and cross-platform interchange is the strongest connection because it removes a level change entirely. Wayfinding and accessibility must be designed in, not added at the end.

10Traffic impact assessment: scope and steps

A traffic impact assessment (TIA) asks what a proposed development does to the surrounding transport network, and it follows a fixed scope.

  • Set the study area and horizon years — usually the opening year and a design year.
  • Estimate trip generation and attraction from the land use.
  • Assign the trips to the network by mode and route.
  • Test the junctions, crossings and access points the development will affect.
  • Judge junction performance by the degree of saturation: approaches should stay below about 85–90% so queues can recover.
  • Cover access geometry, service vehicles, drop-off and the effect on buses and pedestrians.
  • Where the network fails, design and re-test mitigation — staging, lane changes, access management — and report residual impacts for the authority to weigh.

A TIA is only as good as its trip rates: rates borrowed from another city, or a modal split assumed rather than surveyed, produce confident-looking numbers that mean little.

Trip rates decide everything A TIA built on borrowed trip rates and an unverified modal split produces precise outputs from soft inputs; state the source of every rate before defending the conclusion.

11Standards, models and judgement

Design guides give geometry, capacity equations and safety criteria. Traffic models add what equations cannot: time-dependent demand, queues spreading between junctions, and the effect of one overloaded approach on the whole network.

Empirical methods are quick but assume uniform conditions; simulation captures interaction but needs calibration. The engineer reconciles the two, states assumptions and validates the model against observed flows. Software output is evidence, not a decision: the layout must still be safe, buildable and acceptable to the people who will use it.

Key terms

Degree of saturation
The ratio of demand to capacity at an approach; values near 1 mean queues grow readily.
Saturation flow
The discharge rate of a lane group while its green is fully used, typically about 1,800 pcu/h per lane.
Critical gap
The smallest gap in conflicting traffic that a driver will accept before entering the junction.
Circulating flow
The traffic already travelling around a roundabout ring, which the entering stream must give way to.
Blocking time
The time a section of track is reserved for one train, including approach and clearance, which sets the signalling headway.
Dwell time
The time a train stands at a platform for passengers to board and alight.
Start-up lost time
The extra seconds the first vehicles of a queue take to cross the stop line, excluded when measuring saturation flow.
Design load factor
The factor converting nominal vehicle capacity into the loading assumed in capacity planning, accounting for crowding.
Worked example

Line capacity of a metro with a fixed headway

A metro line runs six-car trains, each car 20 m long. Dwell time is 30 s, line speed is 45 km/h, acceleration and service braking are both about 1.0 m/s², the train plus safety clearance is 145 m, and the signalling and performance margin is 10 s. Calculate the minimum station headway, the number of trains per hour and the line capacity if each train carries 1,200 passengers.

  1. Convert the line speed: 45 km/h = 12.5 m/s.
  2. Acceleration and braking each take v/a = 12.5 / 1.0 = 12.5 s.
  3. The train and clearance take (145 m) / (12.5 m/s) = 11.6 s to pass a point.
  4. Minimum headway = 30 + 12.5 + 12.5 + 11.6 + 10 = 76.6 s, rounded up to 77 s.
  5. Trains per hour = 3600 / 77 = 46.8, so a whole number of 46 trains per hour is the achievable service.
  6. Line capacity = 46 × 1,200 = 55,200 passengers per hour per direction.
  7. Sanity check: a demand of 40,000 passengers per hour would use 40,000 / 55,200 = 72 per cent of the line capacity, leaving useful room for recovery.
AnswerThe minimum station headway is about 77 s, giving 46 trains per hour and a line capacity of 55,200 passengers per hour per direction. Dwell and the stop-start cycle dominate the headway, so cutting dwell by 5 s would lift capacity by roughly 3 trains per hour.

Exam method

  • Convert demand to pcu/h and state the degree of saturation before comparing junction options.
  • For signal capacity, multiply saturation flow by the green ratio; adding green to one stage necessarily takes it from another.
  • For rail questions, work term by term in seconds and keep the units of v, a and lengths consistent.
  • When a model and a manual calculation disagree, explain the assumption behind each before choosing which to trust.
  • In a saturation flow question, exclude the start-up vehicles before dividing the count by the saturated green time; including them understates s.
