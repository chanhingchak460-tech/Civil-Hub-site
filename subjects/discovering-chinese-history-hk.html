Hong Kong's past before and beyond the colonial story: migration and lineage society, the fishing and salt economy, quarry labour, wartime rupture and post-war industry. It is also a lesson in how historians read inscriptions, genealogies and landscapes.

01Read the place, not just the treaty

Hong Kong's history is often dated from 1842, but that is a colonial frame. For centuries the area belonged to the administrative and cultural world of the Pearl River Delta, passing through counties such as 番禺 Panyu, 東莞 Dongguan and finally 新安 Xin'an.

Reading the past from the Chinese side means starting with migration, lineage society, coastal trade and the sea, then treating the colonial period as one chapter in a longer story.

Source criticism
Provenance → Purpose → Perspective → Corroboration
a document is evidence of itself as well as of its subject

02Migration and the great clans

Most settled communities in the New Territories trace ancestry back to migration during the Song and Yuan periods. Five lineages dominate the written record — the 鄧 Tang, 侯 Hau, 廖 Liu, 文 Man and 彭 Pang, usually called the 新界五大族.

A lineage was more than a family:
- ancestral halls
- 族譜 genealogies
- communal land held in trusts
- 圍村 walled villages

These let a clan organise labour, defend resources and assert status. Genealogies are useful and partial: they record what a lineage wished remembered.

03Fishing, salt and a maritime economy

Before industry, the sea paid the bills. Salt evaporation fields on the western shore, notably around 大澳 Tai O, produced revenue for the imperial state, while fishing communities — including boat-dwelling groups known as 蛋家 Tanka — worked coastal waters and markets.

屯門 Tuen Mun was already a recognised anchorage and trading stop in the Tang period, where foreign ships waited for the monsoon. 天后 Tin Hau temples mark this world; their inscriptions often list the donors and land given to maintain them.

04Granite, quarries and stonecutters

Granite underlies much of the territory, and quarrying grew into an export trade. 石匠 stonecutters worked sites from 鯉魚門 Lei Yue Mun to 鑽石山 Diamond Hill, cutting blocks for local building and for ports further afield.

Because stone is heavy, the trade shaped roads, piers and settlement around the quarries. Quarry history links geology, labour and shipping, and it shows that the landscape itself can be a historical source.

05Colonial development, occupation and industry

The colonial period reshaped the coast quickly:
- the cession of Hong Kong Island in 1841–42
- the addition of Kowloon
- the 1898 lease of the New Territories

These brought surveys, roads, railways, reservoirs and a new legal order. The Japanese occupation of 1941–45 broke that system and left the population reduced and hungry.

After 1945 the economy moved from entrepôt trade to manufacturing and then services, with public housing, new towns and container ports following.

06Reading the evidence

History is argued from evidence, and Hong Kong has many kinds: archaeological finds, temple and 碑銘 stone inscriptions, genealogies, land deeds, colonial maps and archives, newspapers and oral testimony.

Each answers some questions and is silent on others — an inscription may list donors but not the worshippers who could not read. Interpretation adds another layer, because a restored building is also a choice about which past to present. Separate what a source says from what the historian infers.

07Before the clans: tombs and the Song court

Archaeology reaches where texts are silent. The Lei Cheng Uk tomb (李鄭屋漢墓), an Eastern Han brick tomb uncovered in the 1950s, carries bricks stamped 大吉番禺 — "great fortune, Panyu" — evidence that the area was administered within Panyu county some 1,800 years before the colonial treaty. It is a reminder that the region's written history has a deep, buried prelude.

Legend supplies another layer: the Sung Wong Toi 宋皇臺 rock is tied to the story of the boy emperors fleeing south before the Mongol conquest in the 1270s, an episode that pulled the region briefly into dynastic politics and seeded the migration stories of the great clans.

Three-way check
Archaeology + Text + Memory
each kind of evidence answers a different question; use them together

08Five layers of source analysis

A methodical reading of any document moves through five layers:
- establish the provenance and genre (who made it, when, for what use)
- read the text closely, glossing terms and tracing names
- weigh its significance for the themes it touches
- place it in historiography — how scholars have used or contested it
- note what further research it invites

Applied to the Tap Mun inscription of 1743, the layers separate what the stone says (two families donated tax fields to the Tin Hau temple, and land-grabbers lost a magistrate's ruling) from what it does not (the tenants' lives, the ordinary worshippers, how typical such gifts were). The framework turns a quote into an argument.

The five layers applied to the 1743 Tap Mun inscription

09Temples, ritual and the local state

Temples did more than house gods. The Tin Hau cult 天后信仰 spread with the maritime economy, and temple property — donated fields whose rent paid for festivals — made a temple a corporate landowner, a mediator of disputes and a point of contact with officials.

The 1743 Tap Mun case shows the loop: a donation, an encroachment, a magistrate's ruling, and a stone erected to make the ruling permanent.

Ritual tied communities together across villages: the 打醮 jiao festivals renew a community's protection through processions, offerings and opera, and their records double as historical sources — donor lists, land registers, the names of villages that attended. Reading a festival as an institution recovers the social map that maps alone cannot show.

Sacred property loop
Donation → 廟產 temple estate → Dispute → Official ruling → Inscription
each stage leaves a different kind of source

10Gazetteers, deeds and oral memory

Local histories 地方志 — county gazetteers such as the Xin'an gazetteer — record administration, taxation, customs and notable sites through the eyes of officials, so they emphasise what the state wanted governed.

Land deeds and contracts record the working world of boundaries, rents and succession; genealogies record what lineages chose to remember; oral history records what elders chose to pass on.

No source is neutral, which is why corroboration is the discipline's core habit: an inscription checked against a gazetteer, a genealogy against a deed. Where sources conflict, the conflict itself is evidence — of rival claims, of memory at work, of what each writer needed the record to say.

Corroboration web
Stone ⇄ Gazetteer ⇄ Deed ⇄ Oral memory
cross-check across genres; disagreement is itself evidence

Key terms

族譜 (genealogy)
A lineage record of descent, marriages and honours, written to establish status as well as to inform.
圍村 (walled village)
A settlement enclosed by walls, and often a moat, built by clans for defence in the coastal New Territories.
碑銘 (inscription)
Text carved on stone, usually recording a donation, a temple's founding or an official decision.
Entrepôt trade
Trade that passes through a port without the goods being produced or consumed there.
Corroboration
Checking one source against another independent one before accepting a claim.
Heritage interpretation
The choices made when presenting a place or object to the public, including what to explain and what to leave out.
地方志 (county gazetteer)
A local history compiled for administration, recording taxation, customs and notable sites from an official viewpoint.
打醮 (jiao festival)
A periodic community ritual of renewal, with processions, offerings and opera, whose records double as historical sources.
Worked example

Reading a temple inscription as evidence

An inscription carved in the mid-eighteenth century records that two named families gave tax fields and hill land to a temple, that other men later claimed the land, and that a magistrate confirmed the gift. What historical claims can this source reasonably support, and what can it not?

  1. Identify the genre and provenance: a stone inscription set up at a temple, paid for by the donors, so it is a public and partly self-serving record.
  2. State its purpose: to make the gift permanent and to warn future claimants that the property is protected by an official ruling.
  3. Extract the claims it does make: land was donated, a dispute occurred, and a magistrate ruled in the donors' favour at a stated date.
  4. Note its silences: it says nothing about the tenants who farmed the land, the size of the congregation, or how typical such gifts were.
  5. Corroborate with a land deed, a county gazetteer or a second inscription before generalising beyond this one temple.
AnswerThe inscription is strong evidence for temple property, donor rivalry and the use of official rulings to defend land. It is weak evidence for general prosperity or for the lives of people who could not afford to donate, and any wider claim needs independent corroboration.

Exam method

  • For source questions, establish provenance and purpose before extracting content; the method carries as many marks as the facts.
  • Give Chinese terms with a short English gloss, then argue in English so the reader can follow the reasoning.
  • Balance the colonial narrative with evidence from before 1841 and after 1997 so the answer is not one-sided.
  • Quote one phrase from the source and gloss it — provenance plus a glossed quotation carries the method marks.
