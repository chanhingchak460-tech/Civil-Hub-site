How rocks, structures and weathering shape the ground a project sits on, and how site investigation turns observations into a design model. It is the link between a geological map and a safe excavation.

01The ground records its history

A site is the end product of deposition, burial, deformation and weathering over geological time. Two boreholes a few metres apart can meet completely different conditions, because a fault, a buried channel or a weathering front can pass between them.

The task in engineering geology is to build a ground model — a three-dimensional interpretation of what is there — and then to state how confident that model is. That model decides foundation type, excavation method, slope angle, dewatering and the risk allowance in the price. Most surprises on site are geological, and most of them were predictable from a map, a core log or a photograph.

Vertical and effective stress
\[\sigma_v = \gamma z, \quad \sigma' = \sigma_v - u\]
γ is the unit weight, z the depth and u the pore water pressure

02Minerals: the working vocabulary

A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic solid with a defined composition and an ordered atomic structure. Rock-forming minerals are mostly silicates — quartz, feldspars, micas, amphiboles and pyroxenes — with calcite, dolomite, halite and clay minerals making up most of the rest.

Identification uses hardness on the Mohs scale, cleavage, lustre, colour and streak, but for engineering the useful properties are hardness, solubility and the tendency of clays to swell. Quartz is hard and inert; calcite is soft and dissolves in acidic water; mica splits into flakes and weakens a rock along one plane.

03Igneous rocks

Igneous rocks crystallise from magma or lava. Slow cooling at depth produces coarse, interlocking grains — granite, diorite, gabbro — while rapid cooling at the surface produces fine-grained basalt or rhyolite, and explosive eruptions produce tuff and volcanic breccia.

Intact igneous rock is usually strong and abrasive, which makes it good foundation material and hard excavation. Its weakness is the discontinuity pattern: cooling joints, flow contacts and later faults divide the mass into blocks. In humid climates granite weathers along joints into grus and then saprolite, so strong rock can sit above a soil-like profile only a few metres down.

04Sedimentary rocks

Sedimentary rocks form from deposited material: clastic types such as sandstone, siltstone and mudstone, chemical and biological types such as limestone and chalk, and evaporites such as gypsum and halite. Their defining feature is bedding, and bedding planes are the surfaces along which the rock most readily separates, slides and leaks.

Sandstone behaviour depends on its cement: silica-cemented sandstone is strong, clay-cemented sandstone is not. Mudstone is weak, slakes on exposure and loses strength when wetted. Limestone is strong but soluble, so acidic water opens joints into conduits and cavities, producing karst, sinkholes and very localised permeability.

05Metamorphic rocks

Heat and pressure alter existing rocks without melting them. Limestone becomes marble, sandstone becomes quartzite, shale becomes slate, and deeper metamorphism produces schist and gneiss. New minerals grow in preferred orientations, producing foliation — the layered fabric that gives slate its cleavage and schist its sheen.

Foliation makes the rock anisotropic: it is weaker and more deformable across the fabric than along it, and it controls how a tunnel or a slope fails. Marble is strong but dissolves like limestone, and some schists contain low-friction or swelling minerals that turn a stable excavation into a problem.

06Folds, faults and joints

The attitude of a plane is described by its strike (the compass direction of the horizontal line on it) and its dip (the angle it makes with the horizontal, measured perpendicular to strike). Folds bend strata into anticlines and synclines; faults break them and displace one side relative to the other. Normal faults form in extension, reverse and thrust faults in compression, and strike-slip faults in shear.

Fault zones are rarely clean planes: they contain breccia, gouge or clay, which are weak and often wet. Joints are the most common discontinuities of all. Their spacing, persistence, roughness and infill decide whether a rock mass behaves as a blocky solid or as a pile of loose material.

07Weathering and the weathered profile

Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion then removes the debris. Physical processes include freeze–thaw, salt crystallisation, unloading and thermal cycling. Chemical processes include hydrolysis of feldspars, oxidation of iron minerals, carbonation and dissolution of carbonate. Roots and burrowing organisms add biological breakdown.

Weathered rock is described by grades from fresh to residual soil, and the boundaries matter: hard rock can be ripped, highly weathered rock needs support, and residual soil behaves as a soil. In tropical climates the profile can be tens of metres deep, so the strong rock assumed at design stage may be far below the surface.

08Site investigation

Site investigation starts at the desk: geological and topographic maps, aerial photographs, old borehole records and the previous use of the site. A walkover comes next, looking for outcrops, springs, seepage, slope movement and made ground. Only then are boreholes and trial pits justified.

Cores are logged for recovery, RQD, fracture spacing, weathering grade and water strikes. In situ tests — standard penetration, cone penetration, packer permeability, plate loading — give parameters in the ground rather than in a sample. Groundwater monitoring over time is essential; a single water level reading is a snapshot, not a design value.

09Rock mass classification and maps

Intact rock strength is not the design parameter; the discontinuities are. RQD measures the proportion of core recovered as intact pieces longer than 100 mm. RMR combines strength, RQD, spacing, joint condition and groundwater into one rating, and the Q system combines six ratios for tunnel support. GSI uses blockiness and joint condition to estimate strength and stiffness for numerical models.

Geological maps put this in context: boundaries, dip and strike symbols, faults and borehole positions, with cross-sections between them. A map is an interpretation at a stated scale, not a photograph of the truth, so treat it as a hypothesis to be tested by the next borehole.

Rock quality designation
\[\mathrm{RQD} = \frac{\sum (L \ge 100\ \mathrm{mm})}{L_{\mathrm{run}}} \times 100\%\]
Total core recovery
\[\mathrm{TCR} = \frac{\text{recovered length}}{\text{drilled length}} \times 100\%\]
RMR rating
\[\mathrm{RMR} = R_{\mathrm{UCS}} + R_{\mathrm{RQD}} + R_{\mathrm{spacing}} + R_{\mathrm{joint}} + R_{\mathrm{water}}\]
Q value
\[Q = \left(\frac{\mathrm{RQD}}{J_n}\right)\left(\frac{J_r}{J_a}\right)\left(\frac{J_w}{\mathrm{SRF}}\right)\]

10Measuring planes: compass–clinometer field work

Strike and dip are read on the outcrop with a compass–clinometer. The compass plate is laid along the steepest water-drop line to read the dip direction; turning the plate 90° gives the strike, a horizontal line quoted in two directions 180° apart. The clinometer, a weighted pendulum inside the instrument, reads the dip angle.

A single reading is rarely enough, because joints scatter: several readings on the same set are averaged. One coastal outcrop gave strikes of 114–119° and dips of 40–45°, reported as about 116°/42° with dip direction 205°. When a vertical section cuts a plane obliquely it shows an apparent dip smaller than the true dip — the classic trap when drawing cross-sections.

Apparent dip
\[\tan \beta = \tan \delta \cdot \sin \alpha\]
β apparent dip, δ true dip, α the angle between the section line and the strike

11Groundwater: aquifers and the water table

An aquifer stores and transmits useful amounts of water — sand, gravel or fractured rock; an aquitard transmits it only slowly, and an aquiclude hardly at all. Where the aquifer has a free upper surface, that surface is the water table; where it is sealed above and below by low-permeability strata it is confined, and a borehole can bring water above the local ground under artesian pressure.

The water table is measured directly by probing shallow holes or standpipes — on one beach three holes showed water at 8, 11 and 16 cm depth — and it is rarely level: it slopes with the regional hydraulic gradient, and groundwater flows down that gradient.

Hydraulic gradient
\[i = \frac{\Delta h}{L}\]
head difference over flow length between two observation points

12Mass movements: names and responses

Slope failures are grouped by how the material moves. A fall drops rock through the air from a face; a topple rotates a column forward about its base; a slide moves along a discrete surface, rotational in soil or planar on a bedding plane or joint; a flow deforms like a viscous fluid, from slow earthflows to rapid debris flows.

Rain is the common trigger, because rising pore pressure cuts effective stress and therefore strength. Signs to read on a slope: arcuate tension cracks at the crest, tilted trees, and springs or seepage lines. Responses work from cheap to heavy — drainage first, then regrading, then retaining structures. A simple planar block slide is checked with a factor of safety of resisting to driving force.

Planar slide factor of safety
\[F = \frac{c A + W \cos \beta \tan \varphi}{W \sin \beta}\]
c and φ along the joint, W block weight, β the joint dip

Key terms

Rock cycle
The continuous transformation of igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rock by melting, weathering, deposition, burial and deformation.
Bedding plane
The surface separating two beds of sedimentary rock; a persistent weakness for sliding, seepage and excavation.
Strike and dip
The compass direction of a plane's horizontal line and its angle below the horizontal, measured perpendicular to strike.
Karst
Terrain in soluble rock such as limestone, where dissolution creates cavities, conduits, sinkholes and irregular groundwater flow.
RQD
Rock quality designation: the percentage of a core run recovered as intact pieces longer than 100 mm.
Weathering grade
A six-step description of rock decomposition from fresh rock to residual soil, used to judge excavatability and support.
Fault gouge
Fine, clay-rich crushed rock within a fault zone, typically weak, low-friction and often a pathway for water.
Anisotropy
Direction-dependent behaviour, common in foliated metamorphic rocks, where strength and stiffness differ across and along the fabric.
Apparent dip
The dip seen on a section that is not perpendicular to strike; it is always smaller than the true dip.
Aquifer
A permeable body — sand, gravel or fractured rock — that stores and transmits significant quantities of groundwater.
Worked example

Classifying a 1.5 m core run

A 1.5 m core run recovers 1.42 m of rock. The intact pieces are 320, 240, 180, 60, 400, 90 and 130 mm long. Intact rock strength is about 60 MPa, joints are spaced about 0.5 m apart and are slightly rough with thin, slightly weathered coatings, and the core is damp. Two main joint sets are present. Estimate RQD, RMR and Q, and comment on the rock mass.

  1. Total core recovery: TCR = 1.42/1.50 × 100% = 94.7%, so little material was lost.
  2. RQD counts only pieces of 100 mm or more: (320 + 240 + 180 + 400 + 130)/1500 = 1270/1500 = 84.7%, rounded to about 85%.
  3. RMR inputs and ratings on the standard tables: strength 60 MPa gives 7; RQD 85% gives 17; spacing 0.5 m gives 10; slightly rough, slightly weathered joints give 25; damp conditions give 10. Total RMR = 69.
  4. An RMR of 69 places the rock mass in class II, described as good rock with stand-up time of many years for a moderate span.
  5. Q inputs: RQD/J_n = 85/4 = 21.3 for two joint sets; J_r/J_a = 3/2 = 1.5 for rough, undulating, slightly altered joints; J_w/SRF = 0.66/1.0 for damp conditions in competent rock.
  6. Q = 21.3 × 1.5 × 0.66 = 21, which is also in the good rock range.
  7. Sanity check: both systems agree the mass is good, but the strength rating is the weakest input (7 out of 15), so the block size and joint condition are doing the work — support design should follow the jointing rather than the intact strength.
AnswerRQD = 84.7%, RMR = 69 (class II, good rock) and Q ≈ 21 (good rock). The mass is of good quality, but the moderately strong intact rock means behaviour is joint-controlled, so support and excavation should be designed from the discontinuity pattern rather than from the intact strength alone.

Exam method

  • Describe a rock for engineering with texture, mineralogy, strength and discontinuities — the geological name alone earns very little.
  • RQD is a length ratio, not a recovery; state that pieces shorter than 100 mm are excluded and quote it with the run length.
  • For slope or tunnel questions, first check whether the structure daylights in the face, then discuss kinematic admissibility before recommending support.
  • Treat a geological map as an interpretation at a scale; say what it shows, where the uncertainty is, and what borehole or exposure would test it.
  • Always quote dip as direction plus angle (e.g. 205°/42°); when reading a dip from a cross-section, check whether the section is perpendicular to strike before trusting the angle.

Weathering (decomposition) grades of rock material

The six-grade scale used in Hong Kong to describe how far rock material has decomposed. The grade is assessed in the field from discolouration, strength and simple index tests, and it feeds into logging, description and foundation decisions.

GradeDescriptive termTypical field characteristics
IFreshNo visible sign of decomposition; the rock material retains its original strength.
IISlightly decomposedDiscolouration and staining, mainly along discontinuities; strength slightly reduced from fresh.
IIIModerately decomposedDecomposition visible throughout the material; the rock is noticeably weakened.
IVHighly decomposedMaterial can be crumbled or broken by hand; slakes in water (granitic rocks).

VIResidual soilAll rock material converted to soil; the original texture and fabric are lost.

Q01A core run is 1.2 m long and recovers 1.1 m of rock, of which 0.9 m arrives as pieces longer than 100 mm. What is the RQD?

WhyRQD uses the run length as the denominator: 0.9/1.2 × 100% = 75%, not 82% which would wrongly use the recovered length.

Q02A normal fault is one in which:

WhyNormal faults dip steeply and form in extension, with the hanging wall dropping; reverse and thrust faults form in compression.

Q03Which ground condition is most characteristic of limestone terrain?

WhyLimestone dissolves in acidic water, producing karst: cavities, conduits, sinkholes and permeability that is high but very uneven.

Q04A metamorphic rock splits easily into flat sheets because:

WhyDirected pressure aligns platy minerals such as mica, creating foliation or cleavage; the rock is weakest and most deformable across that fabric.

Q05A vertical section cuts obliquely across the strike of a dipping bed. The dip shown on that section is:

WhyThe apparent dip on an oblique section is always less than the true dip, reaching the true value only when the section is perpendicular to strike.

Q06A stratum is saturated but transmits water only very slowly. It is best described as:

WhyAn aquitard allows some movement but at rates far below an aquifer; an aquiclude is effectively impermeable.
