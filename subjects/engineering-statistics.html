The language of uncertainty for engineers: describing a sample, choosing a distribution, putting a range around an estimate and testing a claim. It is the bridge between measurements and decisions on site.

01Describing a sample

Two features summarise most data: where it sits and how spread out it is. The mean is the balance point, the median resists outliers and the mode is the most frequent value.

For spread, the sample standard deviation divides by n − 1 rather than n, correcting the tendency of a sample to understate variation in the population. Always plot before you summarise: a histogram shows skew, gaps and outliers that a mean cannot.

  • Comparing mean and median is a quick skew check.
  • The coefficient of variation, s divided by x̄, compares spread between variables measured on different scales.
Sample mean
\[\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum x_i\]
Sample standard deviation
\[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}}\]
n − 1 degrees of freedom

02Probability and random variables

Probability assigns a number between 0 and 1 to an event. The multiplication rule introduces conditional probability: P(A | B) is the probability of A given that B has happened. Events are independent when P(A | B) = P(A), and only then does the joint probability factorise as P(A) P(B).

A random variable maps outcomes to numbers. Its expectation E(X) is the long-run average and its variance Var(X) measures spread around it.

  • Shifting a variable changes its mean but not its spread.
  • Scaling it multiplies the spread by the scale factor.
Conditional probability
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\]
Linear transformations
\[E(aX + b) = a\,E(X) + b; \quad \mathrm{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)\]

03The normal distribution

The normal distribution N(μ, σ²) is symmetric and bell-shaped, fixed entirely by its mean and standard deviation. About 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three — the empirical rule, which makes quick plausibility checks possible.

To use tables, standardise first, then find the tail area for the z value and combine tails for two-sided questions. Draw the curve, shade the area you want and label it before reading a table; sign and tail errors, not misunderstanding, cause most lost marks.

Standard score
\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]
how many standard deviations x lies from the mean

04Binomial and Poisson

The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials with the same probability p: fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independence. Its mean is np and its variance np(1 − p).

The Poisson counts events in a fixed interval of time or space when they occur independently at a constant rate λ; its mean and variance are both λ. Poisson is the large-n, small-p limit of the binomial with λ = np, so it suits rare events such as defects per batch or calls per hour.

  • Check the assumptions first: no fixed number of trials rules out the binomial.
  • A strongly varying rate rules out the Poisson.
Binomial probability
\[P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{n - x}\]
Poisson probability
\[P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x}{x!}\]
with λ = np when approximating the binomial

05Sampling and the central limit theorem

A statistic calculated from a sample is itself random: repeat the sampling and the mean changes. The sampling distribution of the mean has mean μ and standard error σ/√n, so quadrupling the sample halves the standard error.

The central limit theorem says that for large n the sample mean is approximately normal whatever the population shape, which is why normal-based methods survive on skewed data. Roughly thirty observations is often enough, but heavy skew needs more.

When σ is unknown, replace it with s and use the t distribution, whose heavier tails account for the extra uncertainty.

Standard error of the mean
\[\mathrm{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

06Confidence intervals

A confidence interval puts a range around an estimate and states the uncertainty. With σ known the interval is x̄ ± z σ/√n; with σ unknown use s and the t critical value with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

A 95% interval is wider than a 90% one, and it narrows as n grows. The interpretation is about the method, not the single interval: if sampling were repeated many times, 95% of intervals built this way would contain μ. It is not a 95% probability that this particular fixed interval contains μ.

Report the interval with units and its confidence level.

Confidence interval, σ known
\[\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]
Confidence interval, σ unknown
\[\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

07Hypothesis tests

A test asks whether the data are consistent with a null hypothesis H₀, usually no effect or no difference. Choose the alternative, set the significance level α, calculate a test statistic and compare it with a critical value or a p-value. Reject H₀ when p < α; failing to reject does not prove H₀ true.

Two errors are possible: a Type I error rejects a true H₀, with probability α, and a Type II error fails to reject a false H₀. A confidence interval and a two-sided test agree when the interval excludes the hypothesised value.

State the assumptions — independence, normality or a large sample — because a test is only as good as its model.

One-sample t statistic
\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\]
compared with t_(α/2, n−1) for a two-sided test

08Correlation and regression

The correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between −1 and 1. An r near zero means no linear trend, not no relationship, and a single outlier can move it a long way. Correlation is not causation.

Least-squares regression fits ŷ = a + b x. The slope has units of y per unit of x, and r² is the proportion of variation explained.

Do not extrapolate beyond the observed range, and check a residual plot before trusting a prediction.

Regression coefficients
\[b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \quad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}\]
S_xy is the corrected sum of cross products

09Statistical quality control

Quality control applies the same reasoning to production. A control chart plots sample statistics against limits such as x̄ ± 3σ/√n, separating common-cause variation from special-cause signals; a point outside a limit, or a run of points on one side, prompts investigation rather than adjustment.

The capability index C_pk compares the specification width with 3σ and shows whether a process can meet tolerances even when it is in control. A process can be stable and still incapable: control charts describe variation, capability compares that variation with what the specification demands.

Control chart limits
\[\mathrm{UCL},\, \mathrm{LCL} = \bar{x} \pm \frac{3\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]
Process capability
\[C_{pk} = \frac{\min(\mathrm{USL} - \mu,\, \mu - \mathrm{LSL})}{3\sigma}\]
USL and LSL are the specification limits

10Lifetimes and waiting times: exponential and geometric

When a Poisson process produces events at rate λ, the exponential distribution models the time until the next one: gaps between vehicle arrivals, time to the first defect, or a component's service life. Its density is f(x) = λe^(−λx), its mean and standard deviation are both 1/λ, and the cumulative form F(x) = 1 − e^(−λx) gives the probability of failure by time x.

The memoryless property says the past counts for nothing: a part that has survived s hours is as good as new, P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t). Real materials age, so verify the assumption before relying on it.

The geometric distribution is the discrete cousin — the number of trials up to and including the first success, with mean 1/p.

Exponential density and CDF
\[f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\]
mean and standard deviation both equal 1/λ
Memoryless property
\[P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\]
Geometric probability
\[P(X = x) = (1 - p)^{x-1} p\]
x trials up to and including the first success

11Chi-square goodness-of-fit

Count data need a different test. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test compares the observed frequency in each category with the frequency expected under the null hypothesis; a large statistic means the model fits poorly. Applied to a two-way table, the same statistic tests independence between two categorical variables.

Degrees of freedom: k − 1 for a fully specified distribution, minus one more for each parameter estimated from the data; for an r × c table, (r − 1)(c − 1).

The test is approximate, so pool categories until every expected count reaches about 5, and always compare counts, never percentages.

Chi-square statistic
\[\chi^2 = \sum_i \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]
O_i observed and E_i expected counts in category i
Expected count in a two-way table
\[E = \frac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}\]
Pool small categories Expected counts below about 5 inflate the chi-square statistic and make the p-value unreliable; merge neighbouring categories first.

12Power and sample size

Failing to reject H₀ is informative only if the test could have found an effect. The power of a test is the probability of rejecting H₀ when the alternative is true: power = 1 − β, where β is the Type II error rate. It grows with the sample size n, the significance level α and the size of the true effect, and falls as the data get noisier.

For a two-sided z test of a mean, the sample needed to detect a difference δ with power 1 − β is approximately (z_(α/2) + z_β)² σ² / δ². Because the critical values enter squared, detecting half the effect needs four times the sample.

Report power when a test is not significant — a null result from a tiny sample proves nothing.

Required sample size, two-sided z test
\[n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}\]
δ is the smallest difference worth detecting

Key terms

Standard error
The standard deviation of a sample statistic, showing how much an estimate would vary between repeated samples.
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for a population parameter, reported with a confidence level such as 95%.
Null hypothesis
The default claim of no effect or no difference that a significance test tries to find evidence against.
p-value
The probability of a result at least as extreme as the observed one, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true.
Central limit theorem
The result that the sample mean is approximately normally distributed for large samples, whatever the population shape.
Type I error
Rejecting a null hypothesis that is actually true; its probability is the significance level α.
Memoryless property
The feature of the exponential distribution that future waiting time does not depend on how long has already elapsed.
Power
The probability that a test correctly rejects the null hypothesis when the alternative is true, equal to 1 − β.
Worked example

Confidence interval and t-test on cube strengths

Nine concrete cubes are tested at 28 days and give a mean strength of 42.0 MPa with a sample standard deviation of 3.0 MPa. The specification target is a mean of 40 MPa. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the mean strength and test, at the 5% significance level, whether the mean strength differs from 40 MPa (two-sided).

  1. State the model: n = 9, x̄ = 42.0 MPa and s = 3.0 MPa with σ unknown, so use the t distribution with n − 1 = 8 degrees of freedom.
  2. Standard error: SE = s/√n = 3.0/√9 = 1.0 MPa.
  3. Critical value for a 95% two-sided interval with 8 degrees of freedom: t_(0.025, 8) = 2.306.
  4. Confidence interval: 42.0 ± 2.306 × 1.0 = 42.0 ± 2.31, giving 39.69 MPa to 44.31 MPa.
  5. Test statistic: t = (42.0 − 40.0) / 1.0 = 2.00, with 8 degrees of freedom.
  6. Critical value for the two-sided test is also 2.306, and 2.00 < 2.306, so fail to reject H₀; the p-value is above 0.05.
  7. Sanity check: the interval 39.7 to 44.3 MPa contains 40 MPa, which agrees with failing to reject the null hypothesis.
AnswerThe 95% confidence interval for the mean strength is 39.7 to 44.3 MPa. The two-sided t-test gives t = 2.00 with 8 degrees of freedom, which is less than 2.306, so there is insufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that the mean strength differs from 40 MPa.

Exam method

  • Write the null and alternative hypotheses and the significance level before calculating anything, and say fail to reject rather than accept.
  • Convert every question to a z or t value and draw the curve with the shaded area; most errors are sign or tail errors.
  • Learn the assumptions of each distribution — independence, fixed n, constant p, known σ — because marks are often for checking them.
  • Report a confidence interval with its confidence level and units, and interpret it in terms of the method rather than one sample.
  • For chi-square questions, check that every expected count is at least 5 and state the degrees of freedom before reading the table.

Q01In a normal distribution, approximately what proportion of observations lies within one standard deviation of the mean?

WhyThe empirical rule gives about 68% within one standard deviation, 95% within two and 99.7% within three.

Q02Which distribution is the natural model for the number of rare defects occurring per batch when there are many opportunities and a small defect rate?

WhyThe Poisson distribution models counts of rare events in an interval, and it is the large-n, small-p limit of the binomial.

Q03A 95% confidence interval for a population mean is (12.1, 15.9). Which interpretation is correct?

WhyThe confidence level describes the long-run success of the method, not the probability that this fixed interval contains the mean.

Q04A hypothesis test returns a p-value of 0.03 at a significance level of 0.05. What is the correct conclusion?

WhySince 0.03 < 0.05, the null hypothesis is rejected at the 5% level; a small p-value is evidence against H₀, not for it.

Q05The gaps between successive vehicle arrivals at a junction follow an exponential distribution with rate λ = 4 per minute. The probability that the next gap exceeds one minute is:

WhyFor an exponential variable, P(X > t) = e^(−λt); with t = 1 and λ = 4 this is e^(−4), a little under 0.02.

Q06A goodness-of-fit test has six categories, and one parameter of the hypothesised distribution was estimated from the data. The chi-square statistic has how many degrees of freedom?

WhyThe degrees of freedom are k − 1 minus one for each estimated parameter: 6 − 1 − 1 = 4.
