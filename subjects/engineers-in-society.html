Engineering decisions carry legal and ethical weight. This page covers the Hong Kong legal framework, contract basics, negligence, professional ethics and the statutory duties that protect safety and the environment.

01Common law in a statute-based city

Hong Kong's legal system continues the common law tradition: courts decide cases and earlier decisions bind later ones through precedent. Statutes — ordinances and their subsidiary regulations — sit alongside the common law and can override it where they conflict.

The difference between civil and criminal proceedings matters to an engineer:
- Civil disputes are decided on the balance of probabilities.
- Criminal charges must be proved beyond reasonable doubt.
- A construction dispute is usually civil even when the conduct involved was careless.

Burden of proof
civil: balance of probabilities · criminal: beyond reasonable doubt
a single act can give rise to both civil and criminal liability

02Contract: offer, acceptance, consideration

A contract is a voluntary agreement the law will enforce. Formation needs offer and acceptance, consideration in the sense of something of value each side gives, intention to create legal relations, capacity and legality.

  • A reply that changes the terms is a counter-offer, which kills the original offer; silence is not acceptance.
  • Simple contracts may be oral, written or made by conduct.
  • A deed must be signed, sealed and delivered and needs no consideration.
  • Formalities matter because they change remedies and the limitation period — broadly six years for a simple contract and twelve years for a specialty.
Formation test
offer + acceptance + consideration + intention + capacity + legality
each element must be present; a missing element means no enforceable contract

03Misrepresentation and the remedies for breach

Not every false statement is a breach. A misrepresentation is a pre-contractual statement of fact that is untrue and induces the contract. The remedy follows the wrong:
- Fraudulent if made knowingly or recklessly.
- Negligent if made carelessly without reasonable grounds.
- Innocent if honestly believed on reasonable grounds.

Expectation damages put the claimant where performance would have left them — lost profit and consequential loss — while reliance damages repay wasted expenditure. Where money is not enough, specific performance or an injunction may be ordered, and a quantum meruit claim recovers reasonable remuneration for work accepted without an agreed price.

Misrepresentation categories
fraudulent (knowing) → negligent (careless) → innocent (honest)
the state of mind of the maker decides which remedies and damages are open
Misrepresentation compared
TypeMaker's state of mindTypical remedy
FraudulentKnows, or does not care, that it is falseRescission and damages for all loss caused
NegligentCareless, no reasonable groundsRescission and damages
InnocentHonest, reasonable beliefRescission, sometimes damages in lieu

04Breach, damages and risk allocation

When a party fails to perform, the innocent party claims damages. The usual aim is to put the claimant in the position as if the contract had been performed, subject to the duty to mitigate loss.

Construction contracts allocate risk explicitly through variations, extension-of-time clauses and exclusion clauses. Delay is handled by liquidated damages, a pre-agreed sum per week of delay.

  • Such a clause is enforceable only if it is a genuine pre-estimate of loss.
  • A figure designed to frighten the other side is an unenforceable penalty.
  • Standard forms of contract exist because that balance has been tested in practice.
Liquidated damages
\[\mathrm{LD} = r \times w, \quad \text{capped at } c \times \text{contract sum}\]
r is the weekly rate, w the weeks of culpable delay, c the agreed cap

05Negligence and the duty of care

Tort addresses harm done outside contract. Negligence requires a duty of care, a breach of the standard expected of a reasonable engineer, and loss caused by that breach.

  • Causation is tested by asking whether the loss would have happened but for the breach, and a wrongdoer generally takes the victim as found.
  • Defences include contributory negligence, which reduces damages, and voluntary acceptance of risk.
  • Employers are vicariously liable for employees acting in the course of employment.
  • Safety failures can attract criminal liability as well as civil claims.

06Disputes: mediation, adjudication and arbitration

Disputes need not end in court. The resolution ladder runs from negotiation, through mediation — a neutral third party helps the parties reach their own agreement — to adjudication, a fast interim decision on payment disputes, and arbitration, a private and confidential hearing whose award is binding and enforceable. Each rung trades speed for formality.

  • Statutory adjudication protects cash flow: a party to a qualifying construction contract can refer a payment dispute and receive a decision within weeks, and the decision stands until finally resolved elsewhere.
  • Arbitration suits technical disputes because the tribunal is chosen for expertise.
  • Mediation preserves the working relationship that litigation burns.
Dispute resolution ladder
negotiation → mediation → adjudication → arbitration → litigation
each rung is faster or more binding than the last; choose by the relationship, the sums and the need for precedent

07Professional ethics and the code of conduct

Professional ethics is the set of obligations that belongs to the profession rather than to a particular employer. It includes:
- competence within one's own limits
- honesty in reports and measurement
- confidentiality
- declaring conflicts of interest
- refusing inducements
- reporting unsafe practice

The only defensible hierarchy puts public safety and welfare above the client, the employer and personal gain. A code of conduct is the profession's way of making that commitment enforceable, and adherence is a qualifying requirement for membership of a professional institution.

Duty hierarchy
public safety → law → client instruction → employer interest → personal gain
when duties collide, the higher duty is answered first

08Statutory duties: safety and the environment

Legislation imposes duties independently of whatever the contract says.

  • Safety law places obligations on employers, contractors, designers and site management to provide a safe system of work, competent people, safe plant and a safe place; serious incidents must be reported and the scene preserved.
  • Environmental law controls air, noise, waste and discharges, and large projects pass through an environmental impact assessment and statutory approval before they can proceed.

An engineer who signs a document accepts responsibility for it, which is why approvals, method statements and monitoring records are all evidence.

Where an engineer's obligations come from
SourceTypical dutyHeld to account by
ContractPerform the agreed scope, to the agreed standard, on timeThe other contracting party
Law of tortTake reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harmAnyone injured by a breach
StatuteComply with safety, building and environmental requirementsRegulators and the courts
Professional codeAct with integrity and competenceThe professional institution
Public expectationProtect safety, the environment and future usersConsent, consultation and reputation

09Public works approval and statutory planning

Public infrastructure is delivered through a staged approvals process:
- A project is first defined in a statement setting out the need and scope.
- A feasibility statement then covers engineering, environmental and heritage aspects.
- The project enters a rolling funding programme as a category item.
- It is upgraded through planning, design, gazetting and public engagement until it reaches the category approved for tender and construction.

Each stage exists so that spending authority and scope control sit in the same hands. Land use runs in parallel through statutory planning: outline zoning plans divide territory into zones, each listing uses that are permitted or prohibited, and plot ratio — gross floor area divided by site area — caps the density of what can be built. A scheme must clear both the funding gate and the planning gate before it touches the ground.

Plot ratio
\[\mathrm{PR} = \frac{\mathrm{GFA}}{A_{\text{site}}}\]
gross floor area over site area; the zoning plan caps it

10The engineer in society

Engineers act on the built environment, so they make social choices: who benefits, who pays, what is displaced and what is left for the next generation. Sustainability is commonly framed as balancing environmental, social and economic needs, and real projects rarely satisfy all three at once.

That is why judgement should be recorded. State the options, the criteria, the stakeholders and the reasons for the decision. When instructions and the public interest collide, ask whether the decision could be defended openly; if it could not, it needs revisiting before the concrete is poured.

Key terms

Consideration
Something of value given by each party in exchange for the other's promise; a contract under seal needs none.
Counter-offer
A reply that changes the terms of an offer, which terminates the original offer and cannot then be accepted.
Negligence
A breach of a duty of care that causes loss which a reasonable person would have foreseen.
Vicarious liability
An employer's strict responsibility for the acts of employees performed in the course of employment.
Liquidated damages
A pre-agreed sum payable for a specified breach, enforceable only if it is a genuine pre-estimate of loss.
Conflict of interest
A situation where a personal, financial or other interest could improperly influence professional judgement.
Misrepresentation
An untrue pre-contractual statement of fact that induces a party to contract; remedies depend on whether it was fraudulent, negligent or innocent.
Adjudication
A fast, interim resolution of a payment dispute under statutory provisions for construction contracts, binding until finally determined.
Worked example

Liquidated damages on a delayed contract

A contract has a sum of HK$48,000,000. The contract provides liquidated damages of HK$120,000 per week for culpable delay, capped at 10 per cent of the contract sum. The contractor completes nine weeks late, and three of those weeks are covered by an extension of time for exceptional weather. Calculate the damages payable and check the cap.

  1. Only culpable delay counts, so the chargeable period is 9 − 3 = 6 weeks.
  2. Liquidated damages = weekly rate × chargeable weeks = 120,000 × 6 = HK$720,000.
  3. The cap is 10 per cent of HK$48,000,000, which is HK$4,800,000.
  4. The claim of HK$720,000 is well below the cap, so the cap does not limit the recovery.
  5. Sanity check: HK$720,000 is 1.5 per cent of the contract sum, which is a plausible pre-estimate of the loss caused by six weeks of delay; a figure far larger would invite an argument that it is a penalty.
AnswerLiquidated damages of HK$720,000 are payable for the six chargeable weeks. Extensions of time remove the covered delay from the calculation, and the capped sum of HK$4,800,000 is not reached.

Exam method

  • Structure an answer around the legal test, then apply the facts: duty, breach, causation, loss for negligence; offer, acceptance, consideration for contract.
  • Distinguish civil from criminal liability and state the standard of proof whenever the question is about liability.
  • In ethics questions, identify the competing duties first, then rank them with public safety at the top and say why.
  • Dispute questions are ladder questions: name the forum, then justify the rung — speed and cash flow point to adjudication, finality and expertise to arbitration, and the relationship to mediation.
