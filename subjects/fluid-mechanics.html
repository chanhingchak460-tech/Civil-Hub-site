How fluids at rest and in motion push on structures, and how water moves through pipes, channels and machines. Hydrostatics, buoyancy, Bernoulli, momentum, friction losses and boundary layers form one connected toolkit for water engineering.

01What makes a fluid a fluid

A fluid deforms continuously under shear, however small the shear. Two properties dominate: density ρ, the mass per unit volume, and dynamic viscosity μ, the resistance to shearing. For a Newtonian fluid the shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient, with μ as the constant.

Water near room temperature has ρ ≈ 998 kg/m³ and μ ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa·s; air is about 1.2 kg/m³ and 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s. Kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ is convenient because it appears directly in the Reynolds number.

A real fluid sticks to a solid surface — the no-slip condition — so velocity is zero at the wall. That one fact creates the boundary layer and most of the friction in engineering.

Newtonian shear stress
\[\tau = \mu \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}y}\]
velocity gradient du/dy
Kinematic viscosity
\[\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}\]
units of m²/s

02Pressure in a fluid at rest

Pressure acts equally in all directions in a fluid at rest, and depth sets its value: p = p₀ + ρ g h below a free surface. The same fluid at the same depth has the same pressure whatever the container's shape.

A manometer reads a pressure difference as a column height, and pressure head p/(ρg), quoted in metres of water, keeps the arithmetic in the same units as elevation.

A hydraulic jack uses the same principle: modest pressure over a small piston, multiplied by a much larger area.

Hydrostatic pressure
\[p = p_0 + \rho g h\]
below the free surface

03Forces on submerged surfaces

The force on a plane surface is the pressure at its centroid times its area: F = ρ g h̄ A, where h̄ is the depth of the centroid. On a horizontal base this is pressure times area; on a vertical gate the pressure grows with depth, so the resultant acts lower.

That point is the centre of pressure, y_cp = y_c + I_x/(y_c A), with y measured along the plane from the free surface. For a rectangular gate of depth d and width b, I_x = b d³/12.

Keep the dry side in the balance. A gate with water on one side and air on the other feels a net force, and that difference is what the hinge must resist.

Force on a plane surface
\[F = \rho g \bar{h} A\]
depth of the centroid
Centre of pressure
\[y_{cp} = y_c + \frac{I_x}{y_c A}\]
always below the centroid

04Buoyancy and floating stability

Archimedes: the buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced, F_B = ρ_fluid g V_displaced, acting through the centroid of the displaced volume. A body denser than the fluid sinks until the displaced weight balances it; a floating body displaces exactly its own weight.

Floating upright is a separate question. When the body tilts, the centre of buoyancy moves and the weight and buoyancy forces form a couple. The metacentric height GM measures that restoring effect: positive is stable, negative capsizes.

Wide, shallow hulls are stable; narrow, tall ones are not.

Buoyant force
\[F_B = \rho_{\text{fluid}} g V_{\text{displaced}}\]
at the displaced centroid

05Continuity and the energy equation

Mass is conserved. For steady, incompressible flow through a changing area, Q = A₁V₁ = A₂V₂, so a constriction speeds the fluid up and a wider section slows it down.

Bernoulli's equation relates pressure, velocity and elevation along a streamline for steady, frictionless flow: p/(ρg) + V²/(2g) + z = constant. Each term is a head in metres — pressure, velocity and elevation head.

It explains the venturi meter (higher throat velocity, lower pressure) and the pitot tube, which turns velocity head into a pressure difference. It fails wherever losses or pumps matter, which is most real pipework.

Continuity
\[Q = A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2\]
steady, incompressible
Bernoulli equation
\[\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z = \text{constant}\]
along a streamline, no losses

06Momentum and forces on bodies

Bernoulli gives pressures; momentum gives forces. For a steady stream, the net force on the fluid equals its rate of change of momentum, ΣF = ρQ(V_out − V_in), treated as vectors. The same force acts in reverse on whatever turns the flow — a bend, reducer or vane.

A jet on a plate is the standard case: a flat plate stopping the jet normally feels F = ρQV, while a hemispherical cup turning it through 180° feels about twice that, which is why Pelton wheels use a split bucket. Measured forces fall short because the jet spreads and the flow is never perfectly steady.

For a pipe bend the same balance gives the anchor force, often dominated by the pressure term.

Momentum balance
\[\sum F = \rho Q (V_{\text{out}} - V_{\text{in}})\]
Force of a jet on a vane
\[F = \rho Q V (1 - \cos \theta)\]
90° flat plate, 180° cup

07Pipe flow, friction and minor losses

Real pipes lose energy to friction. The Reynolds number Re = ρVD/μ decides the regime: below about 2000 the flow is laminar, above about 4000 turbulent, with a transition between.

Laminar flow has f = 64/Re; turbulent f depends on Re and relative roughness, read from the Moody chart or the Colebrook equation. The Darcy–Weisbach equation turns f into a head loss, h_f = f L V²/(2gD). Because V is squared, doubling the flow rate roughly quadruples the loss — the most useful fact in pipe design.

Fittings, bends and valves add minor losses, h_m = K V²/(2g). A long pipeline is dominated by friction; a compact plant often by fittings.

Reynolds number
\[\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho V D}{\mu} = \frac{V D}{\nu}\]
inertia over viscosity
Darcy–Weisbach head loss
\[h_f = \frac{f L V^2}{2 g D}\]
not the Fanning factor
Minor loss
\[h_m = \frac{K V^2}{2g}\]
empirical, per fitting

08Boundary layers, separation and drag

At a solid boundary the no-slip condition creates a boundary layer where velocity rises from zero at the wall to the free-stream value. It begins thin and laminar, grows along the surface, and beyond a critical Reynolds number turns turbulent — thicker, but with faster fluid near the wall.

A boundary layer cannot follow a sharp adverse pressure gradient, so it separates and leaves a low-pressure wake. Drag combines friction on the surface with pressure drag from that wake: C_D = F/(½ρV²A).

Streamlining delays separation and cuts pressure drag; a blunt body is dominated by it. The same ideas explain weir flow, where crest shape controls how the nappe springs clear.

Drag coefficient
\[C_D = \frac{F}{\frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A}\]
A is the frontal area

09Weirs: discharge from the upstream level

A sharp-crested weir turns the upstream water level into a flow measurement. For a rectangular notch of width b, Q = (2/3) C_d √(2g) b h^{3/2}; for a V-notch of total angle α, Q = (8/15) C_d √(2g) tan(α/2) h^{5/2}, with h measured from the crest or vertex.

The edges must be sharp so the nappe springs clear; a clinging nappe invalidates the rating. Calibration plots log Q against log h: the slope should read 1.5 or 2.5 respectively, and the intercept gives the discharge coefficient.

A 90° notch passes less water than a 60° notch at the same head — narrowing the angle raises sensitivity for small flows.

Rectangular weir discharge
\[Q = \frac{2}{3} C_d \sqrt{2g}\, b\, h^{3/2}\]
h above the crest, b the notch width
V-notch weir discharge
\[Q = \frac{8}{15} C_d \sqrt{2g} \tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\, h^{5/2}\]
α the total notch angle, h from the vertex

10The venturi meter as a working gauge

A venturi meters flow from a measured pressure drop. With upstream area A₁ and throat area A₂, the ideal discharge follows from continuity and Bernoulli; friction and contraction fold into the coefficient of discharge C_d, typically 0.92–0.99, so Q = C_d A₂ √[2g Δh / (1 − (A₂/A₁)²)] with Δh the piezometric head drop.

The laboratory calibration plotted Q against √Δh and read C_d ≈ 0.96 from the slope, constant across the flow range.

Piezometer tubes along the meter map the pressure drop and show most of it recovering in the diffuser.

Venturi discharge
\[Q = C_d A_2 \sqrt{\frac{2 g \Delta h}{1 - (A_2/A_1)^2}}\]
Δh piezometric head difference between inlet and throat

11Floating stability in numbers

Stability of a floating body is quantified by the metacentric height GM = I/V − BG, where I is the second moment of the waterplane area, V the displaced volume and BG the height of the centre of gravity above the centre of buoyancy. Positive GM means the metacentre sits above G and small heels restore; negative GM capsizes.

A rectangular barge 10 m by 4 m displacing 100 m³ has I/V = (10 × 4³/12)/100 = 0.53 m, so G must stay within about half a metre above B.

Wide, shallow hulls forgive; tall, narrow loads do not.

Metacentric height
\[\mathrm{GM} = \frac{I}{V} - \mathrm{BG}\]
positive for stable equilibrium

Key terms

Viscosity
A fluid's resistance to shearing, measured as the proportionality between shear stress and velocity gradient.
Centre of pressure
The point on a submerged surface through which the resultant hydrostatic force acts; it lies below the centroid.
Metacentric height
The distance between the centre of gravity and the metacentre; positive values make a floating body stable.
Reynolds number
The dimensionless ratio ρVD/μ that predicts whether flow is laminar or turbulent.
Darcy friction factor
The dimensionless factor f in the Darcy–Weisbach equation, a function of Reynolds number and relative roughness.
Boundary layer
The thin region next to a surface where velocity rises from zero at the wall to the free-stream value.
Coefficient of discharge
The ratio of actual to theoretical flow through a meter or weir, folding in contraction and friction; typically 0.92–0.99 for a venturi.
Nappe
The sheet of water springing clear over a weir crest; a clinging nappe means the weir is not properly ventilated and its rating fails.
Worked example

Friction head loss in a water main

Water with kinematic viscosity ν = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s flows at 0.050 m³/s through a 150 mm diameter steel pipe 200 m long. Take the Darcy friction factor as 0.020. Find the velocity, check the Reynolds number, compute the friction head loss, and state the pressure drop it represents.

  1. Cross-sectional area: A = πD²/4 = π × 0.15²/4 = 0.01767 m².
  2. Velocity: V = Q/A = 0.050/0.01767 = 2.83 m/s.
  3. Reynolds number: Re = VD/ν = (2.83 × 0.15)/(1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 4.2 × 10⁵, well above 4000, so the flow is turbulent and f = 0.020 is plausible.
  4. Friction head loss: h_f = f L V²/(2gD) = (0.020 × 200 × 2.83²)/(2 × 9.81 × 0.15) = 32.0/2.94 = 10.9 m.
  5. Pressure drop: Δp = ρ g h_f = 1000 × 9.81 × 10.9 = 1.07 × 10⁵ Pa ≈ 107 kPa.
AnswerThe velocity is 2.83 m/s and Re ≈ 4.2 × 10⁵, so the flow is turbulent. The friction head loss is about 10.9 m, equivalent to a pressure drop of roughly 107 kPa. That is about 5.4 m of head per 100 m of pipe, high but believable at this velocity.

Exam method

  • Sketch the free body and mark the pressure on both sides of a gate; forgetting the dry side is the commonest error.
  • Check the Reynolds number before choosing a friction law — quoting 64/Re for a turbulent flow loses marks immediately.
  • In Bernoulli questions, state the assumptions (steady, incompressible, frictionless, along a streamline) and where losses enter the energy line.
  • Keep units consistent: convert mm to m, and remember that head in metres converts to pressure through ρg.
  • Weir and venturi calibration questions want a log–log or √Δh plot, not a table of numbers — quote the expected slope (1.5 or 2.5) and compare before reading off the coefficient.

Q01A vertical rectangular gate is lowered so that its centroid is twice as deep. The total hydrostatic force on it:

WhyF = ρgh̄A and the area is unchanged, so doubling the centroid depth doubles the force.

Q02In a horizontal venturi meter carrying a steady incompressible flow, the pressure at the throat is:

WhyContinuity gives a higher velocity at the smaller throat, and Bernoulli converts that rise in velocity head into a fall in pressure.

Q03If the flow rate in a turbulent pipe doubles while the friction factor stays roughly constant, the friction head loss becomes:

Whyh_f ∝ V² and V doubles with Q, so the head loss rises by a factor of about four.

Q04A ship floats in equilibrium. The buoyant force acting on it equals:

WhyA floating body displaces water whose weight equals the total weight of the vessel and its contents, so the buoyant force balances that total weight.

Q05For a sharp-crested V-notch weir, the discharge scales with the head h as:

WhyQ = (8/15) C_d √(2g) tan(α/2) h^{5/2}, so the V-notch follows a 5/2 power law — steeper than the rectangular weir's 3/2.

Q06A floating body has its centre of gravity 0.2 m above the centre of buoyancy, and I/V = 0.6 m. Its metacentric height is:
