Geotechnical design turns ground investigation into foundations, retaining structures and slopes that are safe against failure and acceptable in settlement. Almost every decision depends on effective stress, drainage and the water table.

01Site investigation and the ground model

Every geotechnical design starts by turning a site into a ground model: the sequence of soil and rock layers, their strength and stiffness, and the position of the water table.

  • Desk study of maps and records first, then boreholes and in-situ tests, then laboratory testing.
  • The standard penetration test and cone penetration test give a continuous feel for density and strength with depth; sampling gives parameters that tests in the ground cannot.
  • Pore pressure reduces effective stress and changes every strength and settlement calculation.

One borehole proves one point, not a site, so parameters are selected as characteristic values and combined with partial factors.

02Ultimate bearing capacity

Bearing capacity asks whether the ground can carry the foundation load without a shear failure. For a strip footing the ultimate pressure is

q_u = c′ N_c + q′ N_q + 0.5 γ B N_γ,

where c′ is effective cohesion, q′ the surcharge at foundation level, γ the soil unit weight, B the footing width, and N the bearing capacity factors that grow with friction angle.

  • Shape factors adjust the result for square and circular footings.
  • A water table in the failure zone reduces the effective unit weight.
  • A factor of safety or a set of partial factors brings the ultimate capacity down to an allowable or design value.
Terzaghi bearing capacity, strip footing
\[q_u = c' N_c + q' N_q + 0.5\gamma B N_\gamma\]
N factors depend on φ′; use effective stresses below the water table

03Eccentric loading and contact pressure

Real columns arrive with moment as well as load, so the contact pressure under a footing is rarely uniform. The eccentricity is e = M/Q.

  • While the resultant stays within the middle third of the base — e ≤ B/6 — the whole base remains in contact and the pressure is trapezoidal: q_max,min = Q/(B L) (1 ± 6e/B).
  • Once e exceeds B/6, part of the base lifts off and contact is lost over a width B − 2e from the load side; the peak pressure then concentrates near the edge: q_max = 4Q / [3 L (B − 2e)].

The design response is the effective area concept: shrink the footing to the loaded zone B′ = B − 2e, check bearing on the reduced dimensions, and accept that a wide footing with a small moment is usually better than a narrow one with a large one.

Peak pressure, resultant within the middle third
\[q_{\max} = \frac{Q}{B L}\left(1 + \frac{6e}{B}\right)\]
e = M/Q; with e ≤ B/6 the minimum pressure is Q/(BL)(1 − 6e/B)
Peak pressure, partial contact
\[q_{\max} = \frac{4Q}{3L(B - 2e)}\]
e > B/6; contact is lost over the unloaded side and pressure concentrates near the edge

04Settlement and serviceability

Serviceability is checked by estimating settlement. Sands settle almost immediately, mainly by elastic compression; clays settle slowly as excess pore water drains, which is consolidation.

  • Elastic settlement is often estimated as S_e = q B (1 − ν²) I / E, where q is the applied pressure, ν Poisson's ratio, E the soil modulus and I an influence factor for shape and depth.
  • For a normally consolidated clay layer, the one-dimensional consolidation settlement is S_c = [C_c H / (1 + e_0)] log[(σ′_0 + Δσ′)/σ′_0], with compression index C_c, initial void ratio e_0, layer thickness H and the effective stress increase Δσ′.

Total settlement matters, but differential settlement is what cracks structures, so it is checked between adjacent supports.

Consolidation settlement
\[S_c = \frac{C_c H}{1 + e_0}\log\frac{\sigma'_0 + \Delta\sigma'}{\sigma'_0}\]
σ′ in kPa, H in metres, log to base 10

05Piles: capacity and groups

A pile carries load by end bearing at its base and by shaft friction along its length:

Q_u = Q_p + Q_s = A_p q_p + Σ p L f_s,

where A_p is the base area, q_p the base pressure, p the perimeter and f_s the shaft friction of each layer.

  • In sand, base resistance grows with depth and effective stress; in clay, shaft friction relates to undrained strength.
  • Codes give correlations for each soil type; the method must suit the soil and the installation.
  • Piles are used when shallow foundations cannot reach competent ground, when uplift or lateral loads dominate, or when settlement must be limited.

A pile group is not a simple sum of single piles: overlapping stress bulbs increase settlement and group efficiency must be checked. Driven piles densify sand but vibrate; bored piles are quieter but produce spoil.

Ultimate pile capacity
\[Q_u = Q_p + Q_s = A_p q_p + \sum p L f_s\]

06Pile capacity: the standard methods

The methods behind Q_u = Q_p + Q_s differ by soil type.

  • In saturated clay, the base carries Q_p = 9 c_u A_p — nine times the undrained shear strength over the base area, which is simply a deep-bearing factor — and the shaft mobilises a fraction of the undrained strength through an adhesion factor: f_s = α c_u, with α falling from around 1.0 for soft clays to 0.5 or less for stiff clays.
  • In sand, end bearing grows with the effective surcharge at the base through a bearing factor limited by a cap, and shaft friction follows f_s = K σ′_v tan δ, with K the lateral pressure coefficient and δ the interface friction angle.

Soft clays bring a trap: settling fill or lowering groundwater can drag a pile down, and that negative skin friction adds to the applied load instead of resisting it — a pile in settling ground must be long enough to reach a firm, non-settling stratum.

End bearing, pile in saturated clay
\[Q_p = 9\,c_u A_p\]
c_u is the undrained shear strength at the base and A_p the base area
Shaft friction, pile in clay
\[f_s = \alpha\,c_u\]
α is the adhesion factor, roughly 0.5–1.0 depending on the clay stiffness
Shaft friction, pile in sand
\[f_s = K\,\sigma'_v \tan\delta\]
K lateral pressure coefficient, σ′_v effective vertical stress, δ interface friction

07Mat foundations

A mat or raft foundation covers the whole footprint and spreads load over a large area. It suits weak ground, heavy column loads, basements, and sites where individual footings would overlap.

  • Contact pressure depends on the relative stiffness of raft and soil: a flexible raft follows the load pattern, while a rigid raft settles evenly and redistributes pressure.
  • Where a basement displaces soil of about the same weight as the structure above, the result is a compensated foundation and the net loading can be small.

Rafts are often analysed with the superstructure, and checked for punching shear at columns and buoyancy uplift where the water table is high.

08Retaining walls and earth pressure

Retaining walls hold back soil. The horizontal pressure depends on how much the wall moves.

  • Active pressure develops when the wall yields slightly and the soil relaxes.
  • Passive pressure develops when the wall pushes into the soil and the soil resists.
  • For a dry, cohesionless backfill, Rankine's coefficient is K_a = tan²(45° − φ′/2), and the total active force grows as 0.5 γ H² K_a.

Design checks three modes: overturning about the toe, sliding along the base, and bearing capacity under the toe. Water is the usual cause of failure, so drainage, weep holes and a filter layer behind the wall are part of the design, not extras.

Rankine active earth pressure coefficient
\[K_a = \tan^2\left(45^\circ - \frac{\phi'}{2}\right)\]
the passive coefficient is the reciprocal for a horizontal backfill

09Wall stability as a ratio problem

The three wall checks are best written as ratios with target values, because the geometry and the partial factors then stay visible.

  • Sliding compares the horizontal resistance along the base with the horizontal thrust: F_sliding = ΣR / ΣH, where ΣR is the sum of friction and adhesion along the base (plus any passive soil at the toe that can be relied on) and ΣH is the total lateral thrust. A value of at least about 1.5 is normally demanded.
  • Overturning compares restoring moments about the toe with the overturning moments from the thrust, again with a target near 1.5–2.0, and the base width is chosen so the resultant lands in the middle third — the same e ≤ B/6 rule that governs footings.
  • Bearing is then checked under the toe with the eccentric contact pressure.

Iterating between these three checks is the actual design: a wider base helps all three, at the price of excavation and concrete.

Factor of safety against sliding
\[F_{\mathrm{sliding}} = \frac{\sum R}{\sum H}\]
ΣR is the resisting shear along the base; aim for at least about 1.5

10Slope stability

Slope stability compares the shear strength available along a potential failure surface with the shear stress needed to cause movement. For a circular slip in a uniform slope, a method of slices divides the mass and sums moments or forces; the ratio is the factor of safety.

F_s = Σ [c′ l + (W cos α − u l) tan φ′] / Σ W sin α

for the simple Fellenius approach, where W is the slice weight, α the inclination of its base and u the pore pressure.

Seepage lowers effective stress and therefore stability, which is why slopes often fail after heavy rain. Stabilisation uses gentler angles, drainage, retaining structures, soil nails or rock bolts, and surface protection against erosion.

Fellenius factor of safety
\[F_s = \frac{\sum\left[c' l + (W\cos\alpha - u l)\tan\phi'\right]}{\sum W\sin\alpha}\]

11Ground improvement

Ground improvement changes the ground so that ordinary foundations become feasible.

  • Compaction increases density and reduces settlement; replacement removes weak material.
  • Prefabricated vertical drains shorten the drainage path so consolidation happens during construction under a surcharge.
  • Stone columns and deep soil mixing add reinforcement and stiffness; grouting fills voids and reduces permeability; geotextiles and geogrids add tensile reinforcement and separate layers.

The choice depends on what is wrong with the ground — strength, stiffness, permeability or variability — and on the time available before the structure is loaded.

Key terms

Effective stress
Total stress minus pore water pressure; it controls soil strength and compression.
Characteristic value
A cautious estimate of a ground parameter for design, chosen with attention to the amount and quality of test data.
Consolidation
Time-dependent volume decrease of a saturated soil as excess pore water drains and effective stress rises.
Active earth pressure
The reduced horizontal pressure on a wall that has moved slightly away from the backfill.
End bearing
The part of a pile's capacity carried by pressure at its base.
Group efficiency
The ratio of the capacity of a pile group to the sum of the capacities of its individual piles.
Compensated foundation
A foundation whose excavation removes soil of about the same weight as the structure, reducing net settlement.
Middle third rule
Keeping the load resultant within the middle third of the base, e ≤ B/6, so the whole base stays in compression.
Adhesion factor
The ratio between pile shaft friction and undrained shear strength in clay, used in the form f_s = α c_u.
Worked example

Allowable bearing pressure for a square footing

A square footing 2.0 m wide is founded 1.2 m below ground level on a medium dense sand with φ′ = 30°, c′ = 0 and unit weight 17.5 kN/m³. The water table is below the failure zone. Using Terzaghi's square footing expression and a factor of safety of 3 on the ultimate capacity, find the allowable bearing pressure and the allowable column load.

  1. For a square footing, q_u = 1.3 c′ N_c + q′ N_q + 0.4 γ B N_γ; with c′ = 0 the first term vanishes.
  2. Take N_q = 22.5 and N_γ = 19.1 for φ′ = 30° from the table.
  3. Surcharge at foundation level: q′ = γ D_f = 17.5 × 1.2 = 21.0 kPa.
  4. q_u = 21.0 × 22.5 + 0.4 × 17.5 × 2.0 × 19.1 = 472 + 268 = 740 kPa.
  5. Allowable pressure: q_all = 740/3 = 247 kPa.
  6. Allowable column load: Q_all = q_all × B² = 247 × 4.0 = 988 kN.
  7. Sanity check: 247 kPa is a plausible value for medium dense sand, and the footing is narrow, so punching shear rather than general shear might govern in reality.
AnswerThe allowable bearing pressure is about 247 kPa, giving an allowable column load of about 990 kN on the 2.0 m square footing.

Exam method

  • Compute effective stresses and pore pressures before touching the bearing capacity or earth pressure equations.
  • Work in kN and metres throughout, so pressures come out in kPa without unit conversions at the end.
  • Answer both limit states: bearing, sliding or overturning at ULS, and settlement at SLS.
  • State the drainage condition — short-term undrained or long-term drained — because the parameters and the analysis both change.
  • Turn any eccentric moment into e = M/Q and compare it with B/6 before choosing a contact pressure formula; the formula changes once contact is lost.
