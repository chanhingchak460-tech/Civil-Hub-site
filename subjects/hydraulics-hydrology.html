01Two ways water flows

Open-channel flow has a free surface at atmospheric pressure; gravity drives it, and the depth can rise and fall. Pipe flow runs full, driven by a pressure gradient, with a fixed cross-section. Open-channel depth is set by the bed slope and the downstream control; a full pipe's head loss follows from discharge and roughness.

Flow is uniform when depth, area and velocity do not change along the reach — an equilibrium between gravity and friction. Gradually varied flow changes slowly, as behind a weir; rapidly varied flow changes over a few depths, as at a jump.

02Manning's equation

Manning's equation estimates mean velocity from the channel geometry, the bed slope and a roughness coefficient. It uses the hydraulic radius R = A/P — area divided by wetted perimeter — because friction acts along the perimeter. It is not a derivation but a fit to measurements.

The roughness coefficient n is empirical and absorbs vegetation and bed irregularities. Values are indicative: smooth concrete around 0.012–0.015, earth channels 0.020–0.030, vegetated streams higher. The 1/n form applies to SI units only.

Manning velocity
\[V = \frac{1}{n} R^{2/3} S^{1/2}\]
SI units; R in metres and S as a gradient

03Specific energy and critical depth

Specific energy is the head of the water relative to the channel bed: E = y + V²/(2g). At a fixed discharge per unit width q, the E–y curve is a parabola with a minimum, and for any energy above it there are two possible depths — the alternate depths. The minimum is the critical depth, at Fr = 1.

Flow is subcritical when Fr < 1 (controlled from downstream) and supercritical when Fr > 1 (controlled from upstream). A control such as a weir forces the depth to critical and fixes the upstream profile.

Specific energy
\[E = y + \frac{V^2}{2g}\]
y depth, V mean velocity
Critical depth, rectangular channel
\[y_c = \left(\frac{q^2}{g}\right)^{1/3}\]
q is discharge per unit width
Froude number
\[\mathrm{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gD}}\]
D is hydraulic depth, A/T

04The hydraulic jump

When supercritical flow meets an obstruction or a rise in tailwater, it jumps to subcritical flow across a short turbulent transition. Depth rises, velocity falls, and much of the kinetic energy is dissipated as heat — which is why jumps are built below spillways to protect the bed downstream.

The depth change is found from momentum, since the energy loss is not known in advance. For a rectangular channel the conjugate depths follow a relation in the approaching Froude number, and the loss grows steeply with jump strength. An oscillating jump is unstable and should be designed out.

Conjugate depths
\[\frac{y_2}{y_1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + 8\,\mathrm{Fr}_1^2} - 1\right)\]
rectangular channel, approaching Froude number Fr₁

05Pipe networks and friction

In a full pipe the head loss has two parts: friction, proportional to length and velocity squared, and minor losses at bends, valves and fittings, quoted through a coefficient K. The Darcy–Weisbach equation is the standard form; f comes from the Moody chart and depends on Reynolds number and relative roughness ε/D.

Networks add continuity at every junction and a single head at each node. Parallel pipes share the same head loss, so flow splits in inverse proportion to resistance; pipes in series share the same discharge and add their losses. Large networks are solved iteratively by adjusting flows until the heads close.

Darcy–Weisbach head loss
\[h_f = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{V^2}{2g}\]
f from the Moody chart

06Well hydraulics in an unconfined aquifer

Pumping a well in an unconfined aquifer creates a cone of depression: radial flow converges on the well, and Darcy's law integrated over the cone gives the discharge in terms of the water levels in two observation wells at distances r₁ and r₂:

Q = π k (h₂² − h₁²) / ln(r₂/r₁),

with k the permeability and h the saturated thickness at each well.

A pumping test therefore measures k in the ground at full scale, rather than in a small sample: pump at constant discharge until the levels stabilise, read h₁ and h₂, and solve for k. The assumptions deserve respect — horizontal radial flow, a homogeneous aquifer, steady conditions — because real drawdown takes time to reach equilibrium and layered ground violates the homogeneity quietly. The same equation, inverted, sizes a well's yield or predicts the drawdown at a neighbouring borehole, which matters when a new abstraction could steal water from an existing one.

Well discharge, unconfined aquifer
\[Q = \frac{\pi k\,(h_2^2 - h_1^2)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}\]
h₁ and h₂ are the saturated thicknesses at observation wells r₁ and r₂

07Catchment, rainfall and losses

Hydrology converts rainfall into the flow at a point. A catchment is the area that drains to that point; its response depends on area, slope, soil, land cover and storm pattern. Rainfall recorded over time is a hyetograph; the flow leaving the catchment is a hydrograph.

Only part of the rain becomes runoff. Water is intercepted, fills surface depressions and infiltrates the soil; the remainder is effective rainfall. The time of concentration is the travel time from the most remote part of the catchment to the outlet, and it sets how quickly the hydrograph can peak. Urban sealing shortens it and raises the peak.

08The rational method

For small catchments, the rational method estimates peak flow from a runoff coefficient C, a rainfall intensity i for the design duration and the catchment area A. The coefficient lumps together soil, slope and land use: values range from about 0.1 for open ground to 0.9 for impervious paving.

The method assumes the storm lasts long enough for the whole catchment to contribute, so the design intensity is taken for the time of concentration. It gives a peak flow only — no hydrograph, no volume — and it is sensitive to C and to the chosen intensity–duration–frequency curve.

Rational method
\[Q = \frac{C\,i\,A}{3.6}\]
Q in m³/s, i in mm/h and A in km²

09Unit hydrographs

A unit hydrograph is the direct runoff hydrograph produced by one unit of effective rainfall — commonly 1 mm — falling uniformly over the catchment in a stated duration. The assumption is that the catchment responds linearly: twice the rainfall gives twice the flow, and storms can be added and shifted in time by convolution.

This builds a design hydrograph: separate a recorded storm into effective rainfall blocks, apply the unit hydrograph to each block, and sum the shifted responses. The method assumes a constant baseflow and an unchanged catchment; urbanisation, storage or a different storm duration each undermine it.

Convolution of effective rainfall
\[Q(t) = \sum P(\tau)\,U(t - \tau)\]
P is the effective rainfall and U the unit hydrograph

10Return periods and design risk

A return period T is the average interval between events of a given size, not a schedule: a 1-in-100-year storm has a 1% chance of being exceeded each year. Over a 50-year design life, such an event has about a 39% chance of occurring at least once.

Design storms come from an intensity–duration–frequency curve for the chosen duration and return period; climate change makes the historical curve a starting point, not a guarantee.

Risk over n years
\[R = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{T}\right)^n\]
annual exceedance probability is p = 1/T

Key terms

Froude number
The ratio of flow velocity to the speed of a shallow-water wave; below 1 the flow is subcritical, above 1 supercritical.
Hydraulic radius
Flow area divided by wetted perimeter, used as the length scale in Manning's equation.
Critical depth
The depth at which specific energy is minimum and the Froude number equals one.
Hydraulic jump
A rapid transition from supercritical to subcritical flow in which depth rises and energy is dissipated.
Time of concentration
The travel time for water from the most remote point of a catchment to reach the outlet.
Unit hydrograph
The direct runoff hydrograph from one unit of effective rainfall falling uniformly over a catchment in a stated duration.
Return period
The average interval between events that equal or exceed a given magnitude, equal to the reciprocal of the annual exceedance probability.
Cone of depression
The funnel-shaped lowering of the water table around a pumped well, over which radial flow converges.
Chezy coefficient
A roughness-dependent velocity coefficient in the Chezy equation, linked to Manning's n by C = R^(1/6)/n.
Worked example

Uniform flow in a rectangular channel

A rectangular channel 3.0 m wide carries water at a uniform depth of 1.2 m on a bed slope of 0.001. Manning's n is 0.015. Find the discharge, the mean velocity and the Froude number, and state whether the flow is subcritical or supercritical.

  1. Flow area A = b y = 3.0 × 1.2 = 3.6 m². Wetted perimeter P = b + 2y = 3.0 + 2.4 = 5.4 m.
  2. Hydraulic radius R = A/P = 3.6/5.4 = 0.667 m, so R^(2/3) = 0.763.
  3. Velocity: V = (1/0.015)(0.763)(0.001^(1/2)) = (66.67)(0.763)(0.0316) = 1.61 m/s.
  4. Discharge: Q = A V = 3.6 × 1.61 = 5.79 m³/s.
  5. Hydraulic depth D = A/T = 3.6/3.0 = 1.2 m, so Fr = V/√(gD) = 1.61/√(9.81 × 1.2) = 1.61/3.43 = 0.47.
  6. Sanity check: q = 5.79/3.0 = 1.93 m²/s gives y_c = (1.93²/9.81)^(1/3) = 0.724 m. The actual depth of 1.2 m is above critical, consistent with subcritical flow.
AnswerQ ≈ 5.79 m³/s, V ≈ 1.61 m/s and Fr ≈ 0.47, so the flow is subcritical. The depth is comfortably above the critical depth of 0.72 m, which places the reach on a mild slope.

Exam method

  • Write Manning's equation with units beside R and S; the 1/n form is for SI only.
  • Classify the flow first: the Froude number and whether the reach is uniform or varied decide which equations apply.
  • Keep peak-flow methods and hydrograph methods apart: the rational method gives a peak, a unit hydrograph gives a shape.
  • Treat a return period as a probability; state the risk over the design life, not just T.
  • For pipe flow, identify the regime first — laminar uses f = 64/Re, turbulent needs the Moody chart — before any head-loss arithmetic.
