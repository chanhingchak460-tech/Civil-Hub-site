Keeping built assets useful over decades: registers, condition assessment, deterioration, maintenance strategy, risk, whole-life cost and the digital tools that hold the data together. It is engineering economics applied to things that already exist.

01Assets and the register

An asset is a physical component that delivers a service: a bridge, a pumping station, a pipe, a sign. Managing them starts with an asset register, a structured inventory giving every item an identifier, location, attributes, condition, criticality, value and history.

  • Hierarchy matters — network, facility, component — because decisions are made at different levels.
  • A register is only useful if current, so ownership and update rules matter more than the software.

02Condition assessment

Condition is judged by inspection: routine visual checks, detailed inspections, and special investigations after damage. Inspectors record distress against a defined scale, such as 1 to 5, with photographs and measurements; a rating only means something if everyone applies the same definitions.

  • Non-destructive tests — cover meters, half-cell potential, ultrasonic thickness — add evidence without opening the structure.
  • Sampling is inevitable, so results are estimates with a confidence range, not facts.

03Deterioration

Assets decline in different ways. Corrosion, fatigue, abrasion, settlement and weathering all depend on age, loading, environment and maintenance history, so two identical components can age very differently.

  • Deterioration models are probabilistic: a Markov model gives the probability of moving from one condition state to another in a period.
  • Survival curves describe the fraction still serviceable at each age.
  • Models support timing decisions, but they are only as good as the inspection data behind them.
Markov condition transition
\[C_{t+1} = C_t P\]
C is the distribution over condition states and P the transition probability matrix, estimated from inspection records

04Reliability, availability and failure rates

Reliability asks how long an asset works before it fails. For a fleet, mean time between failures is total running time divided by the number of failures, and its inverse is the failure rate. Availability combines reliability with repairability through mean time to repair: an asset that fails rarely but takes months to fix can be less available than one that fails often and is repaired in hours.

  • Components in series multiply their availabilities: ten links at 99% each is at best about 90% available — parallel redundancy buys the extra nines.
  • Quote MTBF with its maintenance context, because a design change or deferred servicing moves it more than the equipment itself does.
Mean time between failures
\[\mathrm{MTBF} = \frac{\text{operating time}}{\text{failures}}\]
a fleet measure: total running hours divided by the number of failures
Failure rate
\[\lambda = \frac{1}{\mathrm{MTBF}}\]
failures per unit time; the reciprocal of MTBF
Availability
\[A = \frac{\mathrm{MTBF}}{\mathrm{MTBF} + \mathrm{MTTR}}\]
MTTR is mean time to repair; express both in the same unit
Series availability
\[A = A_1 A_2 \cdots A_n\]
a chain of dependent links multiplies its availabilities, so long chains need redundancy

05Maintenance strategies

Maintenance ranges from reactive (fix it when it breaks) through planned preventive (service on a calendar) and condition-based (act when inspection shows a threshold) to predictive (act when data suggests failure is approaching). The options for an asset are usually do nothing, repair, rehabilitate or replace.

  • Reactive work is cheap until a failure closes a road or a water main.
  • Preventive work is predictable but may replace healthy parts.
  • Condition-based work needs reliable monitoring.

06Level of service and performance measurement

Assets are funded to deliver a level of service, so management needs measures that describe the service, not the asset. For a road: travel time, roughness and closures; for water: pressure, interruptions and response time. A performance indicator turns raw data into a comparable number, and a target turns it into a decision.

  • Customer minutes lost multiplies incidents by duration and customers affected.
  • Backlog is the share of the network below its condition target.
  • Report indicators with their trend, not just this year's number — a network can look fine this quarter while the backlog quietly compounds.
Customer minutes lost
\[\mathrm{CML} = n \times d \times c\]
weights disruption by scale and length; compare it against the service target

07Risk and prioritisation

Money is always short, so work is prioritised by risk. Risk = likelihood × consequence, with likelihood informed by condition and deterioration, and consequence measured in safety, service disruption, cost, environment and reputation.

  • A risk matrix sorts assets into bands.
  • A criticality ranking protects the components whose failure matters most.
  • Resilience adds a network view: a single pipe may be low risk alone but be the only supply route for a hospital.
Risk score
\[R = L \times C\]
score likelihood and consequence on defined scales before multiplying

08Multi-criteria decision analysis

Ranking assets by risk answers what to fix, but not always what to build: options often differ on cost, safety, environment, disruption and public preference at once. Multi-criteria analysis scores each option on weighted criteria, summing score times weight into one comparable number, with weights agreed before the scores are known so the exercise cannot be steered.

  • A benefit–cost ratio folds the same logic into economics: benefits divided by costs, where benefits include time saved, accidents avoided and emissions cut, valued explicitly.
  • Both methods are transparent rather than objective — the value sits in the stated weights and valuations, so always show them and test how the ranking changes when they move.
Weighted score
\[S = \sum w_i s_i\]
weights sum to 1; score every option on every criterion before weighting
Benefit–cost ratio
\[\mathrm{BCR} = \frac{\mathrm{PV}_{\mathrm{benefits}}}{\mathrm{PV}_{\mathrm{costs}}}\]
both streams discounted to present value; a ratio above 1 passes the economic test

09Whole-life cost

Whole-life costing compares options over the asset's life, not just the capital price: construction, operation, maintenance, renewal and disposal, discounted to present value. Net present value converts future costs at a chosen discount rate.

  • When options have different lives, an equivalent annual cost puts them on a common annual basis.
  • The discount rate is a policy choice and dominates long-horizon results, so test its sensitivity rather than treating the answer as exact.
Net present value
\[\mathrm{NPV} = \sum \frac{C_t}{(1 + i)^t}\]
C_t is the net cost in year t and i the discount rate
Equivalent annual cost
\[\mathrm{EUAC} = P (A/P, i, n) - S (A/F, i, n) + A\]
P capital, S salvage, (A/P) capital recovery factor, (A/F) sinking fund factor

10Renewal timing and the bathtub curve

Most assets follow the bathtub curve: an early period of infant failures, a long useful life at a low, roughly constant failure rate, then a wear-out phase where the rate climbs.

  • Fix defects early under warranty, monitor through the flat middle, and renew before wear-out makes failures frequent and repair expensive.
  • The optimal renewal point is where the annualised cost of keeping the asset beats the annualised cost of replacing it — the same equivalent annual cost arithmetic, re-run as the asset ages.
  • Renewing early wastes remaining life; renewing late pays in unplanned outages, so the timing decision is economic, not sentimental.
Discount rates drive the answer Long-horizon comparisons can flip when the discount rate changes. Repeat the calculation at a lower and a higher rate before recommending an option.

11BIM, GIS and the digital asset

GIS answers where: location, network connectivity, catchment and proximity. BIM answers what: geometry, components, properties and relationships. Together they carry the register into a digital model that can be queried, simulated and updated.

  • Asset information should be handed over at practical completion, not reconstructed years later, which is why information requirements are specified early.
  • The hard parts are not the models but interoperability, data ownership and keeping the twin current.

Key terms

Asset register
A structured inventory recording each asset's identifier, location, attributes, condition, criticality and history.
Condition rating
A score on a defined scale that records the severity of observed deterioration, comparable only if the scale is applied consistently.
Criticality
A ranking of how much an asset's failure matters, combining consequence with the likelihood of failure.
Whole-life cost
The total discounted cost of an asset from construction through operation and renewal to disposal.
Equivalent annual cost
A capital and operating cost converted to an equal annual amount, allowing options with different lives to be compared.
Markov deterioration model
A probabilistic model of condition moving between states over time according to transition probabilities.
Availability
The fraction of time an asset is able to perform its service, combining failure frequency with repair time.
Level of service
A defined statement of the service an asset network must deliver, expressed in measurable indicators with targets.
Worked example

Comparing two renewal options by annual cost

Two pumping options deliver the same service. Option A costs 1,200,000 to install, lasts 8 years, has a salvage value of 100,000 and needs 20,000 a year in maintenance. Option B costs 800,000, lasts 5 years, has a salvage value of 60,000 and needs 35,000 a year. Using a 6% discount rate, which option has the lower equivalent annual cost?

  1. For Option A, the capital recovery factor (A/P, 6%, 8) = 0.1610 and the sinking fund factor (A/F, 6%, 8) = 0.1010.
  2. Annualise A's capital and salvage: 1,200,000 × 0.1610 − 100,000 × 0.1010 = 193,200 − 10,100 = 183,100.
  3. Add annual maintenance: EUAC_A = 183,100 + 20,000 = 203,100 per year.
  4. For Option B, (A/P, 6%, 5) = 0.2374 and (A/F, 6%, 5) = 0.1774, so 800,000 × 0.2374 − 60,000 × 0.1774 = 189,920 − 10,644 = 179,276.
  5. Add maintenance: EUAC_B = 179,276 + 35,000 = 214,300 per year, about 11,200 more than Option A.
AnswerOption A has the lower equivalent annual cost, about 203,100 against 214,300, despite its higher capital price, because it lasts longer and costs less to maintain. The conclusion should be tested at other discount rates, since the ranking may change.

Exam method

  • In economic comparison questions, convert every option to the same basis — present value or annual cost — before comparing anything.
  • State the consequence categories when ranking risk; a bare 'high risk' label without reasoning earns little credit.
  • For deterioration questions, name the model's assumptions, especially the transition probabilities and the inspection interval.
  • For availability questions, never mix units: express MTBF and MTTR in the same unit before dividing, and state the answer as a fraction of time.
