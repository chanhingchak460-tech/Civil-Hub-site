How projects are organised so that what is built matches what was specified: quality systems, inspection plans, non-conformance, prefabrication, model coordination and handover. It is the professional layer that holds the technical work together.

01Quality is planned, not inspected in

A quality management system is the set of responsibilities, procedures and records that make quality repeatable rather than accidental. It runs on a process loop: agree what is required, plan how it will be achieved, do the work, check the result against the specification, and act on the gap.

  • Quality assurance is prevention — method statements, mock-ups, training, supplier assessment, design checks.
  • Quality control is detection — inspection, testing and measurement against acceptance criteria.
  • A project leaning entirely on control is paying to find its own mistakes.
  • The cost of quality is how that trade-off becomes visible.

02The inspection and test plan

An inspection and test plan (ITP) lists, for each activity, what is checked, against which acceptance criterion, by whom, at what frequency, and what record proves it happened. It is written before work starts and follows the construction sequence, so it doubles as a quality programme.

  • Hold points: work cannot continue until the inspection is passed and signed.
  • Witness points: the inspector is invited but the work may proceed.
  • Writing an ITP is an exercise in risk: hold the points that would be expensive or unsafe to discover late, and witness the rest.

03Inspection, testing and non-conformance

Inspection follows the work: receiving inspection on delivered material, in-process inspection during construction, and final inspection before handover. Testing may be destructive (cubes, cores) or non-destructive (covermeter, rebound hammer, ultrasonic, half-cell potential), and a result means little without its method and sampling plan.

When something fails, the response is a non-conformance report:
- contain the item
- record the facts
- agree a disposition (rework, repair, accept by concession, or reject)
- find the root cause
- close the corrective action

Treating each report as an isolated complaint hides the pattern; grouping them by trade or location is where the improvement sits.

04The seven basic quality tools

Seven simple tools do most of the analysis a quality engineer needs.

  • Check sheet collects counts as events happen.
  • Histogram shows the spread of a measurement.
  • Pareto chart ranks defect causes by frequency so the vital few are tackled first.
  • Cause-and-effect diagram traces an effect back through materials, methods, people, plant and environment.
  • Scatter diagram tests whether two variables move together.
  • Control chart separates common-cause variation from special causes over time.
  • Flow chart maps the process so it can be improved.

The tools are cheap because they run on honesty: they only work when data is collected at the point of work, labelled with the process that produced it, and then acted on. A Pareto chart of defects that nobody categorised is a picture, not a decision.

The seven tools and the question each answers
ToolQuestion it answers
Check sheetHow often does each event occur?
HistogramHow is the measurement distributed?
Pareto chartWhich few causes produce most of the defects?
Cause-and-effect diagramWhat could be driving this effect?
Scatter diagramDo two variables move together?
Control chartIs the process stable or out of control?
Flow chartWhat are the actual process steps?

05Process capability and control limits

A process in control is not automatically capable. Control limits describe what the process is doing; specification limits describe what the customer will accept. Plotting sample means against control limits reveals drift and special causes, while capability indices compare the natural spread with the tolerance.

  • Cp asks whether the spread fits the tolerance at all.
  • Cpk also asks whether the process is centred.
  • A Cp above 1.33 with a Cpk near 0.6 means the process is consistent but badly aimed — adjust the setting, not the inspection frequency.

Sampling plans then decide how many items to test for the confidence required, which is why acceptance criteria always pair a method with a sampling rate.

Process capability
\[C_p = \frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mathrm{LSL}}{6\sigma}\]
USL and LSL are the specification limits and σ the process standard deviation
Centred capability
\[C_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mu}{3\sigma},\, \frac{\mu - \mathrm{LSL}}{3\sigma}\right)\]
the smaller side rules; centring matters as much as spread
Control limits for sample means
\[\bar{x} \pm \frac{3\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]
for samples of size n; a point outside suggests a special cause

06MiC and DfMA move quality into the factory

Modular integrated construction (MiC) and design for manufacture and assembly (DfMA) shift work from site into a controlled factory. Modules arrive with finishes, services and sometimes fittings installed, so the quality problem changes shape: repetition improves consistency and makes inspection easier, but design must freeze earlier, tolerances must stack up honestly, and the interface between module and site structure becomes the new risk.

Quality then depends on jigs, mock-ups, factory inspection plans, digital records and traceability, plus protection during transport and lifting. On site the work is connection, sealing and alignment, and those few operations carry a disproportionate share of the risk.

07BIM coordination and the common data environment

BIM is only useful if the information is trustworthy. Coordination federates discipline models, runs clash detection and resolves interfaces before they become site arguments; models also support quantities, sequencing and setting out.

Information is exchanged through a common data environment (CDE), with clear states — work in progress, shared, published and archived — plus naming, revision and status codes. An unauthorised model must never be built from. At handover the model becomes an asset information model, so the data that operations will need has to be requested at the start, not reconstructed at the end.

08BIM data quality and digital QA/QC

Digital QA/QC treats the model like any other deliverable.

  • Visual check inspects geometry and presentation.
  • Integrity check confirms properties are defined, sensible and unique.
  • Rule-based checks verify that the required information exists for each agreed purpose.

Naming and status codes make the checks automatable — an unnamed file cannot be checked against its deliverable. For modular work the same discipline follows the module: QR-coded identifiers, factory photos at each hold point, sensor readings of curing or transport conditions, and a digital record linked to the model. Traceability is the point: when a defect appears on site, the record should identify the module, its batch, its inspector and its test results in one query, not a week of archive hunting.

Checks run on a schedule Model checks are scheduled before each information exchange and at the end of each work period, not performed once at the end of the project.

09Stages, roles and handover

Projects move through brief, feasibility and design, tender, construction, commissioning, handover and operation. Each stage ends with an information drop that the next stage depends on, which is why late changes are so expensive: they invalidate work already checked.

Roles are separated deliberately:
- The client sets requirements.
- The project manager controls time, cost and coordination.
- Designers write the specification.
- The contractor plans and builds.
- The supervisor or clerk of works inspects independently.
- The quantity surveyor controls cost.

Handover needs a complete pack — as-built records, test certificates, warranties, operating manuals and training — and the defects liability period is where poor workmanship returns as cost.

10Contracts, records and handover evidence

Quality obligations live in the contract as much as in the specification. Who owns the model data and its intellectual property, who may rely on shared information, and what happens when a checked deliverable turns out wrong must be stated in the appointment documents, not discovered after a dispute. Clear drafting keeps the model a tool for coordination instead of a source of claims.

Records are evidence: inspection sheets, test certificates, delivery checklists and photographs should be named, dated, linked to the element and retrievable at handover. A complete, structured record pack protects both parties and is itself a deliverable. Where the contract is silent, the CDE rules and the responsibility matrix become the de facto contract — write them as carefully.

11Programme reliability and daily discipline

Quality is a schedule problem as much as a technical one. A weekly plan that names tasks, owners and dates, a look-ahead for the next few weeks, and a daily report of what was actually done form a feedback loop.

Percent plan complete measures how much promised work was finished as planned; a low value predicts disruption, rework and a rushed finish. Risk is managed the same way: log it, score it, assign an owner, review it. A risk register written once and never reopened is decoration.

Cost of quality
CoQ = prevention + appraisal + failure
Percent plan complete
\[\mathrm{PPC} = \frac{\text{completed tasks}}{\text{planned tasks}} \times 100\%\]
Risk score
\[\text{risk} = \text{likelihood} \times \text{consequence}\]

Key terms

Inspection and test plan
A document setting out what will be checked, against what criterion, when, by whom and with what record.
Hold point
A stage in an inspection plan where work may not continue until a documented inspection is passed.
Non-conformance report
A formal record of work that fails to meet the specification, with its disposition and corrective action.
Common data environment
The single agreed digital source for project information, with controlled status and revision states.
DfMA
Design for manufacture and assembly: designing components so they can be made in a factory and assembled efficiently on site.
Cost of quality
The combined cost of prevention, appraisal and failure, used to judge how much inspection a project really needs.
Percent plan complete
The share of planned tasks actually completed as planned in a period, used as a measure of programme reliability.
Pareto chart
A bar chart ranking causes by frequency, used to find the vital few defects worth attacking first.
Process capability index
A ratio comparing the tolerance width with the natural process spread, with Cpk penalising poor centring.
Worked example

Cost of quality on a modular project

A volumetric modular project with 300 modules has a construction budget of HK$96 million. Twelve modules are rejected at the factory and reworked at HK$9,500 each before delivery. Inspection and testing costs HK$180,000, and prevention activities such as mock-ups, training and factory audits cost HK$120,000. Find the cost of quality, its share of the budget, and the proportion attributable to failure.

  1. The rejected modules were found before handover, so the cost is internal failure: 12 × HK$9,500 = HK$114,000.
  2. Prevention plus appraisal = HK$120,000 + HK$180,000 = HK$300,000.
  3. Cost of quality = prevention + appraisal + failure = HK$300,000 + HK$114,000 = HK$414,000.
  4. As a share of the budget: 414,000 / 96,000,000 = 0.0043, or about 0.43%.
  5. Failure share = 114,000 / 414,000 = 27.5%, while prevention is 29.0% of the total.
  6. Sanity check: the same defects discovered after handover would be external failure, carrying repair, access and liability costs, so the 0.43% is a floor rather than a true worst case.
AnswerCost of quality is HK$414,000, about 0.43% of the construction budget, of which 27.5% is failure cost. The ratio suggests more value may lie in prevention than in finding rejects at the factory gate.

Exam method

  • Learn the difference between assurance and control, and be ready to give a project example of each.
  • For an ITP question, always state the acceptance criterion and the record, not just the activity name.
  • In a non-conformance question, separate containment, disposition and root cause; marks are usually allocated per stage.
  • Use the cost-of-quality categories when asked why prevention is cheaper than repair, and quote the failure share.
  • When quoting Cp and Cpk, state what each measures: Cp tests spread against tolerance, Cpk adds centring — a high Cp with a low Cpk means shift the process, not the limits.
