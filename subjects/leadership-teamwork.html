How people actually work together: communication loops, empathy and I-statements, the stages every team passes through, and the feedback that keeps conflict useful. Engineering projects are delivered by teams, not by calculations.

01Communication is the work

On a project, most failures are not calculations. They are information failures: a drawing revision nobody opened, a risk raised in a meeting but never recorded, a worker who misunderstood the sequence.

Communication is a technical skill, not a personality trait. A sender encodes a message, chooses a channel, a receiver decodes it, and feedback closes the loop. Noise — jargon, a noisy site, a rushed message — enters at every stage.

A one-way broadcast with no feedback is not communication; it is an announcement.

Communication loop
Sender → Message → Channel → Receiver → Feedback
noise acts at every stage until feedback proves otherwise

02Empathy and I-statements

Empathy is the attempt to understand another person's position before responding to it. It is usually split into:

  • affective empathy (feeling a response to emotion)
  • cognitive empathy (working out what someone is thinking)
  • compassionate empathy (being moved to help)

In engineering teams the cognitive kind does most of the work: it predicts where misunderstanding occurs.

When something goes wrong the temptation is a you-statement such as "you keep sending late drawings". It assigns blame and invites defence. An I-statement describes the behaviour, the feeling and the concrete impact, and leaves the other person room to act.

I-statement
When [behaviour], I feel [feeling] because [impact].
describe the behaviour, not the person's character

03Teams pass through stages

Teams rarely become effective by announcement. They pass through recognisable stages, and the leader's job changes with each one.

At first people are polite. Disagreement follows, and that is normal: roles, standards and authority have to be negotiated. Only then can shared habits settle.

The practical lesson is to expect the dip. A team that argues in week three is often healthier than one that stays agreeable and never tests an idea.

04Roles and social loafing

A group is not automatically a team. Teams work when each person has a clear, distinct contribution and everyone can see who is doing what.

When responsibilities blur, social loafing appears: individuals reduce effort because the link between their work and the outcome is invisible.

  • Give every task one named owner and a visible status.
  • Make contributions reviewable.
  • Rotate the role of challenger so that disagreement becomes a duty rather than a risk.

None of this survives without psychological safety — the shared belief that raising a problem will not be punished.

05Conflict and feedback

Conflict about the work is often productive; conflict about people is corrosive. The aim is not to remove disagreement but to keep it on evidence and consequences.

Constructive feedback describes what happened, what effect it had and what to do differently, without labels. The SBI pattern keeps it observable: situation, behaviour, impact.

"In yesterday's briefing the pile locations were not mentioned, so the crew arrived at the wrong area" is something a person can change. "You are careless" is not. Timing matters as much as wording: feedback given close to the event, in private, is heard; feedback saved for an annual review is a verdict.

SBI feedback
Situation + Behaviour + Impact + next step
observable facts only, no labels

06Leadership without a title

Situational leadership matches the amount of direction and support to the person and the task: a new task needs clear instruction, a familiar task needs delegation.

Transformational leadership works through purpose and example; transactional leadership works through clear exchange. Most leaders mix both.

Engineers often lead without authority — coordinating a consultant, chasing a subcontractor, persuading a client. Influence then rests on reliability, preparation and the ability to explain a decision in the other person's terms. That is why leadership and communication are hard to separate.

07Task roles and relationship roles

A team needs two kinds of contribution:

  • Task roles move the work: the organiser who structures it, the challenger who tests it, the finisher who lands it.
  • Relationship roles keep the people working: the encourager, the harmoniser, the member who notices a quiet colleague and invites them in.

One person can carry both, but no team survives on one type alone. All task roles and no relationship roles produces a competent team that fragments under stress; the reverse produces a friendly team that never finishes.

Watch which roles are missing and adopt them deliberately.

Role balance
Task roles (organiser · challenger · finisher) + Relationship roles (encourager · harmoniser · gatekeeper)
a healthy team carries both

08Meetings that decide

A meeting without a decision is a discussion, and teams have too many of them.

  • Send an agenda beforehand; it names the purpose, the items and who owns each.
  • Appoint a chair, a timekeeper and a minute-taker.
  • Agree the decision rule — consensus, majority or the chair decides — before the first item.
  • Close every item with a decision, and every decision with an owner and a date.

Minutes record those two facts, not a transcript of opinions. A decision with no owner is a suggestion; a decision with no date is a wish.

Decision record
Decision = Option chosen + Owner + Deadline
written in the minutes and circulated within a day

09Motivation and delegation

People stay engaged when the work is meaningful, the progress visible and the contribution recognised. Motivation is less about speeches than about removing the barriers — unclear scope, missing tools, invisible credit — that drain effort.

Delegation hands over a task with the authority to decide and the support to recover. State the outcome, not the method; agree checkpoints; and debrief so the next delegation can be bigger.

Delegating the work while keeping every decision is not delegation — it is queueing.

Delegation package
Outcome + Authority + Support + Checkpoint
state the outcome, not the method

10Group decisions and groupthink

Groups can decide worse than their members would alone. Groupthink is the drive for agreement that silences doubt: the team rewards consensus, suppresses criticism and dismisses outside warnings. It shows up as overconfidence, stereotyping outsiders and self-appointed censors of bad news.

Counter it with structure:

  • assign a devil's advocate before the debate
  • hold a silent round of written opinions before discussion
  • invite an outsider to test the plan

The goal is not endless dissent but decisions that have survived a real challenge.

Groupthink red flags Unanimity that arrived suspiciously fast, self-censorship ("I won't raise it, everyone agrees") and dismissing outside criticism without examining it.

Worked example

Opening a difficult conversation with a teammate

A teammate has missed two promised deadlines for a shared analysis. The next task is due in a week and the rest of the team is waiting. Draft the opening of a conversation that is honest, specific and likely to keep the team working.

  1. Write down the observable facts with dates and deliverables, and no interpretation of motive.
  2. State the impact concretely: the downstream task was delayed and two people were redeployed while waiting.
  3. Use an I-statement to own your concern, then ask an open question before proposing anything.
  4. Agree one specific change, a checkpoint, and what happens if it slips — then record it.
AnswerAn I-statement opening: "When the two analysis sections arrived after the agreed dates, I could not start the comparison, and two people were redeployed while waiting. I want to understand what got in the way. Can we agree the remaining sections by Thursday, with a midday checkpoint and a plan B if they slip?"

Exam method

  • Describe behaviour and impact rather than intent; intent is invisible and arguing about it wastes the conversation.
  • Name the stage a team is in before prescribing a fix — a storming team needs decision rules, not more encouragement.
  • In case questions, separate the individual, the team and the system when diagnosing a communication failure.
  • Give feedback in private and close to the event; public correction usually produces defence, not learning.
  • In a case about a failing team, name both the task gap and the relationship gap — most answers stop at the task.
