Learning from mobility data: regression for speeds, classification for road signs, and neural networks that must generalise. The page covers the training loop, honest evaluation, and the privacy questions that come with location records.

01What a mobility model predicts

Smart mobility turns sensor records into forecasts: link speed in fifteen minutes, the class of a road sign in a camera frame, the path a pedestrian will take next.

The first decision is the task type, because it fixes the labels, the loss function and the evaluation.

  • Supervised learning: every example pairs a feature vector x with a known target y.
  • Regression: predicts a number.
  • Classification: predicts a category.
  • Unsupervised methods find structure without targets, such as clustering similar journeys.

02Regression: fitting a line to movement

A linear model predicts a target as a weighted sum of inputs, ŷ = w · x + b. Training chooses weights that make predictions close to the observed values, usually by minimising the mean squared error.

Squaring punishes large misses hardest — sensible for speed forecasts, where wild errors matter most.

The loss is a surface in weight space. Gradient descent steps downhill, using the derivative for direction and a learning rate for step size.

Linear regression model
\[\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\]
x features, w weights, b bias
Mean squared error
\[\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\]
n training examples
Gradient descent update
\[w \leftarrow w - \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\]
η is the learning rate

03Classification, labels and the split

A classifier outputs a score for each class, converted into a probability by a sigmoid for two classes or a softmax across several. The predicted label is the highest score, and the surface where two scores tie is the decision boundary.

Before any tuning, hold back a test set that the model never sees. A model scored on its own training rows is only being checked for memory.

For small datasets, cross-validation rotates the holdout so that every row is tested once.

04Overfitting is the default failure

Training loss almost always falls as capacity grows; validation loss is the honest signal. When validation loss bottoms out and then rises while training loss keeps falling, the model has begun to fit noise.

That gap is overfitting; a model too simple for the pattern is underfitting, and both losses stay high.

Controls include:
- fewer features or layers
- regularisation that penalises large weights
- augmentation that enlarges the effective dataset
- early stopping at the validation minimum

Regularised loss
\[L = \mathrm{loss} + \lambda \sum w_i^2\]
λ sets the strength of the penalty

05Neural networks: stacked transformations

A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias and passes the result through a non-linear activation such as ReLU. Stacking layers lets the network build its own features: early layers detect simple patterns, later ones combine them.

Backpropagation applies the chain rule to find how each weight affected the loss, and an optimiser updates all weights together. Mini-batches trade noisy gradients for speed, and one pass over the data is an epoch.

Without non-linearity, stacked linear layers collapse into one linear map.

Neuron output
\[a = f(Wx + b)\]
f is a non-linear activation such as ReLU

06Convolutional networks for road signs

A camera image is a grid of pixels, and flattening it destroys neighbourhood structure. A convolutional layer slides small learnable filters across the image, sharing weights at every position, so an edge detector learnt in one corner works in every corner. Pooling compresses the feature map and adds tolerance to small shifts.

Road-sign datasets are often small and imbalanced, so transfer learning is standard:
- start from a network trained on a large image collection
- freeze the early layers
- retrain the final layer for the sign classes

Augmentation — rotation, brightness and crop — widens the training set.

Convolution output size
\[n_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{in}} - k + 2p}{s} + 1\]
k filter size, p padding, s stride

07Evaluation that matches the decision

Accuracy misleads when classes are imbalanced: a detector that never predicts the rare give-way sign can still score 95%.

A confusion matrix separates the error types, and precision and recall score them differently — precision asks how many detections were right, recall how many real signs were found.

For speed, report MAE in the data's units and RMSE, which penalises big misses; MAPE explodes near zero speeds. State the test set, the baseline and the per-class results.

08Mobility data is personal data

A trajectory identifies a person once it is joined to a home or a workplace. Aggregate before release, strip identifiers from the pipeline, and treat fine-grained location as sensitive by default.

Training data also carries bias: cameras cover some roads better than others, and labels record who was observed rather than who was there. A model taught to imitate enforcement patterns will reproduce them.

Document the data's provenance, keep a human in the loop, and monitor deployed predictions for drift and unequal error, not just average accuracy.

09Object detection: boxes and IoU

Detecting a road sign means predicting a bounding box and a class for it. Two boxes rarely coincide exactly, so detections are scored by the intersection over union: the shared area divided by the combined area.

A prediction counts as a hit when its IoU with the ground-truth box exceeds a threshold, commonly 0.5. Counting hits per class gives average precision, and the mean over classes is the headline detector metric.

Report the IoU threshold beside it — a detector that scores well at 0.5 can collapse at 0.75, and the stricter threshold is the one that matters for safety.

Intersection over union
\[\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{\mathrm{area}(A \cap B)}{\mathrm{area}(A \cup B)}\]
between 0 and 1; above about 0.5 the prediction matches the ground truth

10Sequence models for trajectory prediction

A trajectory is a sequence of positions, and the past steps are the features. Temporal convolutional networks use causal convolutions with growing dilation so a layer can see far into the history without an explosion of parameters; transformer encoders instead attend directly across time steps. Both map the observed path into a hidden state that a final layer turns into the next positions.

Train with a loss suited to the data: plain mean absolute error is robust, and the Smooth L1 loss behaves like L1 for large errors but like squared error near zero, giving smooth gradients during fine adjustment while resisting outliers.

Evaluate on held-out scenes with MAE and RMSE in metres.

Smooth L1 loss
\[L(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5 x^2 & |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]
x is the prediction error; quadratic near zero, linear far away
Dilated convolution
\[\text{dilation } d: \quad y_t = \sum_k w_k x_{t - dk}\]
stacking layers with d = 1, 2, 4, … widens the receptive field

11Fine-tuning a pretrained detector

Mobility datasets are rarely large enough to train a detector from scratch, so the standard route is transfer learning: start from a backbone pretrained on a large general image collection, swap its classification head for the new classes, and train only that head while the early layers stay frozen. Frozen layers keep their learned features and receive no gradient updates.

Once the head converges, unfreeze the later backbone layers at a much smaller learning rate so the features adapt without wrecking what was learned.

Augmentation — horizontal flips, colour jitter, resizing and normalisation — expands the effective dataset and teaches invariance. Preprocess test images with the same pipeline as training, or the model sees a different data distribution than it learned.

Freeze first, unfreeze late Train the new head with the backbone frozen, then unfreeze with a learning rate about ten times smaller; jumping straight to full fine-tuning often destroys useful features.

Key terms

Supervised learning
Learning a mapping from labelled examples, where each input is paired with the correct output.
Overfitting
Fitting the noise in the training data so closely that performance on unseen data falls.
Validation set
Data held aside from training and used to compare model choices before the final test.
Convolutional layer
A layer that applies shared learnable filters across an image to build translation-tolerant features.
Transfer learning
Reusing a model trained on a large dataset as the starting point for a smaller, related task.
Recall
The share of real positive cases that the model successfully finds.
Intersection over union
The shared area between a predicted and a ground-truth box divided by their union, the standard match measure in object detection.
Fine-tuning
Continuing to train a pretrained model on a new, related dataset, often with early layers frozen.
Worked example

Error on a held-out speed forecast

A model predicts link speed in km/h at five successive intervals. Observed speeds are 60, 55, 70, 65 and 80; predicted speeds are 58, 58, 66, 65 and 74. Find the MAE and RMSE, and compare both with a baseline that always predicts the training mean of 66 km/h.

  1. Errors (observed − predicted): 2, −3, 4, 0 and 6 km/h.
  2. MAE = (2 + 3 + 4 + 0 + 6) / 5 = 15 / 5 = 3.0 km/h.
  3. Squared errors: 4, 9, 16, 0 and 36, summing to 65. RMSE = √(65/5) = √13 ≈ 3.6 km/h.
  4. Baseline errors from 66 km/h: −6, −11, 4, −1 and 14, so its MAE = 36/5 = 7.2 km/h.
  5. Baseline RMSE = √((36 + 121 + 16 + 1 + 196)/5) = √74 ≈ 8.6 km/h.
  6. Sanity check: the model beats the baseline on both metrics, and RMSE exceeds MAE because the 6 km/h miss is weighted heavily.
AnswerMAE = 3.0 km/h and RMSE ≈ 3.6 km/h, against baseline values of 7.2 km/h and 8.6 km/h. The forecast is better than the baseline on this sample, but five intervals are too few to claim a general improvement.

Exam method

  • Name the task first: regression and classification need different losses and different metrics.
  • Report each test-set metric with its units and a baseline; accuracy alone rarely answers the question asked.
  • For an imbalanced class, quote precision and recall rather than overall accuracy.
  • When fine-tuning, freeze the backbone for the first passes and drop the learning rate by about ten times when you unfreeze it.
