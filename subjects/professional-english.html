Writing and speaking for engineering practice: reports, executive summaries, emails and presentations that a busy reader can act on. It is the difference between work that is done and work that is understood.

01Write for the reader's job

A professional document exists to change what a reader knows or does. Before drafting, answer three questions: who is reading, what they need to decide or do, and what the one main message is.

Write the purpose in the first two lines, keep one idea per paragraph, and lead each paragraph with its point rather than its background. A busy reader should not have to reconstruct your logic.

The reader's job is the test:
- A checking engineer needs the calculation and its assumptions.
- A client needs the outcome, the cost and the decision required.

Planning frame
Purpose → Audience → Action → Tone
decide before writing

02The shape of a technical report

Reports follow a predictable order because readers use them differently. A common spine is context and objective, method, evidence, interpretation, and conclusions with recommendations.

  • The introduction says why the work was done and what it covers.
  • The method is repeatable.
  • Findings come before explanation.
  • Recommendations are specific and assigned.

Signposting helps: headings, numbering and a short opening sentence in each section tell the reader what the section does. Appendices hold raw data and long calculations so that the body stays readable.

Report spine
Context → Method → Evidence → Interpretation → Action
order of the body

03Executive summaries earn the meeting

Many readers will read the executive summary first, and some will read only it. It should stand alone: purpose, what was done, the main findings, and the recommendation, in roughly a tenth of the report's length.

Write it last, when findings are settled, and add no information not in the report. Use concrete numbers and plain verbs.

A summary that says testing indicated a potential concern hides the finding; one that says three of nine samples failed the strength requirement lets the reader act.

Summary length
\[L_{\text{summary}} \approx \frac{L_{\text{report}}}{10}\]
a guide, not a rule

04Email that gets a reply

An email is a request with context. The subject line names the project and the action. Open with the purpose, give the minimum context, state what you need and by when, and close with a clear next step.

Tone follows the relationship, the topic and the context: directness, formality and politeness are separate dials, not one switch. Cut wordy phrases: “due to the fact that” becomes “because”; “in order to” becomes “to”. Prefer the active voice and concrete verbs, and keep one register.

05Presenting technical work

Slides support a talk; they are not the talk. Put one message on each slide and make the title a claim the audience can read in three seconds, such as “drainage capacity fails at peak flow”. Show the chart, not a table of every number, and remove decoration that competes with the evidence.

Signpost the argument: what the problem is, what you found, what it means, what you recommend.

Rehearse to time, prepare one sentence for each likely question, and answer the question asked before adding context. In discussion, restate the question so the room hears it, answer briefly, then stop.

06Editing for concision

Concise writing is not short writing; it is writing where every word carries weight.

  • Turn nominalisations back into verbs — “we investigated” rather than “an investigation was carried out”.
  • Cut redundant pairs such as “advance planning” or “true facts”.
  • Keep bullets parallel so they can be compared line by line.

Read the draft aloud. Long sentences, missing verbs and repeated points reveal themselves to the ear. Then proofread for agreement, articles and consistent terminology; one wrong unit or figure costs more credibility than a slightly plain style.

07The job application package: CV and cover letter

Applications are read fast, so design them like a report. A chronological CV lists roles in reverse date order and suits a clear, continuous track record; a functional CV groups evidence under skills and suits graduates or career changers. Either way, lead each entry with an action verb — designed, surveyed, coordinated — and quantify results where you can.

A cover letter is not a CV in prose. It pitches (why this firm and this role), matches (which of your achievements fit the requirements) and supplements (what the CV cannot show, such as why the work interests you).

Address the named contact, mirror the language of the advertisement, and close by inviting the interview.

08Interview craft: questions and evidence

Interview questions fall into three families:
- Open questions invite a full answer ("what are your main strengths?").
- Probing questions ask you to go deeper ("how exactly did you organise that?").
- Directive questions seek one fact ("when can you start?").

Recognising the type tells you how long and how structured the answer should be.

For strengths, show rather than tell: name the responsibility, describe a success, and quote the feedback it earned. For weaknesses, tell the story briefly, name what you learned and end on the improvement — never finish on the fault.

Evidence for a strength
Responsibility + Success + Feedback
three moves, in that order

09Numbers in findings: the language of approximation

Report findings convert raw figures into claims a reader can weigh. Round where precision adds nothing — "about two-thirds of the firms" reads better than 66.7% — and keep exact values in tables or appendices. State the sample size next to every percentage, because 9 of 15 and 180 of 300 are very different evidence.

Findings describe; discussion interprets. Keep them separate, and anchor every interpretation to a figure, a table or a quoted response.

Conclusions then generalise cautiously; recommendations convert each conclusion into an action with an owner.

From data to action
Finding → Interpretation → Conclusion → Recommendation
one step at a time; never skip the interpretation

10Reflective writing at work

A work reflection is evidence of learning, not a diary. Four questions structure it: what happened, how you felt, what you tried and learned, and why it matters for the future.

Choose one incident and dig into it rather than summarising the whole project. Categorise the learning — technical, leadership or communication — and tie it to the next project: a reflection that ends with "next time I will …" shows the loop has closed.

Keep claims specific; "I learned to prepare earlier" earns more trust when attached to the morning the laser level ran out of battery.

Key terms

Executive summary
A short, self-contained account of a report's purpose, findings and recommendation, written for a reader who may read nothing else.
Register
The level of formality and directness appropriate to a particular reader and situation.
Signposting
Language that tells the reader or listener where the argument is going and what each part does.
Active voice
A sentence pattern in which the doer of the action is the subject, making responsibility and action clear.
Parallelism
Keeping items in a list or comparison in the same grammatical form so they can be read and compared easily.
Chronological CV
A CV that lists roles in reverse date order, best when the work history is continuous and relevant.
Reflective writing
Structured writing about one incident that names what was learned and how it will change future practice.
Worked example

From a rambling sentence to an executive summary

A draft reads: 'Due to the fact that the site investigation was carried out over a period of three weeks, and in view of the fact that the results were not entirely consistent, it is recommended that consideration be given to the undertaking of further testing prior to the finalisation of the foundation design.' Rewrite it as the opening of an executive summary that a project director can act on.

  1. Identify the finding and the action: the investigation results were inconsistent, and further testing is needed before the foundation design is finalised.
  2. Delete empty phrases and nominalisations: due to the fact that becomes because; prior to the finalisation of becomes before finalising; it is recommended that consideration be given to becomes we recommend.
  3. Put the finding first and the action second, with one idea per sentence.
  4. Rewrite: 'The three-week site investigation produced inconsistent results. We recommend further testing before the foundation design is finalised.'
AnswerThe rewritten version is two sentences, states the finding and the action, and removes the empty phrases. The director can now authorise the testing.

Exam method

  • Answer the task, not the topic: state the purpose of the document in the first line so the marker can see that you understood it.
  • Budget words for the summary and the recommendation; in professional writing these carry most of the marks.
  • Leave a few minutes to read your work aloud, then fix long sentences, missing verbs and inconsistent terms.
  • In a timed email task, spend the first ten minutes identifying the reader, the purpose and the tone before typing; the structure marks outweigh the vocabulary.
  • State every percentage next to its sample size, and round the sentence while keeping the exact figure in the table.
