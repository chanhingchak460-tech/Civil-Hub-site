How engineers draw conclusions from limited data: sampling, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, designed experiments and regression. It is the discipline that keeps a decision honest when the measurements are noisy.

01Variation is the data

Measurements vary, even when the thing measured does not. Engineering data therefore needs a centre and a spread: the sample mean estimates the population mean, and the sample standard deviation estimates the typical distance of a reading from that mean.

  • The coefficient of variation, CV = s / x̄, compares variability between datasets with different units or means.
  • Always report the sample size and the units with the summary.
  • A mean without a spread hides the risk, and a spread without a mean has no scale; the pair is the minimum honest description of a dataset.
Sample mean
\[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n}\]
the sum of the readings divided by the sample size
Sample standard deviation
\[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}}\]
the divisor n − 1 removes the bias of estimating from a sample
Coefficient of variation
\[\mathrm{CV} = \frac{s}{\bar{x}}\]
a unitless measure of relative variability

02Sampling and the central limit theorem

A sample is useful only if it represents the population. Random selection, independence and a consistent measurement method are the assumptions that make inference valid; a biased sample cannot be rescued by a larger n.

  • The standard error of the mean is SE = s / √n, which shrinks as the square root of the sample size.
  • The central limit theorem says that the distribution of the sample mean approaches normal as n grows, whatever the shape of the population.
  • This is why the t and z procedures work for means of moderately large samples even when the data are not normal.
Standard error of the mean
\[\mathrm{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]
halving the SE needs four times the sample size

03Confidence intervals

A confidence interval puts a range around an estimate. For a mean with an unknown population standard deviation, the interval is x̄ ± t s / √n, where t comes from the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. The width grows with the confidence level and the variability, and falls with the sample size.

The interpretation is easy to get wrong. A 95% interval does not mean there is a 95% probability that μ lies inside this particular interval; it means that 95% of intervals built this way would contain μ. The interval estimates the parameter, not the individual readings.

Confidence interval for a mean
\[\bar{x} \pm t\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]
t is the two-tailed value for n − 1 degrees of freedom

04Hypothesis tests

A hypothesis test asks whether the data are consistent with a stated value. Set a null hypothesis H0 (no effect, no difference) and an alternative H1, choose a significance level α, and calculate a test statistic. For a mean, t = (x̄ − μ0) / (s / √n).

  • The p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the observed one, assuming H0 is true; reject H0 when p < α.
  • A Type I error rejects a true null; a Type II error fails to reject a false one.
  • Statistical significance is not practical importance: a tiny, precise difference can be significant and still irrelevant.
One-sample t statistic
\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\]
μ0 is the hypothesised mean; df = n − 1

05Comparing two populations

Two designs, two suppliers or two curing methods are compared by their means.

  • For independent samples with similar variances, pool the standard deviations and use a two-sample t test; the degrees of freedom are n1 + n2 − 2.
  • For paired observations — the same specimen before and after, or matched pairs — test the differences instead, which removes the between-pair variability.
  • Report the estimated difference with a confidence interval, not just a p-value.
  • A significant difference of 0.2 MPa may be real but too small to change a specification; the interval shows both the size and the precision of the effect.
Pooled variance
\[s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]
assumes the two populations have similar variances
Two-sample t statistic
\[t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}\]
df = n1 + n2 − 2 for the pooled test

06ANOVA and designed experiments

To compare three or more means, use analysis of variance rather than repeated t tests, which would inflate the chance of a false positive. One-way ANOVA splits the total variation into that between group means and that within groups; the F ratio compares them, and a large F suggests at least one mean differs. A significant F does not say which one — a post-hoc comparison does.

  • Designed experiments go further: randomise the runs, replicate, and block known sources of nuisance variation.
  • In a factorial experiment every combination of factor levels is tested, so main effects and interactions can be estimated.
  • An interaction means the effect of one factor depends on the level of another.
F ratio, one-way ANOVA
\[F = \frac{\mathrm{MS}_{\text{between}}}{\mathrm{MS}_{\text{within}}}\]
large F means between-group variation dominates within-group variation

07Regression and prediction

Regression fits a relationship between a response y and one or more explanatory variables x. The least squares line y = a + b x minimises the sum of squared residuals, with slope b = S_xy / S_xx and intercept a = ȳ − b x̄.

  • The coefficient of determination r² is the proportion of total variation explained by the model.
  • A high r² does not prove causation, and it does not guarantee good predictions outside the observed range.
  • Check the residuals for curvature, changing spread and outliers; a pattern in the residuals means the model is missing something.
Least squares slope
\[b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\]
S_xy is the covariance sum; S_xx is the sum of squared x deviations
Coefficient of determination
\[r^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{res}}}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\]
the fraction of variation explained by the fitted model

08Quality control and capability

Control charts separate common-cause variation, which is built into the process, from special-cause variation, which signals that something has changed.

  • A mean chart plots the sample average with limits at three standard errors either side of the centre line; a range chart tracks variability.
  • Points outside the limits, or runs of points on one side, prompt investigation rather than adjustment of every reading.
  • Process capability compares the specification with the process spread: Cp = (USL − LSL) / (6σ), and Cpk accounts for the process being off-centre.
  • A capable process is both precise and centred; a high Cp with a low Cpk means the spread is fine but the setting is wrong.
Control limits for a mean chart
\[\mathrm{UCL},\, \mathrm{LCL} = \bar{x} \pm \frac{3\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]
σ is the process standard deviation and n the subgroup size
Process capability
\[C_p = \frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mathrm{LSL}}{6\sigma}\]
USL and LSL are the upper and lower specification limits

09Chi-square tests: what the counts say

Not every question is about a mean. When the data are counts in categories, the chi-square framework replaces the t and F tests.

  • A goodness-of-fit test asks whether observed frequencies follow a hypothesised distribution; a test of independence asks whether two categorical variables are related, using a two-way table of counts.
  • Both compare observed counts with the counts expected under the null hypothesis, and both are one-sided: only large discrepancies matter.
  • The assumptions are the weak points — categories must be mutually exclusive, and every expected count should be at least 5, so merge sparse categories before testing.
Chi-square statistic
\[\chi^2 = \sum_i \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]
large values indicate poor agreement with the null model
Degrees of freedom
\[\text{fit: } k - 1 - m; \qquad \text{table: } (r-1)(c-1)\]
m is the number of parameters estimated from the data
Expected counts, not observed ones The chi-square approximation needs expected counts of roughly 5 or more in every cell; if some fall below, merge categories and state the new degrees of freedom.

10Multiple regression

Most engineering responses have several drivers, so the model becomes y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + … + ε. Each coefficient is a partial effect: the expected change in y per unit increase in that predictor, with the others held fixed. That is a different quantity from the simple two-variable slope, and mixing the two up is a classic exam error.

  • The plain R² rises whenever a predictor is added, even a useless one, so multiple regression should quote the adjusted R², which penalises each extra parameter.
  • Check for multicollinearity — predictors so strongly correlated that the model cannot separate their effects — via correlations or variance inflation factors.
  • Test the whole model with the overall F test before dissecting individual coefficients.
Multiple regression model
\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \varepsilon\]
Adjusted coefficient of determination
\[\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{(1 - R^2)(n - 1)}{n - k - 1}\]
k predictors; it rises only when a predictor earns its place

11Power, effect size and planning a study

A non-significant result means little if the study had no chance of detecting the effect that mattered. Power is the probability of rejecting H₀ when a particular alternative is true, driven by four quantities: the significance level α, the true effect size δ, the noise σ and the sample size n.

  • For a two-sided z test, the required sample is approximately (z_(α/2) + z_β)² σ² / δ², where 1 − β is the target power.
  • Read the formula as a budget: halving the difference you must detect quadruples the sample, and a cleaner measurement method (smaller σ) is as valuable as more replicates.
  • State the power and the assumed effect size whenever you fail to reject.
Sample size for a two-sided z test
\[n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}\]
β is the Type II error rate, so 1 − β is the power

Key terms

Standard error
The standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution, showing how precisely a sample estimates a population value.
Confidence interval
A range of values, built from the data, that would contain the true parameter in a stated proportion of repeated samples.
p-value
The probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the data, assuming the null hypothesis is true.
Type I error
Rejecting a null hypothesis that is actually true; its probability is the significance level α.
ANOVA
Analysis of variance, a method that compares several group means by splitting total variation into between-group and within-group parts.
Coefficient of determination
The proportion of the response variation explained by a regression model, written r².
Goodness-of-fit test
A chi-square test of whether observed counts agree with a hypothesised distribution or model.
Power
The probability that a hypothesis test rejects the null hypothesis when the specified alternative is true.
Worked example

Comparing two curing methods

Two curing methods are compared on 10 concrete cubes each. Method A gives a mean strength of 42.1 MPa with standard deviation 2.0 MPa; method B gives 40.3 MPa with standard deviation 1.8 MPa. Test at the 5% significance level whether the mean strengths differ. Take the critical t value for 18 degrees of freedom as 2.101.

  1. State the hypotheses: H0: μA = μB; H1: μA ≠ μB. Use α = 0.05 in a two-tailed test with df = 10 + 10 − 2 = 18.
  2. Pooled variance: s_p² = [(9)(2.0²) + (9)(1.8²)] / 18 = (36.0 + 29.16) / 18 = 3.62, so s_p = 1.90 MPa.
  3. Standard error of the difference: s_p √(1/10 + 1/10) = 1.90 × 0.447 = 0.850 MPa.
  4. Test statistic: t = (42.1 − 40.3) / 0.850 = 2.12.
  5. Since |t| = 2.12 is greater than the critical value 2.101, reject H0. The difference is statistically significant, though only 1.8 MPa, so practical significance needs engineering judgement.
AnswerReject H0 at the 5% level: the mean strengths differ significantly (t = 2.12, df = 18, p just below 0.05). The estimated difference is 1.8 MPa, and a larger sample would give a more precise estimate.

Exam method

  • State H0, H1 and α before touching the calculator; most marks in a test question come from a correct setup.
  • Interpret a confidence interval in terms of repeated sampling, not as a probability that the parameter lies inside this interval.
  • Check the assumptions of independence, normality and equal variance before trusting a t or F result.
  • A significant ANOVA F says that at least one mean differs, not which one; say so in the conclusion.
  • In multiple regression, write the words holding the other predictors fixed beside every coefficient you interpret.
