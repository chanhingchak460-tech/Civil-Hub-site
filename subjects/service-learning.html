Working with a community partner on a real project while learning from the work itself. It covers needs assessment, co-design, delivery, honest evaluation and structured reflection, with an emphasis on reciprocity rather than charity.

01More than volunteering

Service learning is a course with a partner. Students work on a real community project, usually with a school, charity or residents' group, and the experience carries academic credit because it is assessed through reflection and evidence of learning.

The difference from volunteering is reciprocity: the community gains something it asked for, and the students gain knowledge they could not get from a lecture. Charity does something for people; service learning does something with them.

02Start with a needs assessment

Never design before listening. A needs assessment asks who is affected, what they already have, and what they say is missing.

  • Interview the people who will use the output, not only the staff who host you.
  • Walk the site rather than reading about it.
  • Take an asset-based view: a school may lack equipment but has a hall, a timetable and teachers who know their students.
  • Record what the partner asked for, so the brief is theirs as well as yours.

03Co-design, not delivery from above

Participatory design gives users real decisions, not a consultation at the end.

  • Prototype early and cheaply — a paper model, a draft lesson, a walk-through — because people react to something concrete far better than to an abstract proposal.
  • Watch for barriers of language, reading level, mobility, cost and timing.
  • Agree what is fixed and what is open before a workshop starts, so the group knows where its power actually lies.

04Deliver within real constraints

A service project is still a project: scope, schedule, budget and risk. Partners have calendars, rules and busy staff, and a plan that ignores a school's examination period will fail.

  • Plan for safeguarding and consent when working with children or vulnerable adults.
  • Check insurance and site safety before the first session.
  • Build capacity so the work survives your departure: leave instructions, materials and a named contact.

05Reflection is the learning

Reflection is not a diary of what happened; it is structured thinking that connects the experience to theory and to your own practice.

The Gibbs cycle gives one route: describe the situation, name your feelings, evaluate what went well, analyse why, conclude what you would do differently, and commit to an action. Strong reflection is specific, admits difficulty and shows changed understanding. Incidents are evidence; general claims are not.

Gibbs reflective cycle
Description → Feelings → Evaluation → Analysis → Conclusion → Action plan
the last stage is a commitment, not a summary

06Evaluate impact honestly

Outputs are countable — a workshop ran, a model was built. Outcomes are changes in knowledge, behaviour or confidence; impact is the longer-term difference.

  • Ask the partner how they will judge success.
  • Use a short before-and-after measure rather than impression alone.
  • Report what did not work, who was left out and what you would change.

Honest evaluation serves a partner better than a polished success story.

Impact chain
Output → Outcome → Impact
move one step at a time; a delivered activity is not automatically a change
Partner-led success criterion
Success = what the partner said they needed, evidenced
not what the team found interesting to build

07Quick reflection frames

Gibbs is not the only route. The What? So what? Now what? frame compresses reflection to three moves: describe the incident, explain why it matters and what it teaches, then commit to a change. It suits short reflections and quick debriefs after a session.

Whatever the model, the evidence test is the same: a good reflection names one incident, connects it to a concept, and ends with a changed intention. "The students were quiet" is a diary; "the students were quiet until we switched to hands-on building, which showed me that engagement follows activity, so next time I will plan less talk" is reflection.

What? So what? Now what?
What happened → So what (why it matters) → Now what (what changes)
three moves, one incident

08Ethics, consent and positionality

You arrive as an outsider with a university badge, so notice the power gap: positionality is the honest account of who you are in the project — your background, resources and assumptions — and how it shapes what you see. Community members are experts on their own lives; treat them as co-owners, not subjects.

  • Get written permission before taking or publishing photos, especially of children.
  • Let participants withdraw at any time.
  • Report findings without naming individuals or groups.
  • Write up what the partner said, not what you wish they had said.
Consent is not optional Photographing or naming participants — especially minors — without written consent breaks both trust and safeguarding rules; check before every session, not just the first.

09Sessions that adapt: rehearsal and plan B

Every session needs a trial run. Build a prototype of the activity and time it — a model that takes the team ninety minutes will take a classroom of newcomers twice that. Rehearse the instructions out loud, because the fastest way to lose a group is a task nobody understood.

Then prepare the fallbacks: extra materials for what breaks, a simpler and a harder version for different abilities, and a time buffer so one slow step does not eat the next activity. A plan B is not pessimism; it is respect for the people who showed up.

Session plan
Objective + Materials + Timing + Plan B
write the objective first; cut anything that does not serve it

10Handover and sustainability

The project ends; the community remains. A handover leaves the partner able to continue without you: written instructions, materials that survive normal use, and a named person on the partner's side who understands the work. Run one session where they lead and you watch.

Sustainability also means honesty about maintenance — a sensor or garden that needs weekly care is a burden unless someone owns it. Agree a follow-up measure with the partner a few months after you leave, so the final report can say what actually lasted rather than what was promised.

Handover package
Instructions + Materials + Named owner + Follow-up check
leave capacity, not just artefacts

Key terms

Reciprocity
A two-way exchange in which the community and the students each gain something the other needs.
Needs assessment
A structured process of finding out what a community has, what it lacks and what it asks for before designing a response.
Participatory design
Designing with users as decision-makers rather than presenting finished proposals to them.
Asset-based approach
Starting from the strengths and resources a community already has rather than from its deficits.
Safeguarding
Policies and practices that protect children and vulnerable adults from harm during an activity.
Reflective practice
Deliberate, structured thinking about experience in order to improve future action.
Positionality
An honest account of who you are in a project — background, resources and assumptions — and how it shapes what you see.
Handover
Leaving the partner with instructions, materials and a named owner so the work can continue without the team.
Worked example

Turning a request into a project brief

A partner school asks a student team to "teach our students about sustainability". The team has six weeks and a small budget. How should the team turn this request into a workable project with an assessable outcome?

  1. Ask the partner what problem they see: perhaps students find the topic abstract, not that it has never been taught.
  2. Check what already exists — lessons, clubs, a roof garden — so the project builds on assets rather than duplicating them.
  3. Agree a specific, observable outcome with the partner, such as students explaining three green-city features in their own words.
  4. Design one activity that tests that outcome with limited time and materials, and prototype it with a small group first.
  5. Agree how success will be judged, then write a reflection that links the result back to course concepts.
AnswerThe vague request becomes a brief with a named outcome, an agreed audience, a single measurable activity and a partner-defined success criterion. The reflection can then evidence learning against that outcome instead of listing activities.

Exam method

  • Name the partner's need before describing your solution; assessors look for evidence that you listened first.
  • Focus each reflection on one turning point with a concrete incident, not on a summary of the whole project.
  • Keep outputs and outcomes separate in evaluation questions; they are judged by different criteria.
  • In ethics questions, separate what you are legally allowed to do from what the partner would consider respectful — consent and anonymity carry the first marks.
