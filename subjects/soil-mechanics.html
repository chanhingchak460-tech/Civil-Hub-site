Soil is a three-phase material whose strength and settlement depend on effective stress, drainage and stress history. This page connects the phase relationships, classification tests, consolidation theory and shear strength that underpin foundation and slope design.

01The three-phase picture

Soil is a skeleton of particles with water and air in the gaps. The void volume is V_v = V_w + V_a, and the phase relationships compare those volumes and weights.

  • Void ratio e = V_v/V_s measures looseness; porosity n = e/(1+e) is the same idea as a volume fraction.
  • Water content w = W_w/W_s compares water mass with solid mass, and degree of saturation S_r = V_w/V_v says how full the voids are.
  • Specific gravity G_s is about 2.65 for quartz particles.
  • The unit weights follow from the phase diagram: bulk γ = (G_s + e S_r) γ_w/(1+e) and dry γ_d = γ/(1+w) = G_s γ_w/(1+e).

Consistency saves later errors.

Void ratio
\[e = \frac{V_v}{V_s}\]
volume of voids over volume of solids
Dry unit weight
\[\gamma_d = \frac{G_s \gamma_w}{1 + e}\]
also γ/(1+w)

02Classification and Atterberg limits

Coarse soils are classified by sieve analysis and fine soils by the hydrometer test. The grading curve gives D₁₀, D₃₀ and D₆₀, and the uniformity coefficient C_u = D₆₀/D₁₀: a high value means a well-graded soil.

For fine soils the Atterberg limits mark the water contents where behaviour changes.

  • The liquid limit LL is where the soil stops flowing (20 mm cone penetration in the fall-cone test).
  • The plastic limit PL is where a 3 mm thread crumbles.
  • The plasticity index PI = LL − PL measures the range of plastic behaviour.

A high PI signals a compressible clay, and a plasticity chart separates silts from clays.

Plasticity index
\[\mathrm{PI} = \mathrm{LL} - \mathrm{PL}\]
width of the plastic range
Uniformity coefficient
\[C_u = \frac{D_{60}}{D_{10}}\]
grading curve spread

03Compaction

Compaction densifies soil by expelling air, usually with rollers. The laboratory Proctor test compacts a sample at several water contents and plots dry density against water content, giving a curve that peaks at the optimum moisture content.

Too dry and particles lock against each other; too wet and water occupies the voids the soil would otherwise fill.

Compaction raises strength and bearing capacity and reduces settlement and permeability, which is why fill is placed in thin layers and compacted to a specified relative compaction rather than simply dumped.

Relative compaction
\[\mathrm{RC} = \frac{\gamma_d(\text{field})}{\gamma_d(\text{max, lab})}\]
usually specified as a percentage

04Effective stress and seepage

Strength and settlement are governed by effective stress: σ' = σ − u, where σ is the total stress from the weight of soil and water above and u is the pore water pressure. Same total stress and different pore pressure give completely different behaviour.

  • Below the water table, u = ρ_w g h and effective stress uses the submerged unit weight γ' = γ_sat − γ_w.
  • Above it, capillary water can give negative pore pressure and temporarily raise σ'.
  • Seepage adds a force. Darcy's law gives v = k i and q = k i A, with gradient i = Δh/L.
  • When upward seepage approaches the submerged weight, effective stress reaches zero: the critical hydraulic gradient is i_c = (G_s − 1)/(1 + e), and beyond it the soil boils and piping can begin.
Effective stress
\[\sigma' = \sigma - u\]
carried by the skeleton
Darcy's law
\[v = k i\]
permeability coefficient
Critical hydraulic gradient
\[i_c = \frac{G_s - 1}{1 + e}\]
effective stress falls to zero

05Consolidation and settlement

Consolidation is the time-dependent compression of a saturated clay as excess pore water pressure dissipates. The load is carried by water at first; as it drains, the pressure transfers to the soil skeleton and the void ratio falls, producing settlement.

The oedometer test loads a sample in increments and plots void ratio against log pressure. The slope of the virgin compression line is the compression index C_c, and the preconsolidation pressure marks the largest stress ever carried. The overconsolidation ratio OCR compares that stress with the present one; an overconsolidated clay settles much less.

Settlement follows s_c = C_c H/(1 + e₀) · log₁₀((σ'₀ + Δσ)/σ'₀), and the rate depends on the coefficient of consolidation c_v.

One-dimensional settlement
\[s_c = \frac{C_c H}{1 + e_0} \log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma'_0 + \Delta \sigma}{\sigma'_0}\right)\]
layer thickness

06Shear strength and Mohr–Coulomb

Shear strength is the resistance to sliding between particles. The Mohr–Coulomb criterion is τ_f = c' + σ' tan φ', where c' is effective cohesion, φ' the effective friction angle and σ' the effective normal stress on the failure plane.

  • Plot τ_f against σ' and the intercept is c' while the slope is tan φ'.
  • The Mohr circle at failure touches that envelope, and the failure plane makes 45° + φ'/2 with the major principal plane.
  • Loose sand has φ' near 30°, dense sand more.
  • A saturated soft clay sheared without drainage behaves as if φ_u = 0, and its strength is the undrained shear strength s_u.
  • A direct shear box shears a sample along a forced plane; the triaxial test loads a cylindrical sample under a controlled confining pressure and can measure pore pressure, so total and effective parameters are both obtainable.
Mohr–Coulomb criterion
\[\tau_f = c' + \sigma' \tan \varphi'\]
drained, effective stress form

07Earth pressure on retaining walls

Earth pressure behind a wall depends on how far the wall moves. With no movement the soil is at rest: σ'_h = K₀ σ'_v with K₀ ≈ 1 − sin φ'.

  • If the wall yields outward, the soil reaches an active state with K_a = tan²(45° − φ'/2).
  • Pushing the wall into the soil mobilises passive resistance with K_p = tan²(45° + φ'/2), typically several times larger.
  • Water pressure must be added separately.

A wall that cannot drain builds pore pressure the soil skeleton never feels, and that is a common cause of failure.

Active earth pressure coefficient
\[K_a = \tan^2\left(45^{\circ} - \frac{\varphi'}{2}\right)\]
wall moves away from the soil
Passive earth pressure coefficient
\[K_p = \tan^2\left(45^{\circ} + \frac{\varphi'}{2}\right)\]
wall pushes into the soil

08Bearing capacity and slope stability

A footing fails when the soil beneath shears. The bearing capacity equation combines three terms: q_ult = c N_c + q N_q + 0.5 γ B N_γ, where the N factors depend on φ' and q is the surcharge at foundation level. The allowable pressure is q_ult divided by a factor of safety of about 3.

Slopes fail when driving shear exceeds available strength; stability is FoS = resisting/driving.

Drainage is the strongest lever: lowering the water table raises effective stress and cuts pore pressure, which is why so many slope failures follow heavy rain.

Bearing capacity
\[q_{\text{ult}} = c N_c + q N_q + 0.5 \gamma B N_\gamma\]
N factors from φ'
Factor of safety
\[\mathrm{FoS} = \frac{\text{resisting shear}}{\text{driving shear}}\]
1.5 to 3 in design

09Permeability: constant head and falling head

The constant head test suits coarse soils: a steady flow through a cylinder of length L and area A under head difference Δh is collected and timed, giving k = QL/(A Δh t). A laboratory run on coarse sand gave k₂₀ ≈ 7.9 × 10⁻³ m/s after correcting for temperature with k₂₀ = k_T η_T/η₂₀.

Fine soils pass too little water to meter, so the falling head test watches a standpipe of area a drain through the sample: k = (a L)/(A t) ln(h₁/h₂). Samples of decomposed granite and decomposed volcanic rock gave 1.8 × 10⁻⁸ and 5.8 × 10⁻⁸ m/s — nine orders of magnitude slower than the sand, which is why drainage design must know the soil.

Constant head permeability
\[k = \frac{Q L}{A \Delta h t}\]
steady flow collected over time t
Falling head permeability
\[k = \frac{a L}{A t} \ln\frac{h_1}{h_2}\]
a is the standpipe area, h₁ and h₂ the initial and final heads
Temperature correction
\[k_{20} = k_T \frac{\eta_T}{\eta_{20}}\]
viscosity correction to a standard 20 °C value

10Reading the oedometer: c_v from t90

The magnitude of settlement comes from C_c, but the rate needs the coefficient of consolidation c_v. Plot compression against the square root of time: the early readings lie on a straight line whose back-projection to zero gives the corrected origin, and a second line with abscissae 1.15 times larger intersects the curve at t90, the time for 90% consolidation.

Then c_v = T_v H²/t90 with T_90 = 0.848 and H the drainage path — half the sample height for two-way drainage.

The companion coefficient of volume compressibility links settlement per unit of stress increase, and in the laboratory the higher pressure increments gave lower t90 as the clay stiffened.

Coefficient of consolidation
\[c_v = \frac{T_v H^2}{t_{90}}\]
H is the longest drainage path
Time factor at 90% consolidation
\[T_{90} = 0.848\]
Coefficient of volume compressibility
\[m_v = \frac{\Delta e}{(1 + e_0) \Delta \sigma'}\]
volumetric strain per unit effective stress increase

11Triaxial tests: UU, CU and CD

The triaxial cell controls drainage, which is what distinguishes the three standard tests.

  • Unconsolidated undrained (UU): no drainage at any stage, so it is quick and cheap; a saturated clay then shows φ_u ≈ 0 and its strength is the undrained shear strength s_u = (σ₁ − σ₃)/2, valid only for the undrained condition.
  • Consolidated undrained (CU): the sample consolidates under cell pressure, then shears closed while pore pressure is measured, so effective parameters c′ and φ′ emerge.
  • Consolidated drained (CD): drainage throughout, sheared slowly, giving the true drained strength for sand and long-term clay behaviour.

A laboratory CD run on granular soil returned c′ = 0 and φ′ ≈ 40°.

Undrained shear strength, UU
\[s_u = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2}\]
radius of the failure Mohr circle in total stress

Key terms

Void ratio
The volume of voids divided by the volume of solids, a basic measure of how loose or dense a soil is.
Plasticity index
The range of water content over which a fine soil behaves plastically, equal to LL − PL.
Effective stress
Total stress minus pore water pressure; it is the stress carried by the soil skeleton and controls strength and compression.
Overconsolidation ratio
The past maximum effective stress divided by the present effective stress, showing how heavily a clay was preloaded.
Compression index
The slope of the virgin compression line on the void ratio versus log pressure plot from an oedometer test.
Factor of safety
The ratio of available shear resistance to the shear stress needed to cause failure.
Coefficient of consolidation
The parameter c_v governing how fast excess pore water pressure dissipates and settlement proceeds in a consolidating layer.
Falling head test
A permeability test for fine soils in which a standpipe drains through the sample and k is found from the timed fall of the water level.
Worked example

Settlement of a clay layer under a new embankment

A 4.0 m thick normally consolidated clay layer has an initial void ratio of 1.10 and a compression index of 0.35. The initial effective vertical stress at mid-depth is 100 kPa. The embankment adds a uniform stress increase of 40 kPa. Estimate the final consolidation settlement of the layer.

  1. The settlement formula is s_c = [C_c H/(1 + e₀)] · log₁₀[(σ'₀ + Δσ)/σ'₀].
  2. Substitute the values: s_c = [0.35 × 4.0/(1 + 1.10)] × log₁₀[(100 + 40)/100].
  3. The coefficient is 1.40/2.10 = 0.6667 m.
  4. The stress ratio is 140/100 = 1.40, and log₁₀(1.40) = 0.1461.
  5. So s_c = 0.6667 × 0.1461 = 0.0974 m ≈ 97 mm.
  6. Sanity check: a 40% stress increase on a soft normally consolidated clay giving under 100 mm is reasonable; a layer twice as thick would settle about twice as much.
AnswerThe final consolidation settlement is about 97 mm, reached over time as the excess pore pressure dissipates. The rate, not just the magnitude, must be checked against the structure's tolerance to differential movement.

Exam method

  • Draw the soil profile and compute total stress, pore pressure and effective stress at each layer boundary before attempting any strength or settlement calculation.
  • State whether a test gave total or effective stress parameters; most shear strength errors come from mixing the two.
  • For settlement questions, quote both the magnitude and the drainage path, because the thickness and permeability control how fast it occurs.
  • Check units: kPa for stress, kN/m³ for unit weight, and metres for layer thickness; a factor of 1000 slips in easily.
  • For t90 use the square-root-of-time plot and the 1.15 offset line — quoting c_v from a guessed end-of-primary point on the raw curve is a classic lost mark.
