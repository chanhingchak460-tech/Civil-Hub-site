How the forces and deflections of real frames are found: determinacy, virtual work, influence lines, moment distribution and stiffness methods. It turns a sketch of a structure into numbers an engineer can defend.

01Determinacy and stability

Equilibrium supplies three independent equations for each planar free body. Count the members, joints and reaction components: for a rigid-jointed frame the degree of static indeterminacy is D_s = 3m + r − 3j.

  • D_s = 0: reactions follow from equilibrium.
  • D_s > 0: that many redundants remain.
  • D_s < 0: warns of a mechanism.
  • Internal hinges reduce the count.

Determinacy is arithmetic, not proof of stability. Parallel supports, or reactions meeting at one point, can leave a determinate structure free to move, so sketch the mechanism first.

Degree of static indeterminacy, planar frame
\[D_s = 3m + r - 3j\]
m members, r reaction components, j joints; D_s < 0 indicates a mechanism

02Virtual work and the unit load method

The unit load method finds one displacement at a time. Apply a virtual unit load at the point and in the direction required, determine the internal virtual forces, then sum the products of virtual and real actions over the structure.

  • For a frame: Σ∫ M m / (EI) ds.
  • For a truss: Σ n N L / (A E).

The sign follows the assumed virtual direction: a negative result means the displacement is opposite to the unit load. It is ideal for checking a computer result at a chosen point.

Unit load method, frame
\[\Delta = \sum \int \frac{M m}{E I}\,\mathrm{d}s\]
M and m are the real and virtual bending moments
Unit load method, truss
\[\Delta = \sum \frac{n N L}{A E}\]
n and N are the virtual and real axial forces

03The flexibility method

The flexibility (force) method handles indeterminate structures by removing supports or internal actions until a statically determinate released structure remains. The unknowns are the redundant forces X; the price of release is that the released structure must be made to behave like the real one.

Compatibility supplies the missing equations: the displacement at each released coordinate, caused by the applied loads plus the redundants, must equal its real value — usually zero. In matrix form f X + D = 0, where f_ij is the displacement at coordinate i from a unit redundant at j and D is the displacement there from the loads alone. Each coefficient follows from virtual work.

When using diagram multiplication, split the moment diagrams into simple shapes whose areas and centroids are known. The slope of a bending moment diagram equals the shear, so a diagram whose slope changes at a joint must be decomposed — a parabola and a triangle, not one imagined shape.

Compatibility equations in matrix form
\[\mathbf{f}\,\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{D} = 0\]
f flexibility matrix, X redundants, D displacements of the released structure under the loads
Flexibility coefficient
\[f_{ij} = \sum \int \frac{m_i m_j}{E I}\,\mathrm{d}s\]
m_i and m_j are the bending moments from unit redundants at i and j

04Influence lines

An influence line shows how one response — reaction, shear, moment or deflection — varies as a unit load travels across the structure. Once the line is known, any load train is handled by superposition: multiply each load by the ordinate beneath it and add. Placing loads over the largest ordinates gives the governing position, which is why they suit bridges and crane girders.

The Müller-Breslau principle gives the shape directly: release the structure at the point of interest, impose a unit displacement or rotation in the direction of the response, and the resulting deflected shape is the influence line. For a simply supported beam, the moment influence line has an ordinate a b / L beneath the section.

Moment influence ordinate, simply supported beam
\[y = \frac{a b}{L}\]
a, b measured from the section to the supports; L is the span

05Moment distribution

Moment distribution solves indeterminate beams and frames joint by joint, without simultaneous equations. Lock every joint, write the fixed-end moments from the member loads, then release one joint at a time. The unbalanced moment is shared between the members in proportion to their stiffness, and part is carried over to fixed far ends; repeat until the carry-over is negligible.

  • For constant EI, stiffness is 4EI/L when the far end is fixed and 3EI/L when it is pinned.
  • The carry-over factor is 0.5 to a fixed end and zero to a pinned end.

Fix the sign convention at the start: hogging on one side of a joint is sagging on the other.

Distribution factor
\[\mathrm{DF} = \frac{k}{\sum k}\]
k = 4EI/L for a fixed far end, 3EI/L for a pinned far end
Carry-over factor
\[C = 0.5\]
constant EI, far end fixed; no carry-over to a pinned end

06The stiffness method

The stiffness method takes joint displacements as the unknowns. Each member is described by a stiffness matrix in local axes; the matrices are rotated to global axes and assembled into K u = F. Member loads become equivalent nodal forces, the equations are solved for u, and member end forces follow from the member displacements.

Written out by hand, the same idea is the slope-deflection equation: M_AB = (4EI/L) θ_A + (2EI/L) θ_B + FEM_AB. It copes with arbitrary geometry and support conditions, which is why it underpins frame analysis software; the model is only as good as its supports and properties.

Assembled stiffness equations
\[\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{F}\]
K global stiffness matrix, u nodal displacements, F applied nodal forces
Slope-deflection equation
\[M_{AB} = \frac{4 E I}{L}\theta_A + \frac{2 E I}{L}\theta_B + \mathrm{FEM}_{AB}\]
θ in radians; FEM is the fixed-end moment from member loading

07Stiffness analysis of plane trusses

For a plane truss the stiffness method becomes a clean recipe. Each member is a bar of axial stiffness EA/L, and its end forces depend on the difference of the end displacements resolved along the bar axis. In global coordinates the member stiffness matrix is k = (EA/L) [[c², cs], [cs, s²]], where c = cos α and s = sin α for a bar inclined at angle α.

Assembly adds each 2 × 2 matrix into the global K at the degrees of freedom of the member's two joints; supports are imposed by deleting the restrained rows and columns. Solve K u = F for the joint displacements, then recover each member force with N = (EA/L) [c s] (u_B − u_A), where a positive value means tension. A truss solution that never states its tension convention is not finished.

Member stiffness matrix, plane truss
\[k = \frac{EA}{L}\begin{bmatrix} c^2 & cs \\ cs & s^2 \end{bmatrix}\]
c = cos α, s = sin α; 2 × 2 in global x–y coordinates
Member force from end displacements
\[N = \frac{EA}{L}\begin{bmatrix} c & s \end{bmatrix}\left(\mathbf{u}_B - \mathbf{u}_A\right)\]
positive N is tension; u_A and u_B are the joint displacement vectors

08Frame behaviour and sway

A rigid-jointed frame resists lateral load by bending its columns and beams together. If the frame can translate, sway adds to the moments and the structure is sway-sensitive; infill, bracing or a core can change the analysis category and must be included. Sway frames need second-order analysis or amplified moments, since P-Δ effects grow with height and axial load.

For preliminary sizing, the portal method assumes a point of contraflexure at mid-height of each column and mid-span of each beam, so the storey shear is shared between columns. It is a sketch, not a design method, but it catches gross imbalance early.

Lateral stiffness of fixed-base columns
\[K_{\mathrm{lat}} \approx \sum \frac{12 E I_c}{h^3}\]
first estimate; beam restraint reduces the effective value

09Checking a computer model

Software returns numbers whether or not the model makes sense. Before using the output, confirm units and axes, supports and releases, and section orientation.

  • Check that the sum of applied loads equals the sum of reactions.
  • Check that moments return to zero at every pin.
  • Check that the deflected shape matches the restraints.
  • Warnings about near-singular stiffness usually mean a free node or a mechanism.
  • Check one member by hand — equilibrium, a standard formula or a known influence line.
  • Refine the mesh and see whether the answer moves; a result that changes with an irrelevant refinement is not converged.
The diagram must close A bending moment diagram that does not return to zero at a pin is evidence of a sign error, a release error or an unbalanced load.

10From analysis to design

Analysis gives actions, not a verdict. Combine the load cases, take the envelope of effects along each member, and check strength, stability and serviceability separately. Second-order effects must be included for slender or sway-sensitive frames, and moving loads need influence lines or influence surfaces to find the governing position.

Record the load combination, sign convention and units behind every number, because the section design depends on all three. A tidy, traceable set of design actions is the real deliverable of structural analysis.

Key terms

Degree of static indeterminacy
The number of unknown reactions or member forces beyond those that equilibrium alone can determine.
Influence line
A diagram of one response as a unit load moves across a structure.
Müller-Breslau principle
The influence line for a response is the deflected shape caused by a unit displacement or rotation in the direction of that response.
Distribution factor
The share of an unbalanced joint moment taken by one member, proportional to its stiffness.
Carry-over factor
The fraction of a distributed moment carried to the far end of a member; 0.5 for constant EI with a fixed far end.
Sway frame
A frame in which lateral translation of joints is not restrained, so second-order effects must be considered.
Flexibility coefficient
The displacement at one released coordinate caused by a unit redundant force at another, used to build the compatibility equations.
Diagram multiplication
Evaluating a virtual-work integral by multiplying the areas of simple bending-moment diagrams by the ordinates at their centroids.
Worked example

Two-span continuous beam by moment distribution

A continuous beam ABC has two equal spans of 6.0 m, constant EI, and carries a uniformly distributed load of 12 kN/m on both spans. Find the hogging moment at the central support B, the three vertical reactions, and the maximum sagging moment in span AB.

  1. Fixed-end moment at B for each span, with the far end pinned: w L² / 8 = 12 × 6.0² / 8 = 54 kNm hogging.
  2. Distribution factors at B: both spans have stiffness 3EI/L, so each factor is 0.5. The fixed-end moments from the two spans act in opposite rotational senses at the joint, so the net unbalanced moment is zero and no distribution or carry-over is required.
  3. The support moment is therefore M_B = 54 kNm hogging. For span AB: R_A = wL/2 − M_B/L = 36 − 9 = 27 kN; the reaction at B from this span is 36 + 9 = 45 kN, and the other span adds the same, so R_B = 90 kN.
  4. Check vertical equilibrium: 27 + 90 + 27 = 144 kN = 12 × 12 m. Correct.
  5. Maximum sagging moment in AB occurs where the shear is zero, at x = 27/12 = 2.25 m from A: M = 27 × 2.25 − 12 × 2.25²/2 = 30.4 kNm.
AnswerM_B = 54 kNm hogging, R_A = R_C = 27 kN, R_B = 90 kN, and the maximum sagging moment in span AB is 30.4 kNm at 2.25 m from A.

Exam method

  • Count indeterminacy and sketch the deflected shape before choosing a method; the method follows from the structure, not from habit.
  • In virtual work questions, keep the virtual system truly virtual and carry its sign through to the answer; a negative displacement is a direction, not an error.
  • For influence lines, find the ordinates at the critical points first; the maximum effect is then a simple superposition of load times ordinate.
  • When a computer analysis is given, spend two minutes checking reactions and the moment diagram before using any output in a design check.
  • In a force-method solution, draw the released structure and every unit-redundant moment diagram before writing the flexibility matrix; most errors are sign errors between those diagrams.
