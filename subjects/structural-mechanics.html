The grammar of structures: how loads travel through members, how stress and strain relate, and how beams, shafts and columns fail. It underpins every later design subject.

01Start with a free body diagram

Every structural question begins the same way: isolate the part you care about, draw every force acting on it, and apply equilibrium. A free body diagram is not decoration — if a reaction or a member force is missing, the numbers that follow are meaningless.

For a planar structure there are three independent equilibrium equations: ΣF_x = 0, ΣF_y = 0 and ΣM = 0. Use them to find reactions first, then cut the structure at the section you need. The internal forces at a cut are always drawn in their positive sense: axial force N, shear force V and bending moment M.

A structure is statically determinate when the reactions can be found from equilibrium alone. Extra supports make it indeterminate, and the solution then needs compatibility — the requirement that deformations fit together.

Planar equilibrium
\[\sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0, \quad \sum M = 0\]

02Stress, strain and the elastic modulus

Stress converts a force into an intensity: normal stress σ = F/A acts perpendicular to the section, while shear stress τ acts along it. Strain is the corresponding deformation per unit length, ε = ΔL/L.

For a linear elastic material, stress and strain are proportional up to the yield point. The constant is Young's modulus E = σ/ε, a measure of stiffness rather than strength. A material can be stiff but weak, or flexible but strong — steel is both stiff and strong; rubber is neither stiff nor strong, but it is very extensible.

When a bar is pulled, it also gets thinner. Poisson's ratio ν = −ε_lateral/ε_axial captures that effect, and it links the elastic constants: G = E / [2(1 + ν)]. The value of ν for steel is about 0.30 and for concrete about 0.15–0.20.

Normal stress
\[\sigma = \frac{F}{A}\]
force perpendicular to the section, in newtons per square metre
Young's modulus
\[E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\]
slope of the linear elastic part of the stress–strain curve
Shear modulus
\[G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}\]

03Axial deformation and compatibility

A prismatic bar of length L, area A and modulus E carrying axial force P changes length by ΔL = P L / (A E). This is the workhorse of indeterminate problems: write the elongation of each member in terms of the unknown force, then impose the geometric condition that the members still meet at the same joint.

Temperature change adds a free expansion ΔL = α L ΔT. If that expansion is restrained, the member develops a thermal stress σ = −E α ΔT. A steel rail fixed at both ends on a hot day is in compression even though nothing touched it — the movement was prevented, not the temperature.

Axial deformation
\[\Delta L = \frac{P L}{A E}\]
Thermal stress, fully restrained
\[\sigma = -E \alpha \Delta T\]

04Bending: the flexure formula

Bending moment produces a stress gradient across a section. The neutral axis passes through the centroid; fibres on one side are in compression and on the other in tension.

The flexure formula is σ = M y / I, where y is the distance from the neutral axis and I is the second moment of area about that axis. The maximum stress occurs at the extreme fibre, so a section is often described by its elastic section modulus Z = I / y_max, giving σ_max = M / Z.

Doubling the depth of a rectangular beam increases I eightfold, because I = b h³ / 12 — depth is far more efficient than width. Shear force does not disappear. In a beam it produces a parabolic shear stress distribution, zero at the extreme fibres and maximum at the neutral axis, with τ_max = 3V/(2A) for a rectangle.

Flexure formula
\[\sigma = \frac{M y}{I}\]
Elastic section modulus
\[Z = \frac{I}{y_{\max}}\]
Second moment of area, rectangle
\[I = \frac{b h^3}{12}\]

05Deflection and the stiffness check

Bending also changes shape. The curvature is related to the moment by EI v'' = M, where EI is the flexural rigidity and v is the transverse displacement. Integrating twice gives slope and deflection; the constants come from the supports.

For common cases the results are worth remembering:
- Simply supported beam with central point load P: δ = PL³/(48EI) at midspan.
- Simply supported beam with uniformly distributed load w: δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI).
- Cantilever with end load: δ = PL³/(3EI).

Superposition lets you add these patterns for combined loading, provided the response stays linear. Deflection limits often control design before strength does, especially for long-span floors and slender roofs.

Beam curvature
\[E I v'' = M\]
Midspan deflection, central point load
\[\delta = \frac{P L^3}{48 E I}\]
Midspan deflection, uniformly distributed load
\[\delta = \frac{5 w L^4}{384 E I}\]

06Torsion in circular shafts

A circular shaft twisted by torque T develops shear stress that grows linearly with radius: τ = T r / J, where J is the polar second moment of area. For a solid circle J = π d⁴ / 32, so τ_max = 16T/(π d³).

The angle of twist over length L is θ = T L / (G J). Torsion is the reason hollow shafts are efficient: most of the torsional resistance lives in the outer fibres, so material near the centre contributes little.

Only circular sections stay plane under torsion; non-circular sections warp, and their analysis needs a different approach.

Torsional shear stress
\[\tau = \frac{T r}{J}\]
Angle of twist
\[\theta = \frac{T L}{G J}\]
θ in radians for consistent units

07Columns and elastic buckling

A slender compression member can fail by buckling long before the material crushes. The Euler load is P_cr = π² E I / (K L)², where K depends on end conditions:
- Pinned-pinned: K = 1.0
- Fixed-fixed: K = 0.5
- Fixed-free: K = 2.0
- Fixed-pinned: K = 0.7

Buckling is governed by slenderness rather than strength. Short, stocky columns yield; long, slender columns buckle. The transition is captured by a slenderness ratio λ = K L / r, where r is the radius of gyration.

Because the buckling load depends on I, the direction of buckling with the smallest I controls the design.

Euler buckling load
\[P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}\]

08From the tensile test to design

The tensile test turns the theory into measured numbers. A ductile specimen such as mild steel shows an initial linear region (E), an upper and lower yield point, a plateau, strain hardening to the ultimate tensile strength, and finally necking and fracture. A high-strength steel may show almost no yield plateau and fail with much less elongation.

Ductility matters because it gives warning and allows redistribution of force. Design uses a factor of safety or partial factors to keep working stress below the relevant limit, and serviceability checks keep deflection and cracking acceptable.

Strength without stiffness, or stiffness without ductility, is rarely an acceptable engineering answer.

09Trusses and zero-force members

A truss is an assembly of pin-jointed bars carrying only axial force, so each joint must satisfy two equilibrium equations.

The method of joints works round the truss writing ΣF_x = 0 and ΣF_y = 0 at each joint, starting where at most two bar forces are unknown. The method of sections cuts through no more than three bars with unknown forces and takes moments, which reaches interior bars in one move.

Zero-force members can be spotted before any arithmetic:
- If only two non-collinear bars meet at an unloaded joint, both are zero-force.
- If three bars meet and two are collinear, the third carries nothing unless an external load acts at the joint.

In the laboratory truss, measured bar forces plotted against applied load fall on straight lines through the origin, and the zero bars read almost exactly zero.

Method of sections
\[F = \frac{M_A}{r}\]
moment about a convenient point divided by the perpendicular distance to the bar

10Shear stress in beams

Where a shear force V acts, the shear stress varies through the depth. The general result is τ = VQ/(Ib), where Q is the first moment of the area beyond the point considered and b is the width there.

For a rectangle the distribution is parabolic: zero at the extreme fibres and a maximum at the neutral axis of τ_max = 3V/(2A). In an I-section almost all of the shear is carried by the web, because the flanges only feed flow into it.

Shear rarely governs a slender beam's design, but it matters for short, deep members, for connection detailing, and at supports, where a high V may meet a section reduced by holes.

Transverse shear stress
\[\tau = \frac{V Q}{I b}\]
Q is the first moment of area beyond the point, b the local width
Maximum shear stress, rectangle
\[\tau_{\max} = \frac{3 V}{2 A}\]

11Combined loading and Mohr's circle

Real points in a member usually carry bending, axial and torsional shear at once. The direct stress σ_x from axial and bending combines with shear τ_xy from torsion and transverse shear; the principal stresses are given below, and the maximum shear stress is the square-root term alone.

A shaft under combined torque and bending is therefore checked against an equivalent stress rather than one or the other. Mohr's circle plots the states graphically: centre at (σ_x + σ_y)/2 and radius √[((σ_x − σ_y)/2)² + τ_xy²].

Principal stresses
\[\sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}\]
Maximum shear stress
\[\tau_{\max} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}\]

Key terms

Neutral axis
The line in a bending section where stress is zero and fibres neither lengthen nor shorten.
Second moment of area
A geometric measure of how far material sits from the neutral axis; it governs bending stiffness and stress.
Young's modulus
The slope of the linear part of the stress–strain curve, describing stiffness.
Ductility
The ability of a material to deform plastically before fracture.
Statically indeterminate
A structure with more reactions than equilibrium equations, requiring compatibility to solve.
Slenderness ratio
Effective length divided by radius of gyration; it predicts whether buckling or yielding controls a column.
Flexural rigidity
The product EI, the resistance of a member to bending deformation.
Zero-force member
A truss bar that carries no force, identifiable from joint geometry before any calculation.
Principal stress
The extreme normal stress on planes where the shear stress is zero, found from the Mohr circle construction.
Worked example

Maximum bending stress in a timber joist

A simply supported timber beam spans 6.0 m and carries a uniformly distributed load of 10 kN/m, including its own weight. Its cross-section is 150 mm wide by 300 mm deep. Find the maximum bending stress and state whether a timber with an allowable bending stress of 18 MPa is adequate.

  1. Maximum moment for a simply supported beam with a UDL is wL²/8 = 10 × 6.0² / 8 = 45 kNm = 45 × 10⁶ Nmm.
  2. Second moment of area about the neutral axis: I = b h³ / 12 = 150 × 300³ / 12 = 3.375 × 10⁸ mm⁴.
  3. Distance to the extreme fibre: y = h/2 = 150 mm.
  4. σ_max = M y / I = (45 × 10⁶ × 150) / (3.375 × 10⁸) = 20.0 N/mm² = 20 MPa.
  5. Sanity check with the section modulus: Z = I / y = 2.25 × 10⁶ mm³, so σ = M/Z = 45 × 10⁶ / 2.25 × 10⁶ = 20 MPa.
AnswerThe maximum bending stress is 20 MPa, which exceeds the 18 MPa allowable value, so the joist is not adequate for bending; a deeper section or a shorter span is needed.

Exam method

  • Write the equilibrium equations before touching a formula sheet; most lost marks come from a missing reaction, not from algebra.
  • For deflection questions, identify the standard case first, then use superposition rather than deriving from scratch.
  • Keep units consistent: convert kN to N and mm to m at the start, and state the unit of every answer.
  • For columns, check both buckling and yielding, and use the smaller capacity in the conclusion.
  • In truss questions, hunt for zero-force members first — removing them often exposes a joint with only two unknowns and saves several equations.

WhyP_cr = π²EI/(KL)², so it falls with the square of the effective length.

WhyThe shear changes sign at midspan, where the moment reaches its peak of PL/4.

WhyWith no external load, equilibrium in the two independent directions at the joint forces both bar forces to zero.
