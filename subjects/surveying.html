How positions and heights are measured, computed and set out on site: levelling, traverse adjustment, coordinates, total stations, GNSS and curves. It is the subject that turns ground observations into numbers you can build from.

01Control first, then detail

Every survey fixes new points relative to points already known. The known ones form the control; everything else is detail. Control is denser and more accurate than the work hanging off it, because every later measurement inherits its error.

The working rule is whole to part: build the frame — horizontal stations with coordinates, benchmarks with reduced levels — then fill in detail from that frame. Coordinates need an origin and heights need a datum, so both must be stated. A survey that starts from detail and works upwards has no way to check itself.

Accuracy is how close a result is to the truth; precision is how repeatable it is. A tight cluster of readings away from the truth is precise but biased.

02Levelling: the book must balance

Levelling measures height differences. Set the instrument between two staffs; its line of sight defines a height of collimation. The first reading after a setup is a back sight onto a point of known level; intermediate sights locate points between changes; a fore sight is the last reading before the instrument moves. The staff then stays as a change point, so it must sit on something stable.

Two booking conventions dominate:
- Height of collimation method: add the back sight to the known level once, then subtract every other reading from that height.
- Rise and fall method: compares consecutive readings and is slower, but it checks every intermediate sight.

Balance back and fore sight distances to cancel collimation and curvature, keep sights short, and avoid readings below about half a metre.

Height of collimation
\[\mathrm{HI} = \mathrm{RL} + \mathrm{BS}\]
Rise and fall
\[\text{rise} = \mathrm{BS} - \mathrm{FS}\]
a negative result is a fall

03Angles, bearings and the whole-circle convention

Directions are measured clockwise from north. A whole-circle bearing runs from 0° to 360°; a reduced bearing quotes the same direction from north or south towards east or west. Turning a sight line clockwise from the back station to the forward station gives an angle to the right, the convention most data loggers expect.

Angles in a closed polygon form a fixed sum, so the gap between measured and theoretical sum is an angular misclosure. Interior angles of an n-sided figure add to (n − 2) × 180°.

Carrying bearings onward is bookkeeping: take the back bearing of the previous leg (forward bearing ± 180°), add the angle to the right, and subtract 360° if needed.

Interior angle sum
\[\sum \theta = (n - 2) \times 180^{\circ}\]
Bearing transfer
\[\mathrm{WCB}_{\text{next}} = \mathrm{WCB}_{\text{back}} + \theta_{\text{right}}\]
WCB_back is the previous bearing ± 180°

04Traverse computation in a fixed order

A traverse measures angles and distances along a chain of stations. Compute in sequence:
- adjust the angles
- carry bearings through the figure
- convert each leg into north and east components called latitude and departure
- add them to test closure
- distribute the remainder

Angular misclosure is spread equally over the angles because each was observed with equal effort. The vector sum of the coordinate errors is the linear misclosure, and its size relative to total length is the relative precision.

The Bowditch (compass) rule shares coordinate corrections in proportion to leg length, so short legs move little. That weighting suits comparable angular and distance error; stronger distances call for another weighting.

Angular misclosure
\[e_\theta = \sum \theta_{\text{measured}} - (n - 2) \times 180^{\circ}\]
Latitude and departure
\[\Delta N = d \cdot \cos \theta, \quad \Delta E = d \cdot \sin \theta\]
Bowditch correction
\[C = -\frac{e\,d_i}{\sum d}\]
e is the closure in that component

06Total stations, EDM and GNSS

A total station combines an electronic theodolite, an EDM and a computer. The EDM times a modulated signal to obtain a slope distance, then corrects it for atmospheric conditions, instrument and reflector constants, and cyclic error. Horizontal distance follows from the slope distance and the zenith angle, and heights come from the same observation once instrument and target heights are accounted for.

GNSS measures ranges to satellites. Code (pseudorange) solutions give metres; carrier-phase solutions reach centimetres after the integer ambiguity is resolved, provided two receivers observe together — static for control, RTK for detail.

GNSS positions refer to a global reference frame rather than the local grid, so transformation and an independent height check belong in the method.

Join distance and bearing
\[d = \sqrt{\Delta N^2 + \Delta E^2}, \quad \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta N}\right)\]
check the quadrant before quoting θ
Trigonometric heighting
\[H_B = H_A + i + d \cdot \cos z - t\]
i instrument height, t target height

07Curves and setting out

Horizontal alignment joins straights with circular curves, often with transition curves of varying radius between them. For a curve of radius R and deflection angle θ, the tangent length fixes where it begins and ends, and the arc length gives the through chainage along the centre line.

Setting out by tangential angles rests on a simple fact: the angle between the tangent and a chord equals half the angle that chord subtends at the centre. Vertical curves join gradients as parabolas, with length set by stopping sight distance through a K value.

Setting out places the design on the ground from control, normally by coordinates: occupy a control station, orient to a reference object, then turn off calculated bearings and distances. Never set out from a point that was itself set out.

Tangent length
\[T = R \cdot \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\]
Circular curve length
\[L = R \cdot \theta_{\mathrm{rad}}\]
Vertical curve length
\[L = K \cdot A\]
A is the change of gradient, in percent

08Errors, closure and the paper trail

Errors are not the same as mistakes. A blunder is a gross mistake — a transposed digit, a misread staff — and redundancy finds it; adjustment must not hide it. Systematic errors repeat with the same sign and are corrected or cancelled by procedure; random errors scatter and shrink by averaging.

Two-peg tests, face-left and face-right observation, and balanced sights make these behaviours predictable. Every survey needs an accuracy statement, usually a closure compared with an allowable value.

Record enough redundant measurements to catch a blunder, and keep field sheets in ink so the computation stays traceable.

Allowable misclosure
\[E_{\text{allow}} = c \cdot \sqrt{n}\]
c depends on the accuracy order required
Relative precision
\[1 : \frac{\sum d}{e}\]

09Areas and volumes from coordinates and offsets

Areas come from coordinates or offsets. The double area formula sums cross products around the polygon, taking coordinates in order and closing the last point on the first. For an irregular boundary, offsets at even spacing are integrated by the trapezoidal rule, or by Simpson's rule, which fits parabolic arcs and needs an odd number of offsets.

Earthwork volumes follow from cross-sectional areas: the end-areas method averages two sections and multiplies by the length, and the prismoidal formula refines it when the mid-section area is known. For deep excavations, spot levels on a grid are averaged with corner, edge and interior points weighted 1, 2 and 4 times.

Double area from coordinates
\[2A = \left|\sum E_i (N_{i+1} - N_{i-1})\right|\]
sum round the closed polygon in order
Trapezoidal rule
\[A \approx d\left(\frac{h_1}{2} + h_2 + \cdots + h_{n-1} + \frac{h_n}{2}\right)\]
d the common offset spacing
Simpson's rule
\[A \approx \frac{d}{3}\left(h_1 + h_n + 4\sum h_{\mathrm{even}} + 2\sum h_{\mathrm{odd}}\right)\]
requires an odd number of offsets
End areas volume
\[V = \frac{L (A_1 + A_2)}{2}\]
mean cross-sectional area times length

10The two-peg test in practice

Levelling assumes the line of sight is horizontal; a collimation error tilts it. The two-peg test finds that tilt.

Set pegs roughly 30–40 m apart and set up midway between them; the true height difference survives even with a tilted line of sight because both distances are equal. Then set up a few metres beyond one peg and read both staffs again; the difference between the two apparent differences is the collimation error over one peg spacing, so the instrument is adjusted or the readings corrected.

Balanced back and fore sight distances achieve the same cancellation on every route, which is why it is a standing rule of precise levelling.

Collimation error from the two-peg test
\[e = (a_2 - b_2) - (a_1 - b_1)\]
difference between the near-setup and mid-setup apparent height differences

11Setting out curves by tangential angles

A circular curve is set out from the tangent by tangential angles: the angle at the tangent point between the tangent and any chord equals half the angle that chord subtends at the centre.

For a full chord c on radius R the tangential angle is c/(2R) radians; successive chords accumulate, so each point is set by turning the running total from the tangent and laying the chord distance. Transition curves are set out the same way using chords of varying radius — the arithmetic is identical, only the radius changes along the curve.

The method checks itself at the far tangent point, where the total deflection must equal half the deflection angle.

Tangential angle
\[\delta = \frac{c}{2R}\]
radians; multiply by 180/π for degrees

Key terms

Control
The network of stations with known coordinates and heights from which all other work is referenced.
Reduced level
The height of a point above or below the stated vertical datum.
Whole-circle bearing
Direction measured clockwise from north, quoted between 0° and 360°.
Angular misclosure
The difference between the measured sum of angles in a closed figure and its theoretical sum.
Bowditch rule
An adjustment that distributes coordinate misclosure in proportion to the length of each traverse leg.
Integer ambiguity
The unknown whole number of carrier wavelengths between a satellite and a receiver, resolved to reach centimetre positions.
Through chainage
Distance measured along a route centre line from a fixed origin, used to reference any point on it.
Two-peg test
A levelling check that quantifies collimation error by comparing height differences observed from midway between two pegs and from near one of them.
Tangential angle
The angle between the tangent and a chord to a curve point, equal to half the central angle subtended by that chord.
Worked example

Closing and adjusting a four-station traverse

A closed-loop traverse runs A, B, C, D and back to A. The four angles to the right each measured 90°00′02″. The bearing of AB is fixed at 090°00′00″ and measurements give AB = 100.010 m, BC = 60.000 m, CD = 99.990 m and DA = 60.000 m. Adjust the angles, compute the bearings and coordinates, then apply a Bowditch adjustment. Point A is fixed at N 1000.000 m, E 500.000 m.

  1. Measured angle sum = 4 × 90°00′02″ = 360°00′08″. Theoretical sum = (4 − 2) × 180° = 360°00′00″, so the angular misclosure is +8″. Allowable 20″√4 = 40″, so the work passes.
  2. Distribute −8″ equally: correction −2″ per angle. Every adjusted angle is exactly 90°00′00″, which suggests one systematic source rather than random scatter.
  3. Carry bearings: WCB_BC = 090° + 90° = 180°, WCB_CD = 270°, WCB_DA = 000°.
  4. Resolve legs: ΔN = d cos θ and ΔE = d sin θ. Latitudes: BC = −60.000, DA = +60.000, others zero, so ΣΔN = 0. Departures: AB = +100.010, CD = −99.990, so ΣΔE = +0.020 m.
  5. Linear misclosure = √(0² + 0.020²) = 0.020 m over a total length of 320.000 m, a relative precision of 1 : 16 000. Correction to each departure = −0.020 × d / 320: AB −0.00625 m, BC −0.00375 m, CD −0.00625 m, DA −0.00375 m.
  6. Corrected departures: AB +100.00375, BC −0.00375, CD −99.99625, DA −0.00375, summing to zero. Corrected latitudes are unchanged and also sum to zero.
  7. Add the corrected components to the coordinates: B (1000.000, 600.004), C (940.000, 600.000), D (940.000, 500.004), closing back on A at (1000.000, 500.000).
  8. Sanity check: the closing coordinates reproduce A exactly after rounding, and the misclosure was carried by legs parallel to the departing direction, which is what a distance-dependent error should look like.
AnswerAngular misclosure +8″ (within 40″ allowable) adjusted to zero; linear misclosure 20 mm, precision about 1 : 16 000, removed by Bowditch; the adjusted loop closes on A at N 1000.000 m, E 500.000 m.

Exam method

  • Do the booking check before computing: ΣBS − ΣFS must equal the difference between the first and last reduced levels.
  • For any traverse, always compute angular misclosure first; bearings cannot be trusted until the angles are closed.
  • Keep a consistent axis order: N before E, and derive bearings from atan2(ΔE, ΔN), not the other way round.
  • State the allowable misclosure and compare with it explicitly; an uncompared closure earns few marks even if the arithmetic is right.
  • Area questions with coordinates: write the double-area table with N and E columns and cross-multiply — one sign slip reverses the polygon's handedness, not just the area.

Q01In the height of collimation method, the reduced level of an intermediate point is found by:

WhyThe collimation height is the level of the line of sight, so every staff reading is subtracted from it to reach the ground point.

Q02A closed traverse has five interior angles summing to 540°00′40″. What is the angular misclosure?

WhyThe theoretical sum is (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°00′00″, so measured minus theoretical is +40″.

Q03Under the Bowditch rule, the correction applied to a traverse leg is:

WhyBowditch shares the misclosure in proportion to distance, so a short leg receives a proportionally small correction.

Q04Which GNSS observable is capable of centimetre-level survey positions?

WhyCode ranges give metres; carrier phase is measured to a few millimetres and becomes a precise range once the whole-cycle ambiguity is fixed.

Q05Simpson's rule for area computation requires the number of offsets to be:

WhySimpson's rule fits parabolic arcs in pairs of intervals, so the number of offsets (including the two ends) must be odd.

WhyWith equal sight lengths a tilted line of sight contaminates both readings equally, so the true height difference survives.
