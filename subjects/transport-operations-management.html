How transport systems are operated, scheduled and priced

Networks are built once but operated every day. This page covers how services are scheduled, how capacity and reliability are measured, and how pricing and demand management shape who travels when.

01Operations is supply meeting demand

A transport system is a physical network operated to a timetable. Supply is what the operator can deliver: vehicles, drivers, track and platform space. Demand is when and where people want to travel, and it is rarely smooth.

Operations is the daily reconciliation of the two. Three decisions dominate:
- Service plan — sets routes, stops and frequencies.
- Schedule — turns the plan into vehicle and crew duties.
- Control — adjusts the plan in real time when traffic, weather or incidents break it.

Good operations are judged by what passengers experience — wait, crowding, reliability — not only by what the timetable promises.

02Headway is the master variable

Frequency and headway are two views of the same fact. A service with headway h seconds runs 3600/h vehicles per hour.

If a bus every 400 s carries a practical capacity of 102 passengers, the line offers about 918 spaces per hour per direction; demand above that cannot be carried without more vehicles, bigger vehicles or demand management.

Headway also sets the passenger cost. With arrivals spread at random, the expected wait is half a headway, so cutting a 12-minute headway to 6 minutes removes three minutes from the average trip.

Frequency and headway
\[f = \frac{3600}{h}\]
f in vehicles per hour, h in seconds
Line capacity
\[C = f \cdot c\]
c is the practical capacity of one vehicle, in passengers
Mean wait, random arrivals
\[E[w] = \frac{h}{2}\]
passengers arrive without consulting the timetable

03Fleet size follows from round trip time

The number of vehicles needed is the round trip time divided by the headway, rounded up. A route that takes 60 minutes to return needs nine vehicles in service to hold a 400 s headway.

This is why a small delay on a long route is expensive: layover and recovery time have to sit inside the round trip, or the timetable cannot be kept.

Crews need the same arithmetic. Driver duties must respect legal limits on continuous driving and rest, so schedules are built from duty blocks rather than vehicle movements alone.

Fleet size at constant headway
\[N = \left\lceil \frac{T_{\mathrm{round}}}{h} \right\rceil\]
T_round is the round trip time including layover, in the same units as h

04Signals, stages and the cycle

A signal junction trades movements in time rather than space. Conflicting streams run in separate stages; a stage plan lists which movements get green together, and the cycle time is the interval before the pattern repeats. The green split divides the cycle among the stages, less the lost time spent starting and clearing each one.

Capacity at a stop line is set by saturation flow — the maximum vehicles per hour a lane can discharge during green — multiplied by the proportion of the cycle that is green. A lane short of capacity queues into the next cycle, so the split, not just the total green, decides delay.

The observed cycle rarely equals the sum of the greens: measure it at the site.

Lane capacity at signals
\[Q = s \times \frac{g}{c}\]
s saturation flow in veh/h, g effective green and c cycle in the same units
Saturation flow
\[s = \frac{3600}{h_s}\]
h_s saturation headway in seconds, about 1.8–2.2 s per vehicle in a straight lane
Read the cycle at the kerbside Summing observed green times misses the intergreen and lost-time periods — the cycle time must be observed directly, and a pedestrian phase adds its own stage.

05Dwell time and stop operations

At a stop, the door cycle governs the busiest part of the journey. Dwell time is the interval from doors open to doors closed: alighting and boarding passengers move in parallel where possible, so the longer of the two flows plus the door opening and closing time sets the dwell.

Dwell matters because it multiplies with passengers: each additional boarding passenger costs roughly a fixed time, and at busy stops dwell dominates the stop's capacity.

Fare media make the difference — smart cards and open boarding are faster than cash and on-bus ticket sales, so the boarding rate is a design variable, not a constant.

Dwell time
\[D = \max(t_{\text{alight}}, t_{\text{board}}) + t_{\text{doors}}\]
t_alight and t_board are the times for the two passenger flows; t_doors the door cycle

06Crew and vehicle scheduling

Vehicles and drivers are scheduled separately. Vehicle blocks run from depot to depot; crew duties run from sign-on to sign-off and must respect legal limits on continuous driving, breaks and spread-over time, so a duty rarely equals a vehicle block. Relief points are chosen where crews can change without missing a trip.

The number of duties needed is the total platform time divided by the length of a practical duty, rounded up — with rules, split shifts and allowances all inside.

Good duty design keeps paid non-driving time small, because crew is the largest operating cost on most networks.

Number of crew duties
\[N_d = \left\lceil \frac{T_{\text{platform}}}{T_{\text{duty}}} \right\rceil\]
T_platform total paid platform time; T_duty practical duty length including breaks

07Reliability: regularity beats punctuality

On a frequent service nobody checks the timetable; they expect a vehicle soon, and what hurts is the gap between vehicles. When one is delayed it collects more passengers, dwells longer and falls further behind, while the one behind catches up. This bunching produces a long wait followed by two near-empty vehicles.

Operators therefore track excess wait time — the wait beyond what a perfectly regular service would give — alongside on-time performance and load factor.

Holding a vehicle at a control point can restore spacing, but holding too long delays the passengers already on board.

MeasureWhat it capturesWhat it can hide
On-time performanceDepartures within a tolerance bandA service can leave early to look punctual

08Pricing and demand management

Transport demand responds to price, but not evenly. Price elasticity measures the percentage change in demand for a one per cent change in fare or charge; short-run car commuting is inelastic, while off-peak and discretionary trips respond more. Peak pricing, congestion charging and parking charges all aim at the trips that are most flexible.

Fares also have to fund the service. The farebox recovery ratio compares fare revenue with operating cost: above 1.0 the service covers itself, below it the gap needs subsidy.

Subsidy is not failure; it is the price of the network and social benefits that fares alone cannot capture.

Price elasticity of demand
\[E = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}\]
usually negative; |E| < 1 is inelastic
Farebox recovery ratio
\[\mathrm{CR} = \frac{R}{C}\]
R is fare revenue, C is operating cost over the same period

09Performance measures drive behaviour

Every operator optimises what it measures, so measures must be chosen carefully. A target for on-time departures at termini can be met by leaving early; a target for load factor can be met by cutting capacity.

Key performance indicators should therefore be balanced across the passenger, the operator and the public purse:
- wait
- reliability
- crowding
- cost per vehicle-km
- safety
- revenue

Benchmarking against comparable networks keeps targets honest. A number is meaningful only with its definition, its sample and its period attached.

10Managing disruption

Incidents are normal, not exceptional. A control room needs a plan for each foreseeable failure:
- a blocked road
- a failed vehicle
- a signal failure
- severe weather

The plan sets who decides, what passengers are told, how vehicles are turned back and how the service returns to timetable.

Recovery time is the unscheduled slack absorbed after an incident. Too little and small delays ripple; too much and the timetable wastes resources. Real-time information and cross-operator ticketing reduce the harm to passengers when the plan breaks.

Key terms

Headway
The time interval between successive vehicles passing the same point, usually in seconds.
Load factor
Passengers on board divided by the vehicle's capacity; the practical design value is often 0.85.
Layover
Scheduled time at the end of a trip for recovery, crew change and turning the vehicle.
Bunching
Loss of even spacing on a frequent route, when one delayed vehicle collects the passengers and the one behind catches it.
Farebox recovery ratio
Fare revenue divided by operating cost; a value below 1 means subsidy is required.
Excess wait time
The average wait beyond the wait a perfectly regular service would deliver at the same headway.
Saturation flow
The maximum discharge rate of a signal lane during green, in vehicles per hour, set by the saturation headway.
Stage
A part of the signal cycle during which a defined set of non-conflicting movements receives green.
Worked example

Fleet size for a peak-hour bus route

A single-direction peak demand of 900 passengers per hour must be carried on a route with a 60-minute round trip. Each bus has a capacity of 120 passengers, and the operator plans for a load factor of 0.85. Find the required frequency, the headway, the fleet size and the capacity check.

  1. Practical capacity per bus = 120 × 0.85 = 102 passengers.
  2. Required frequency = 900 / 102 = 8.8 buses per hour, rounded up to 9 departures per hour.
  3. Headway = 3600 / 9 = 400 s, which is 6 minutes 40 seconds.
  4. Fleet size = round trip / headway = 3600 / 400 = 9 buses, and the rounding is exact.
  5. Capacity check: 9 buses × 102 passenger places = 918 places per hour, just above the 900 demanded; actual load per bus is 900 / 9 = 100 passengers out of 120, a load factor of 0.83.
  6. Sanity check: the mean wait is h/2 = 200 s, about 3 minutes 20 seconds, which is realistic for a high-frequency feeder route.
AnswerNine buses per hour on a 400 s headway, with nine vehicles in service. The offered capacity of 918 places per hour covers the 900 demand with almost no spare allowance, so any lost vehicle will overload the service; a reliability margin or a larger vehicle would be sensible.

Exam method

  • Convert every headway to a frequency before comparing options: 3600 divided by the headway is the number that multiplies capacity.
  • Round fleet size up, never down; a fractional vehicle leaves a gap in the headway.
  • When asked about reliability, separate punctuality from regularity and name the measure you mean.
  • Signal capacity answers are built from three numbers — saturation flow, green time and cycle time — so write Q = s × g/c before quoting any of them.
