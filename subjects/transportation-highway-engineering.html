Roads are designed as a hierarchy of routes where geometry, pavement and traffic behaviour must agree with the speed expected. This page links alignment, sight distance, pavement and traffic flow into one design story.

01Road hierarchy and design control

A road network is organised by function:

  • Primary or trunk roads carry long-distance traffic at high speed with limited access.
  • Secondary roads link districts.
  • Distributors spread traffic into neighbourhoods.
  • Local roads give frontage access at low speed.

The key trade-off is between mobility and access. Every driveway or side road joining a fast route adds a conflict point, so access is deliberately restricted on higher classes.

Signs, markings and junctions should tell a driver what class of road they are on, so speed and behaviour match expectations. Mixing functions on one road is where most safety and capacity problems begin.

02Design speed and geometric consistency

Design speed is the speed chosen as the basis of geometric design: it fixes minimum radius, superelevation, sight distance and crest curve length. The road must be credible at that speed, because drivers read the geometry and settle on a speed that feels comfortable.

Geometric consistency matters more than any single element. A long straight followed by a tight bend invites too much speed into the curve, so elements should change gradually.

The cross-section follows the same logic: carriageway width, emergency shoulders, verges for pedestrians, kerbs and the camber or crossfall that drains the surface.

03Stopping and overtaking sight distance

Stopping sight distance (SSD) is the distance a driver needs to stop before an unexpected object at the design speed. It is the reaction distance plus the braking distance:

SSD = 0.278 V t + V² / [254 (f + G)]

with V in km/h, reaction time t in seconds, longitudinal friction f and gradient G as a decimal. Uphill gradients shorten braking and downhill gradients lengthen it, so the check uses the steepest downgrade.

Overtaking sight distance is longer again, because the manoeuvre must see far enough to pass and return safely. Where it cannot be provided, the road should offer safe passing places or be marked no-overtaking.

Stopping sight distance
\[SSD = 0.278Vt + \frac{V^2}{254(f + G)}\]
V in km/h, t in seconds, G positive uphill

04Horizontal alignment and superelevation

Horizontal alignment is a sequence of straights, circular curves and transition curves. For a circular curve of radius R, the superelevation e and the side friction f together balance the centrifugal force:

e + f = V² / (127 R)

with V in km/h and R in metres. Required superelevation therefore rises with the square of speed and falls as the radius increases.

A transition curve eases the change in curvature, lets the superelevation be applied gradually and gives the driver time to steer. Tight curves are also widened slightly, because the rear wheels of a long vehicle track inside the front wheels.

Superelevation balance
\[e + f = \frac{V^2}{127R}\]
e and f are dimensionless ratios, V in km/h, R in metres

05Vertical alignment: gradients and curves

Vertical alignment uses gradients and parabolic curves. Maximum gradients are limited because long upgrades slow heavy vehicles and steep downgrades overload brakes. On a crest, the curve must keep the road surface below the driver's line of sight.

When the sight distance S is shorter than the curve length L,

L = A S² / 647,

where A is the algebraic change in gradient in percent. If the sight distance is longer than the curve, L = 2S − 647/A.

Sag curves are governed by comfort and headlight sight distance, using a criterion such as L = A V² / 395. The ratio K = L/A should exceed the minimum for the design speed.

Crest curve, sight distance shorter than curve
\[L = \frac{A S^2}{647}\]
A is the gradient change in percent, S the sight distance in metres
Sag curve comfort criterion
\[L = \frac{A V^2}{395}\]

06Pavement structure and subgrade

A pavement spreads wheel loads through layers so that the subgrade receives a stress it can carry. A flexible pavement uses a bituminous surface over granular base and sub-base; a rigid pavement uses a concrete slab that carries load in bending and therefore needs joints and load transfer devices.

Subgrade strength is usually characterised by the California Bearing Ratio, the penetration resistance of a compacted sample against a standard crushed-stone curve:

CBR = (P / P_standard) × 100%. CBR, design traffic and drainage together set the layer thicknesses.

Water weakens the subgrade, pumps fines out of granular layers and accelerates rutting, so crossfall and side drains protect the structure.

California Bearing Ratio
\[CBR = \frac{P}{P_{standard}} \times 100\%\]
P is the penetration force, P_standard the standard load at the same penetration

07Design traffic and the fourth-power law

Pavement thickness is set by the traffic it must carry, expressed as equivalent standard axle loads (ESALs). Axles of different weights are converted to the damage of one standard 80 kN axle through the fourth-power law:

RDF = (P / 80)⁴,

so an axle of 160 kN causes sixteen times the damage of an 80 kN axle, and a lightly loaded axle causes almost none.

Design life, traffic growth and the axle-load distribution of the vehicles are combined into a design ESAL count, and the layer thicknesses follow from that count together with the subgrade CBR and drainage.

It is a brutal non-linearity to remember: overloading by 20% roughly doubles the damage, which is why weighbridge enforcement is pavement protection, not bureaucracy.

Relative damage factor
\[\mathrm{RDF} = \left(\frac{P}{80}\right)^4\]
P is the axle load in kN; 80 kN is the standard axle

08Bituminous mixtures and mix design

Bituminous mixtures are graded aggregates bound with bitumen:

  • Dense-graded mixes are impermeable and strong but noisier.
  • Open-graded mixes drain water and cut spray and noise.
  • Stone mastic asphalt uses a coarse aggregate skeleton for rutting resistance.

Marshall mix design selects the optimum binder content by balancing stability against flow, then checking air voids (VTM), voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA) and the fraction of those voids filled with binder:

VTM = (G_mm − G_mb) / G_mm × 100%. Too much binder bleeds; too little leaves the mix dry and permeable.

Warm-mix technology lowers production temperatures, cutting fuel use and emissions.

Air voids in the total mix
\[VTM = \frac{G_{mm} - G_{mb}}{G_{mm}} \times 100\%\]
G_mm theoretical maximum density, G_mb bulk density of the compacted specimen

09The Marshall procedure end to end

The Marshall method is a fixed nine-step sequence worth memorising:

  • Prepare specimens of about 101.5 mm diameter and 63.5 mm height across a range of binder contents — mixing at roughly 155 °C, compacting at 135–140 °C with 50 blows per face at 60 blows per minute.
  • Weigh in air, water and saturated surface-dry to get the bulk density, then compute the air voids and the aggregate void structure:

VMA = VTM + Vb,

where VTM is the air void content and Vb the binder volume percentage, and the binder fill is VFB = (VMA − VTM) / VMA × 100%.

  • After a 60 °C water bath, crush each specimen at 50.8 mm/min: the peak load is the Marshall stability and the deformation at failure is the flow.
  • Plot stability, flow, density, VTM and VMA against binder content, and take the optimum binder content as the average of the contents giving maximum stability, maximum density and the target air voids (about 4%).
  • Finally re-enter the curves and confirm the mix meets the specification; otherwise redesign.
Voids in the mineral aggregate
\[\mathrm{VMA} = \mathrm{VTM} + V_b\]
VTM is air voids and Vb the binder volume, all as percentages of the mix volume
Voids filled with binder
\[\mathrm{VFB} = \frac{\mathrm{VMA} - \mathrm{VTM}}{\mathrm{VMA}} \times 100\%\]
both VMA and VTM as percentages

10Traffic flow, junctions and safety

Traffic flow links flow q, density k and space-mean speed v:

q = k v. As density rises, flow increases until the road reaches capacity; beyond that, jams form and both flow and speed collapse. Level of service grades conditions from free flow to forced flow.

Junctions are the main constraint:

  • An at-grade junction is cheap, but every turning movement is a conflict point.
  • Grade separation removes conflicts at the cost of land and structures.
  • Traffic islands channel vehicles into defined paths and make turning predictable.

Safety is designed in: junction sight lines, forgiving roadside clearances, skid-resistant surfacing and a road safety audit at design stages.

Fundamental traffic flow relation
\[q = k v\]
q vehicles per hour, k density in vehicles per kilometre, v space-mean speed in km/h

Key terms

Design speed
The speed used as the basis for geometric design, fixing radius, superelevation, sight distance and crest length.
Stopping sight distance
The distance a driver must see ahead to stop before a hazard, equal to reaction distance plus braking distance.
Superelevation
Crossfall applied on a horizontal curve so that gravity helps balance centrifugal force.
California Bearing Ratio
The penetration resistance of a compacted soil or granular material compared with a standard crushed-stone curve.
Level of service
A graded description of traffic conditions from free flow to forced flow.
Voids in the mineral aggregate
The void space between aggregate particles in a compacted bituminous mix, available for binder and air.
Marshall stability
The peak load carried by a compacted bituminous specimen crushed radially at 60 °C, used with flow to select the binder content.
Equivalent standard axle load
A measure of design traffic converting axles of different weights to the damage of one 80 kN standard axle through a fourth-power law.
Worked example

Sight distance and crest curve length

A rural road has a design speed of 80 km/h. The driver reaction time is 2.5 s and the longitudinal friction coefficient is 0.35. On the steepest downgrade the gradient is −3%. Calculate the stopping sight distance, then find the crest curve length needed for a gradient change from +3% to −3%.

  1. Reaction distance: 0.278 × 80 × 2.5 = 55.6 m.
  2. Braking distance: V²/[254(f + G)] = 80²/[254 × (0.35 − 0.03)] = 6400/81.3 = 78.7 m.
  3. Stopping sight distance = 55.6 + 78.7 = 134.3 m.
  4. The gradient change is A = 3 − (−3) = 6%.
  5. Assume the curve is longer than the sight distance: L = A S²/647 = 6 × 134.3²/647 = 167 m.
  6. Check the assumption: 134.3 m < 167 m, so the crest curve is indeed longer than the sight distance and the formula was the right one.
  7. Sanity check with K = L/A = 167/6 = 27.8 m per percent, close to the usual minimum of about 30 m per percent for this speed, so round the curve up.
AnswerProvide a crest curve of about 170 m; the stopping sight distance is 134 m and governs the crest length.

Exam method

  • Sketch the sight line on the vertical curve, state whether S is longer or shorter than L, then choose the matching formula.
  • Keep units tidy: V in km/h and R in metres in the superelevation equation, with e and f as plain ratios.
  • For mix design, report VMA, VTM and voids filled with binder together; one number alone does not describe a mix.
  • When comparing junction types, count conflict points rather than listing advantages without a criterion.
  • In a Marshall question, plot all five curves — stability, flow, density, VTM, VMA — and read the optimum as the average of three binder contents before checking the specification.
